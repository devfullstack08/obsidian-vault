## Five-minute revision

**Origin and extent:** Indian–Eurasian continental collision; geologically young and still active. Main arc: roughly **2,400 km**, from the **Indus gorge to the Dihang gorge**. Western bend: **Nanga Parbat**; eastern bend: **Namcha Barwa**.

**South → north:** **Shiwalik → Himachal → Himadri**. Shiwalik = Outer; Himachal = Lesser; Himadri = Greater. Typical textbook heights: Shiwalik **900–1,100 m**; Lesser ranges **3,700–4,500 m**; Greater Himalaya average about **6,000 m**. These are broad descriptions, not the height of every place.

**West → east, traditional regions:** Punjab **Indus–Satluj**; Kumaon **Satluj–Kali**; Nepal **Kali–Tista**; Assam **Tista–Dihang**. These names do not match present state boundaries.

**Landforms:** Dun = valley between Lesser Himalaya and Shiwalik. Karewa = Kashmir deposits associated with a former lake environment. Bhabar = coarse deposits and stream infiltration. Terai = wetter belt south of Bhabar, where water re-emerges.

**Peaks:** Everest **8,848.86 m**, world highest; K2 **8,611 m**, Karakoram; Kangchenjunga **8,586 m**, Sikkim–Nepal border; Nanda Devi **7,816 m**, entirely within India. “Highest in India” needs the territorial scope explained in the main chapter.

**Passes:** Zoji La = Kashmir–Dras/Ladakh; Rohtang = Kullu–Lahaul; Kunzum = Lahaul–Spiti; Shipki La = Himachal–Tibet; Mana/Niti/Lipulekh = Uttarakhand; Nathu/Jelep = Sikkim; Sela/Bum La = Arunachal Pradesh.

**Ice and water:** Siachen = Karakoram; Gangotri = Uttarakhand; Bara Shigri = Himachal Pradesh; Zemu = Sikkim. **Chandra + Bhaga = Chenab at Tandi. Bhagirathi + Alaknanda = Ganga at Devprayag.** Tsangpo → Siang/Dihang → Brahmaputra.

**Climate and ecology:** uplift of moist air helps produce rain; northern interiors can lie in a rain shadow. Western disturbances bring winter precipitation. Vegetation changes with altitude and moisture; **snowline ≠ tree line**.

**Eastern hills:** Patkai, Naga, Manipur and Mizo/Lushai = Purvanchal. Garo, Khasi and Jaintia = Meghalaya Plateau.

**Hazards:** earthquakes, landslides, avalanches, flash floods and GLOFs. A GLOF releases water from a glacial lake; every mountain flood is not a GLOF. **Perennial rivers flow through the year; their flow is not constant.**

## Map practice

Use the relief map in [[Himalayas]] and a physical atlas. Locate these groups, then try recalling their positions on a blank outline:

1. **Ends of the arc:** Nanga Parbat and Namcha Barwa; Indus and Dihang gorges.
2. **Mountain belts:** Shiwalik, Lesser Himalaya and Greater Himalaya; adjacent Karakoram, Ladakh and Zanskar ranges.
3. **West-to-east regional boundaries:** Indus, Satluj, Kali, Tista and Dihang.
4. **Peaks:** K2 in Karakoram; Nanga Parbat, Nanda Devi, Everest, Kangchenjunga and Namcha Barwa along the high Himalayan belt.
5. **Passes:** Zoji La, Rohtang, Shipki La, Lipulekh, Nathu La and Sela.
6. **Water:** Siachen, Gangotri, Zemu, Devprayag and the three major river systems.

## Two mains practice answers

These are answer frameworks, not official model answers. Expand them in your own words within the stated limit.

### 1. Explain how the Himalayas influence India's climate and drainage. (150 words)

**Opening:** identify the Himalayas as a high, active mountain barrier formed by continental collision.

**Develop four connections:**

- **Moist air rises:** southern slopes receive orographic precipitation; sheltered northern interiors can be dry.
- **Air movement changes:** the barrier influences monsoon circulation and limits direct penetration of cold continental air. It is one part of the wider climate system.
- **Winter weather matters:** western disturbances supply snow to the western Himalaya and rain to nearby plains.
- **Water is stored and released:** snow, glaciers, rainfall and groundwater feed the Indus, Ganga and Brahmaputra systems, with seasonal variation. Rivers also carry sediment to the plains.

**Close:** connect mountain ecosystem protection with downstream water security. Add a small south–north diagram showing moist air rising and a sheltered northern side.

### 2. Why is the Himalayan region vulnerable to disasters? Suggest a balanced development approach. (250 words)

**Opening:** vulnerability results from active geology, steep terrain, weather extremes and the exposure of people and infrastructure.

**Explain the causes:** active faults generate earthquakes; steep slopes and loose material favour landslides; intense rain can trigger flash floods; snow and ice can produce avalanches; unstable glacial lakes may cause GLOFs. A single event can trigger further hazards downstream.

**Connect development choices:** poorly drained road cuts, building on unstable slopes or flood paths, and loss of protective vegetation can increase risk. Climate change affects rainfall, snow, ice and lake conditions, with local differences.

**Give practical measures:** map hazards before choosing sites; enforce earthquake-resistant design; manage road drainage and slopes; monitor weather, lakes and unstable terrain; provide warning and evacuation systems; study the combined effects of projects across a river basin; involve local communities and protect livelihoods.

**Close:** development should improve access and livelihoods while respecting the limits of mountain terrain. Avoid treating every flood as a GLOF or every project as automatically unsafe.
