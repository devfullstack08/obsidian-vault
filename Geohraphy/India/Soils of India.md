**India has different soils because its rocks, rainfall, temperature, vegetation and landforms vary.** Learn each soil through four questions: **How does it form? Where is it found? What is it like? What can grow in it?**

School geography commonly teaches **six major groups**: alluvial, black, red and yellow, laterite, arid, and forest/mountain soils. **Saline/alkaline** and **peaty/marshy** soils are also included here for broader coverage. These are traditional geographical groups; scientific soil surveys use more detailed classifications.

## First understand soil

**Soil is a mixture of mineral particles, organic matter, water, air and living organisms.** It supports plants and stores and filters water.

Rock and sediment gradually break down through **weathering**. Organisms add and transform organic material. Over time, these processes form soil. Formation depends on **parent material, climate, organisms, relief and time**. Parent material means the rock or sediment from which soil develops; relief means the shape and slope of the land. 

Useful words:

- **Humus:** relatively well-decomposed organic matter in soil.
- **Texture:** the proportions of sand, silt and clay. Sand has larger particles; clay has much finer particles. **Loam** contains a useful mixture of these sizes, not necessarily equal amounts.
- **Fertility:** the soil's ability to supply nutrients needed by plants.
- **Leaching:** water moving through soil carries dissolved substances downward, sometimes beyond plant roots.
- **Drainage:** how readily excess water leaves the soil.

**Dark colour does not automatically mean high fertility.** Water, drainage, nutrients, soil depth and climate all affect plant growth.

## 1. Alluvial soil — rivers build the plains

**Formation:** rivers transport and deposit sand, silt and clay. Soils develop in these deposits.

**Where:** the **Indus–Ganga–Brahmaputra plains**, many river valleys and coastal plains, including eastern deltas such as those of the Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna and Kaveri.

**Features:** texture and fertility vary, but many alluvial areas are highly productive. Nitrogen and organic matter can be low; repeated cropping still requires nutrient management.

**Crops:** rice, wheat, sugarcane, pulses and oilseeds; jute in suitable warm, humid eastern areas. These crops need suitable climate and water as well as soil.

### Khadar and Bhangar

- **Khadar:** newer alluvium in active floodplains, often renewed by floods.
- **Bhangar:** older alluvium, usually on higher terraces; it may contain **kankar**, calcium-carbonate nodules.

Khadar is commonly described as more fertile because of fresh deposits, but older alluvium is not automatically infertile.

**Memory: River deposits → alluvial; newer → Khadar; older → Bhangar.**

## 2. Black soil — clay that holds moisture

Also called **regur** or **black cotton soil**.

**Formation:** many Indian black soils developed from weathered **basalt**, the volcanic rock of the Deccan region. Their properties reflect both parent material and climate.

**Where:** large areas of **Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh and Gujarat**, with parts of Telangana, Karnataka and adjoining plateau regions.

**Features:** clay-rich, often deep, and able to store substantial moisture. The clay **swells when wet and shrinks when dry**, producing wide cracks. Wet soil can be sticky and difficult to work; poor drainage may cause waterlogging.

**Nutrients:** commonly relatively rich in lime and certain mineral nutrients, but often low in nitrogen, available phosphorus and organic matter. Black colour does not mean abundant humus.

**Crops:** cotton is the classic association; soybean, sorghum, pulses and other crops are also grown where conditions permit.

**Memory: Black → clay → moisture storage → dry-season cracks → cotton.**

## 3. Red and yellow soils — iron gives the colour

**Formation:** weathering of old crystalline rocks, such as granite and gneiss, across much of peninsular India.

**Where:** parts of **Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Odisha, Chhattisgarh and Jharkhand**, with patches elsewhere.

**Colour:** iron compounds give a reddish colour; hydrated iron compounds can produce yellow shades. **Yellow does not simply mean less iron.**

**Features:** often low in organic matter and nitrogen; depth, texture and water-holding capacity vary. Thin upland soils are generally less productive than deeper soils in lower areas.

**Crops:** millets, pulses and groundnut are common associations. Rice and other crops can grow with suitable water, soil depth and nutrient management.

**Memory: Red/yellow → weathered rocks + iron compounds.**

## 4. Laterite soil — heavy rain removes soluble materials

**Formation:** prolonged weathering in warm, wet conditions, often with seasonal wet and dry periods. Heavy rain causes strong **leaching**. Iron and aluminium compounds become relatively concentrated as other materials are removed.

**Where:** patches in the **Western and Eastern Ghats**, Karnataka, Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Maharashtra, Odisha and northeastern hill regions.

**Features:** often acidic and low in available nutrients. Some lateritic material hardens strongly on exposure. **Heavy rainfall does not always create fertile soil:** it can wash nutrients out.

**Crops:** tea, coffee and cashew are common textbook associations in suitable locations, with appropriate soil and nutrient management. A lateritic site is not automatically suitable for all three.

**Memory: Warm + wet → leaching → iron/aluminium-rich residue.**

The geographical descriptions above follow the broad school-level framework; actual soil boundaries cross state borders and contain many local variations.

## 5. Arid soil — little rain, little organic matter

**Arid means dry.** Low rainfall and sparse vegetation limit organic-matter accumulation; strong evaporation can encourage salt accumulation.

**Where:** especially **western Rajasthan** and adjoining dry areas of northwestern India.

**Features:** commonly sandy, low in humus and moisture, and vulnerable to wind erosion. Calcium-carbonate layers may occur below the surface. Not every desert soil is pure loose sand.

**Crops:** drought-tolerant crops such as **bajra** can grow where seasonal rainfall permits. Irrigation makes other crops possible, but unsuitable irrigation and poor drainage can worsen salinity.

**Memory: Dry climate → low humus + water shortage.**

## 6. Forest and mountain soils — altitude and slope matter

**Where:** Himalayan and other forested hill regions.

**Formation and features:** vegetation, rainfall, temperature and slope create varied soils. Steep slopes often have thin, coarse, easily eroded soils. Valleys can hold deeper, finer deposits. Organic matter and acidity vary; all mountain soils are not equally fertile.

**Crops:** suitable slopes and valleys support fruits, maize, potatoes and other crops. Tea grows in some humid hill regions. **Apples need suitable cool conditions**, not merely soil labelled “mountain soil”.

**Memory: Mountains → changing altitude and slope → changing soils.**

## 7. Saline and alkaline soils — salt and sodium problems

These are often grouped together in school notes, but they are **not identical**.

- **Saline soil:** excess soluble salts make it harder for plants to take up water.
- **Sodic soil:** excess sodium attached to soil particles can damage structure and reduce water entry; these soils are commonly alkaline.
- **Alkaline** describes a high pH. Not every alkaline soil has the same sodium problem.

**Where:** parts of Gujarat, Rajasthan, Punjab, Haryana and Uttar Pradesh, coastal areas and poorly drained irrigated land. Sea-water influence, evaporation and irrigation practices can contribute.

**Improvement:** saline soils generally require suitable water and effective drainage to remove salts. **Gypsum may help sodic soils**, followed by appropriate leaching and drainage. It is not a universal treatment for every salty soil; diagnosis comes first. 

**Memory: Saline = excess salts; sodic = harmful sodium dominance.**

## 8. Peaty and marshy soils — organic matter in wet ground

**Formation:** prolonged waterlogging limits oxygen, slowing decomposition. Plant remains accumulate; peaty soils contain substantial partly decomposed organic material.

**Where:** wetland pockets in **Kerala, coastal Odisha and West Bengal**, and some northern lowlands.

**Features:** often dark and organic-rich, but waterlogging, acidity or salinity may restrict cultivation. **Not every marsh has peat**, and dark organic soil is not automatically easy to farm.

Some managed wetland areas support rice. Wetlands also store carbon and support wildlife; draining them can damage these functions.

**Memory: Waterlogging → slow decomposition → organic accumulation.**

## Soil erosion and conservation

**Weathering forms soil material; erosion removes and transports it.** Fertile topsoil can be lost much faster than it forms.

- **Sheet erosion:** runoff removes a thin layer over a wide area.
- **Rill and gully erosion:** flowing water cuts channels; larger channels become gullies. The **Chambal ravines** are a familiar example.
- **Wind erosion:** wind removes exposed particles, especially in dry areas.

Protection methods include **plant cover and mulching**, contour cultivation across slopes, terraces on suitable hillsides, shelterbelts against wind, controlled grazing, and careful irrigation and drainage. Measures must suit the local slope, soil and rainfall.

## One-minute revision

- **Alluvial:** rivers; northern plains and deltas; **Khadar/Bhangar**.
- **Black:** clay-rich; Deccan region; **moisture retention and cracks**.
- **Red/yellow:** weathered crystalline rocks; **iron colours**.
- **Laterite:** warm, wet conditions; **strong leaching**.
- **Arid:** dry northwest; **little humus and moisture**.
- **Forest/mountain:** hills; **varies with altitude and slope**.
- **Saline/alkaline:** **salt or sodium problems**.
- **Peaty/marshy:** waterlogged areas; **organic accumulation**.

**Three exam traps:** black soil is not necessarily humus-rich; laterite is not naturally fertile just because rainfall is heavy; and no crop belongs exclusively to one soil type.
