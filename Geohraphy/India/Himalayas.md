**The Himalayas are a young, active mountain system created by the collision of the Indian and Eurasian plates.** They shape India's climate, feed major rivers, support diverse ecosystems and influence settlement, transport and security.

## 1. Locate the Himalayas

The main Himalayan arc runs for roughly **2,400 km**, between the **Indus gorge in the west** and the **Dihang/Brahmaputra gorge in the east**. Its width varies: the familiar textbook figures are about **400 km in Kashmir** and **150 km in Arunachal Pradesh**. These are broad regional measurements, not a uniform width.

The **Northern Plains lie to the south**, and the **Tibetan Plateau lies to the north**. The mountain system extends beyond India through neighbouring countries; it is not entirely within Indian territory.

Two great bends help anchor the map:

- **Nanga Parbat:** the western bend, associated with the Indus gorge; in Pakistan-administered Gilgit-Baltistan.
- **Namcha Barwa:** the eastern bend, associated with the Yarlung Tsangpo/Brahmaputra gorge; in Tibet, China, near the eastern end of the Himalayan arc.

These are **syntaxial bends**: places where the mountain belt turns sharply. They are not the highest peaks of the range.

**Map anchors: Indus — Nanga Parbat — Himalayan arc — Namcha Barwa — Dihang.**

## 2. How the Himalayas formed

1. The Indian landmass moved north as part of the **Indian Plate**. An ocean, commonly called the **Tethys/Neo-Tethys** in this context, separated it from Eurasia.
2. Oceanic crust was consumed as the gap narrowed.
3. Continental collision began roughly **50 million years ago**, with details of its timing and stages still studied.
4. Compression shortened, folded and stacked rocks, thickening the crust and raising the mountains and adjacent plateau.
5. Convergence continues. Uplift, weathering, river erosion and glacial erosion continue to reshape the landscape.

**Young fold mountains** means geologically young compared with ancient ranges such as the Aravallis. It does not mean the mountains formed recently in human history. Their rocks include **sedimentary** rocks built from deposits, **metamorphic** rocks changed by heat and pressure, and **igneous** rocks formed from cooled molten material.

A **thrust fault** is a fracture along which compression pushes one rock mass over another. Movement on faults explains much Himalayan earthquake activity. Plate motion and vertical mountain uplift are different measurements; there is no single uplift rate for the entire range.

## 3. The three parallel ranges

A common mistake is to mix **south–north ranges** with **west–east regions**. First imagine travelling from the plains towards Tibet.

![[himalayan-ranges-south-to-north.png|850]]

**South → north: Shiwalik → Himachal → Himadri.** These belts follow the Himalayan arc; they are not three perfectly straight, uninterrupted walls.

### Shiwalik — the Outer Himalaya

- **Position:** the southernmost of the three main belts, next to the plains.
- **Typical textbook elevation:** roughly **900–1,100 m**; local heights vary.
- **Material:** relatively young river deposits carried from the mountains farther north, including sand, gravel and boulders, subsequently uplifted.
- **Character:** low, erosion-prone hills with many weakly consolidated sediments.
- **Key feature:** **Duns** occur between the Shiwalik and Lesser Himalaya.

**Remember: Shiwalik = outer, low and young foothills.**

### Himachal — the Lesser Himalaya

- **Position:** between Shiwalik and Himadri.
- **Typical textbook elevation:** about **3,700–4,500 m** for the ranges; this is not the elevation of every town or valley.
- **Ranges by region:**
  - **Jammu and Kashmir and Himachal Pradesh:** Pir Panjal.
  - **Himachal Pradesh:** Dhauladhar.
  - **Uttarakhand:** Nag Tibba and Mussoorie ranges.
  - **Nepal:** Mahabharat Range.
- **Landscape:** rugged ridges, forested slopes and valleys; many well-known hill stations occur in this broader belt.
- **Pir Panjal** is the major Lesser Himalayan range to remember in the northwest.

**Remember: Himachal = Lesser Himalaya.** Here “Himachal” names a mountain belt; it does not mean only the state of Himachal Pradesh.

### Himadri — the Greater Himalaya

- **Position:** the northernmost of these three main parallel belts.
- **Typical average elevation:** around **6,000 m**.
- **Character:** the most continuous high range, with many very high peaks, extensive snowfields and glaciers.
- **Rocks:** includes major crystalline rocks, such as granite and metamorphic rocks.

**Remember: Himadri = Greater = highest main belt.**

#### Peaks of the Greater Himalaya

A **range** is a long belt of mountains; a **peak** is an individual summit within it. The Greater Himalaya contains many of the world's highest peaks. Following its high mountain belt roughly **from west to east**:

- **Nanga Parbat(killer) — 8,126 m:** near the Indus gorge at the western bend; in Pakistan-administered Gilgit-Baltistan.
- **Nanda Devi — 7,816 m:** in Garhwal, Uttarakhand; the **highest peak entirely within India under its administration**.
- **Dhaulagiri I — 8,167 m** and **Annapurna I — 8,091 m:** in Nepal, west of Everest.
- **Mount Everest — 8,848.86 m:** on the Nepal–China border; the **world's highest summit above sea level**. It is called **Sagarmatha** in Nepal and **Chomolungma** in Tibetan usage.
- **Lhotse — 8,516 m:** close to Everest, on the Nepal–China border.
- **Makalu — 8,485 m:** east of Everest, on the Nepal–China border.
- **Kangchenjunga — 8,586 m:** on the **Sikkim–Nepal border**; the world's third-highest peak and the highest on India's administered territory or its international boundary. Unlike Nanda Devi, it is shared with Nepal. Its name is also written Kanchenjunga or Khangchendzonga.
- **Namcha Barwa — 7,782 m:** near the great Tsangpo gorge at the eastern bend, in Tibet, China.

### Major thrust faults

From south to north, the broad sequence is **Main Frontal Thrust → Main Boundary Thrust → Main Central Thrust**. They are associated respectively with the Himalayan front, the Shiwalik–Lesser Himalayan boundary, and the Lesser–Higher Himalayan boundary. Actual geology is more complicated than three neat lines.

## 4. Northern ranges and eastern hills

### Trans-Himalayan region

Study the **Karakoram, Ladakh and Zanskar ranges** alongside the main Himalayan belts. School guides often group them broadly under the Trans-Himalayan or northern mountain region, although their geological units are not identical.

- **Karakoram:** northwest of the main Himalayan arc. Its highest peak is **K2 — 8,611 m**, the world's second-highest mountain, on the Pakistan-administered Gilgit-Baltistan–China frontier. **Siachen Glacier** also lies in this range and drains towards the Nubra–Shyok–Indus system. K2 appears in Indian lists based on India's territorial claim, but is not under Indian administration.
- **Ladakh Range:** generally north of the Indus valley in Ladakh.
- **Zanskar Range:** generally south of the Indus valley in this region, towards the Greater Himalaya.
- **Kailash Range:** an important range in Tibet, beyond the main Himalayan belt.

**Do not place K2 or Siachen in the Himadri simply because both are very high.** “Himalayan region” is often used more broadly than “Himalaya proper”.

### Purvanchal / Purvachal

Beyond the Dihang gorge, the eastern mountain system bends south. The school-level **Purvanchal** grouping includes the **Patkai, Naga, Manipur and Mizo/Lushai hills**. These hills generally have more north–south alignment than the central Himalayan arc.

**Garo, Khasi and Jaintia hills belong to the Meghalaya Plateau**, an extension of the peninsular plateau system. They are not the standard Purvanchal hill list. Their high rainfall does not make them part of the main Himalayan ranges.

## 5. West-to-east regional divisions

| Himalayan region | Between rivers  |
| ---------------- | --------------- |
| Punjab Himalayas | Indus – Satluj  |
| Kumaon Himalayas | Satluj – Kali   |
| Nepal Himalayas  | Kali – Teesta   |
| Assam Himalayas  | Teesta – Dihang |

The traditional four-part scheme uses **rivers as boundaries**:

1. **Punjab Himalaya: Indus → Satluj.** Includes areas also described regionally as the Kashmir and Himachal Himalayas. “Punjab” here does not mean the present Punjab state alone.
2. **Kumaon Himalaya: Satluj → Kali.** A broad textbook region, wider than today's Kumaon administrative division.
3. **Nepal Himalaya: Kali → Tista.** Includes the central high Himalayan belt.
4. **Assam Himalaya: Tista → Dihang.** “Assam” is the traditional regional label, not the boundary of the modern state.

**Kumaon is a regional name, not a separate Lesser Himalayan range.** The broad Satluj–Kali region includes several mountain belts. This is why **Nanda Devi belongs to the Greater Himalaya**, lies locally in **Garhwal**, and still falls within the traditional **Kumaon Himalaya** region.

**Boundary sequence: Indus → Satluj → Kali → Tista → Dihang.**

**Kali** is also called **Sharda/Mahakali**; **Tista** is also spelled **Teesta**. Other books use five regional divisions, such as Kashmir/Northwestern, Himachal–Uttarakhand, Darjeeling–Sikkim, Arunachal and eastern hills. Use the classification asked in the question; do not combine the labels into one inconsistent list.

### Western and eastern contrasts

The western Himalaya receives important winter snowfall from **western disturbances**; the eastern Himalaya is generally more strongly influenced by summer monsoon moisture. Humid eastern slopes support rich biodiversity, while sheltered northern interiors can be cold and dry. Altitude, slope direction and exposure create exceptions within both regions.

## 6. Valleys, foothills and deposits

### Duns

A **Dun is a long valley between the Lesser Himalaya and Shiwalik**, running broadly along the mountain belt. Examples include **Dehra Dun, Kotli Dun and Patli Dun**.

### Kashmir Valley and Karewas

The **Kashmir Valley lies between the Greater Himalaya and Pir Panjal**. The **Jhelum** drains the valley.

Parts of this region were once occupied by a lake. Sediments accumulated there and were later exposed as the landscape changed. These thick deposits, with later material also added, form terrace-like features called **Karewas**. They are well known for **saffron cultivation**, especially around Pampore.

### Bhabar and Terai

Rivers carry stones, gravel and finer soil out of the mountains. As they reach gentler ground, they deposit this material, helping form two different foothill belts.

![[himalayas-bhabar-terai.png|850]]

- **Bhabar:** the belt closest to the mountain foot, commonly about **8–16 km wide**. Its coarse pebbles and boulders leave spaces through which water can seep underground. Many smaller streams therefore disappear from the surface.
- **Terai:** the lower belt **south of Bhabar**. Groundwater comes close to the surface and streams re-emerge. Finer deposits and a high water table produce a wetter, often marshy landscape.

**From hills towards plains: Bhabar → Terai.** Major rivers do not all disappear underground in Bhabar. Farther into the plains, **Bhangar and Khadar** refer to older and newer river deposits.

## 7. Mountain passes

A **pass is a relatively low crossing through a mountain ridge**. Passes connect valleys and have long served as routes for travel, trade and movement across the mountains.

### Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh

- **Zoji La:** connects the Kashmir Valley with **Dras and Ladakh**, on the Srinagar–Leh route.
- **Banihal:** crosses **Pir Panjal**, connecting the Jammu side with the Kashmir Valley. A pass is the natural crossing; a tunnel is a route built through the mountain.
- **Khardung La:** crosses the **Ladakh Range**, connecting Leh with the Nubra side.
- **Karakoram Pass:** lies on the historic route between Ladakh and Xinjiang.

### Himachal Pradesh

- **Rohtang:** crosses **Pir Panjal** and connects **Kullu with Lahaul**.
- **Bara-lacha La:** lies on the route from Lahaul towards Ladakh, near the headwater region of the Chandra and Bhaga rivers.
- **Kunzum:** connects **Lahaul and Spiti**.
- **Shipki La:** lies in the **Kinnaur–Tibet** border region. The Sutlej enters India near this sector, through its river valley.

### Uttarakhand

- **Mana and Niti:** lie in the **Chamoli** border region, towards Tibet.
- **Lipulekh:** lies on the route from the **Pithoragarh** region towards Tibet and Kailash–Mansarovar. India administers it as part of Uttarakhand; Nepal disputes the surrounding Kalapani–Lipulekh area.

### Sikkim and Arunachal Pradesh

- **Nathu La and Jelep La:** connect **Sikkim with Tibet**, towards the Chumbi Valley.
- **Sela:** lies on the route between the West Kameng side and **Tawang**, Arunachal Pradesh.
- **Bum La:** is a border pass near **Tawang**, on the India–China frontier.

## 8. Glaciers and rivers

High in the mountains, snow can accumulate over many years and become compacted into ice. When this ice slowly moves under its own weight, it forms a **glacier**. Its lower end is the **snout**; the rock debris it carries and deposits is called **moraine**.

Melting snow and glacier ice supply water to streams. Rainfall and groundwater also feed Himalayan rivers, so their water does not come from glaciers alone.

### Glaciers in the high Himalayan region

- **Gangotri — Uttarakhand:** its snout, **Gaumukh**, is associated with the **Bhagirathi** headstream.
- **Pindari and Milam — Uttarakhand**.
- **Bara Shigri — Himachal Pradesh:** in the Chandra valley region.
- **Zemu — Sikkim:** near Kangchenjunga.

### Indus river system

The **Indus rises in Tibet**, flows through Ladakh and continues towards Pakistan. Its major Punjab tributaries lie in this **west-to-east order: Jhelum → Chenab → Ravi → Beas → Sutlej**.

The **Chandra and Bhaga meet at Tandi** to form the **Chenab**, also called Chandrabhaga in its upper course.

### Ganga river system

The **Bhagirathi and Alaknanda meet at Devprayag**. From this confluence, or meeting of rivers, the combined river is called the **Ganga**.

The **Yamuna** originates in the Yamunotri region. Other major Himalayan tributaries of the Ganga include the **Ghaghara, Gandak and Kosi**.

#### The five Prayags on the Alaknanda

Following the Alaknanda downstream, five well-known confluences occur in this order:

1. **Vishnuprayag:** Alaknanda + **Dhauliganga**.
2. **Nandprayag:** Alaknanda + **Nandakini**.
3. **Karnaprayag:** Alaknanda + **Pindar**.
4. **Rudraprayag:** Alaknanda + **Mandakini**.
5. **Devprayag:** Alaknanda + **Bhagirathi** → **Ganga**.

### Brahmaputra river system

The river is called **Yarlung Tsangpo in Tibet**, **Siang/Dihang in Arunachal Pradesh**, and **Brahmaputra in Assam**.

It turns around the **Namcha Barwa** region and cuts through a deep gorge. The **Dibang and Lohit** join the Siang/Dihang before the combined river flows across Assam.

### Why rivers can cross mountain ranges

Some rivers maintained their courses while the surrounding land was rising. They cut down into the rock as uplift continued, producing deep gorges. This is called **antecedent drainage**; the **Indus, Sutlej and Brahmaputra** are standard examples.

The major Himalayan rivers are **perennial**, meaning they normally flow throughout the year. Their flow still changes with rainfall, melting snow and ice, and groundwater supply.

## 9. Climate, vegetation and wildlife

### Rainfall and the rain shadow

When moist air meets a mountain barrier, it rises, expands and cools. Water vapour can then condense and fall as rain or snow. This is **orographic precipitation**.

Exposed southern slopes receive substantial moisture, while sheltered areas beyond the high ranges can be much drier. **Ladakh lies in a rain shadow**, helping explain its cold-desert conditions. It is cold because of its high elevation and dry because it receives little precipitation.

The Himalayas also limit the direct southward movement of cold continental air and influence monsoon circulation. The wider climate system still allows cold waves and regional differences in rainfall.

### Winter snowfall

**Western disturbances** are weather systems that form outside the tropics and travel east towards India, generally from the Mediterranean and West Asian region. They bring **winter snow to the western Himalaya** and rain to adjoining plains. Himalayan precipitation therefore comes from both monsoon and winter weather systems.

### Vegetation changes as we climb

Temperature generally falls with height, so the plants growing on a lower slope differ from those near the snowy peaks.

- **Warmer lower slopes:** tropical and subtropical vegetation, depending on local rainfall.
- **Cooler middle slopes:** temperate forests, including broadleaf trees such as **oak and chestnut**, and conifers such as **deodar, pine, fir and spruce**.
- **Near the upper forest limit:** scattered trees and shrubs, including **birch and rhododendron** in suitable areas.
- **Above the tree line:** alpine grasses and meadows, followed at greater heights by sparse vegetation, bare rock and snow or ice.

These belts overlap and occur at different heights depending on the region, moisture and slope direction. The **tree line** marks the upper limit of tree growth; the **snowline** is the approximate lower limit of snow that persists through the year.

**Bugyals** are high mountain meadows in Uttarakhand. Herders may move livestock to higher pastures in summer and return to lower areas in winter. This seasonal movement is called **transhumance**.

### Wildlife and protected areas

The **snow leopard** lives in high, cold mountain landscapes, often with open, rocky terrain. The **red panda** lives in temperate forests of the eastern Himalayan region. Their different habitats show how much the environment changes across the mountains.

Protected areas conserve these varied landscapes:

- **Great Himalayan National Park — Himachal Pradesh**.
- **Nanda Devi and Valley of Flowers National Parks — Uttarakhand**.
- **Khangchendzonga National Park — Sikkim**, recognised for both natural and cultural values.
- **Hemis National Park — Ladakh**, in the Trans-Himalayan region.

## 10. How the Himalayas support life

**Water:** snow, glaciers, rainfall and springs feed rivers used by people in the mountains and downstream plains. A river's **catchment** is the land area that drains into it.

**Agriculture:** rivers carry sediment that helps build the fertile alluvial plains. Mountain valleys support farming and fruit cultivation, while meadows provide grazing land.

**Climate and habitats:** the mountain barrier changes air movement and rainfall. Differences in altitude and moisture create many habitats within short distances.

**Livelihoods and culture:** tourism, pilgrimage, hydropower, forest products and traditional trade support local communities.

**Connectivity and security:** valleys and passes provide routes through difficult terrain and influence access to border regions.

## 11. Natural hazards and safer development

The Himalayas are still being reshaped by plate movement, rivers, ice and weather. Their steep slopes and active faults make several hazards important:

- **Earthquake:** stress builds up in the crust and is released when rocks move along a fault.
- **Landslide:** rock, debris or soil moves down a slope, sometimes triggered by heavy rain, an earthquake or slope cutting.
- **Avalanche:** snow and ice move rapidly downhill.
- **Flash flood:** river flow rises very quickly after intense rainfall or a sudden release of stored water.
- **Glacial lake outburst flood, or GLOF:** water escapes suddenly from a glacial lake, for example when its natural barrier fails or an avalanche displaces the lake water.

One event can trigger another: **a landslide blocks a river → water collects behind it → the temporary barrier breaks → a flood travels downstream**. A flood is called a GLOF specifically when the released water comes from a glacial lake.

Road cuts without proper drainage, construction on unstable slopes and buildings in flood paths can increase damage. Climate change also affects snow, ice, rainfall and glacial lakes, with different effects in different locations.

**Safer development** means choosing suitable construction sites, maintaining drainage, protecting slopes and using earthquake-resistant buildings. Weather and lake monitoring, early warnings, evacuation routes and local knowledge help communities prepare. Large projects also need assessment of their combined effects across the river basin.
