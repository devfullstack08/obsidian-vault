Rainfall is commonly divided into **three types according to what makes moist air rise**:

1. **Convectional:** heating makes air rise.
2. **Orographic / relief:** a mountain forces air to rise.
3. **Cyclonic:** air rises in a low-pressure weather system; fronts can also force it upward.

## First understand how rain forms

**Moist air rises → expands and cools → water vapour condenses → cloud droplets or ice crystals grow → precipitation falls.**

- **Moist air** contains water vapour, an invisible gas.
- As air rises, the surrounding pressure decreases. The air expands and cools.
- When it cools enough, water vapour condenses onto tiny particles, forming cloud droplets. Ice crystals can also form in cold clouds.
- These droplets or crystals grow large enough to fall. If they reach the ground as liquid drops, we call it **rain**.

**Cloud formation does not always lead to rain.** Cloud particles must grow sufficiently, and falling drops can evaporate before reaching the ground.

## 1. Convectional rainfall — heating lifts the air

**Convection** means movement caused by warmer, less dense air rising.

### How it happens

1. The Sun heats the ground.
2. The ground warms the moist air above it.
3. The warmer air becomes less dense than its surroundings and rises.
4. As it rises, it expands and cools. Clouds form if there is enough moisture.
5. With strong upward movement, tall clouds develop and produce showers or thunderstorms.

### What the rain is usually like

- **Heavy but relatively short showers**, often over a small area.
- Common in the **afternoon or evening** after daytime heating, although it can occur at other times.
- Thunder and lightning may occur. Strong convection can produce **cumulonimbus**, or thunderstorm clouds.

### Examples

- Frequent afternoon showers in hot, humid equatorial regions, such as the **Amazon Basin**.
- Many **pre-monsoon thunderstorms in India** involve convection. Winds and larger weather systems can also help trigger them.

**Imagine:** On a hot, humid afternoon, clouds grow taller and a sudden shower begins. Heating has helped lift the air.

**Remember: Hot ground → rising air → shower.**

## 2. Orographic rainfall — mountains lift the air

**Orographic** means related to mountains or the shape of the land. This is also called **relief rainfall**.

### How it happens

1. Moist winds approach a mountain or hill range.
2. The land forces the air upward.
3. Rising air expands and cools, allowing clouds and rain to form.
4. After crossing the range, air often descends, compresses and warms.
5. Its relative humidity falls, making clouds and rain less likely on that side.

### Two important sides

- **Windward side:** faces the approaching wind; usually receives more rain.
- **Leeward side:** lies away from the approaching wind; usually receives less rain.
- **Rain shadow:** a relatively dry area on the leeward side.

**A rain shadow is not completely rainless.** Other winds or weather systems can still bring rain there.

### Indian example: Western Ghats

During the **southwest monsoon**, moist winds from the Arabian Sea rise over the Western Ghats.

**Western slopes: wetter → air crosses the mountains → parts of the interior Deccan: drier.**

This helps explain why **Mumbai receives much more rain than Pune**, which lies east of the Ghats in their rain shadow.

Moist air rising over the **Khasi Hills** also helps produce exceptionally heavy rain around **Mawsynram and Cherrapunji**.

**Remember: Mountain barrier → forced rise → wetter windward side.**

## 3. Cyclonic rainfall — a weather system lifts the air

A **low-pressure system** is a region where atmospheric pressure is lower than in the surrounding area. Air tends to move towards it and can rise, cool and form rain clouds.

### A. Frontal rainfall

A **front** is a boundary between air masses with different temperatures and other properties.

When warm and cold air meet, the **warmer, less dense air rises over the colder, denser air**. The rising air cools, forming clouds and precipitation.

- **Warm front:** warm air gradually rises over retreating cold air. It often brings widespread, relatively steady precipitation.
- **Cold front:** advancing cold air lifts warm air more sharply. It can bring heavy showers or thunderstorms, especially when the warm air is moist and unstable.

Frontal rainfall is common in **temperate or mid-latitude cyclones**.

### B. Tropical cyclone rainfall

In a tropical cyclone, moist air converges and rises in organised thunderstorms, especially around the eyewall and rainbands. This can produce **very heavy rain**.

**Tropical cyclones generally do not have fronts.** Therefore, *cyclonic rainfall* and *frontal rainfall* are not exact synonyms.

### Indian examples

- **Western disturbances:** weather systems that bring winter rain to parts of northwestern India and snow to the western Himalayas. Mountain uplift can strengthen their precipitation.
- **Bay of Bengal and Arabian Sea cyclones:** can bring intense rain to coastal areas and farther inland.

**Remember: Low pressure or a front → rising moist air → rain.**

## Is monsoon rainfall a fourth type?

**Not in this classification.** A monsoon describes a seasonal change in wind circulation. The three types above describe **how air is lifted**.

The southwest monsoon supplies most of India's annual rainfall, but that rain can involve several mechanisms:

- **Mountains lift moist winds:** orographic rainfall.
- **Heating produces thunderstorms:** convectional rainfall.
- **Monsoon lows and depressions organise rising air:** rainfall associated with low-pressure systems.

**One rain event can involve more than one mechanism.** These categories help us understand the dominant cause; they are not watertight boxes.

## Quick revision

- **Heat lifts air → convectional.** Think: hot afternoon thunderstorm.
- **Hills lift air → orographic.** Think: Western Ghats and rain shadow.
- **Low pressure or fronts lift air → cyclonic/frontal.** Think: cyclones and western disturbances.

**Common process:** air rises, cools and forms clouds; sufficiently large drops fall as rain.

**Common mistakes:** Mountains do not create water; leeward areas are not always rainless; not every cloud produces rain; not every cyclone has a front; and monsoon rain is not exclusively orographic.
