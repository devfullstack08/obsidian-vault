### 1. Find the opening sentence first

The first sentence usually:
- introduces the **person/topic/concept**
- does not depend on a previous sentence
- usually does **not** begin with words like **he, she, it, they, this, these, such, therefore, however, consequently**

Example:

A. **This discovery changed modern medicine.**  
B. **Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928.**

B must come before A because **“This discovery”** refers to Fleming's discovery.

So if options are:

A) A-B-C-D  
B) B-A-D-C  
C) C-A-B-D  
D) A-C-D-B

Immediately eliminate **A and D** because they start with **A**.

---

### 2. Don't find the complete order — find a **mandatory pair**

This is probably the most powerful technique.

Suppose:

**P:** The government introduced a new policy.  
**Q:** However, many experts criticized **this policy**.  
**R:** The criticism mainly concerned its cost.  
**S:** The policy was intended to increase employment.

You can identify:

**P/S → Q**

because **this policy** must refer to an already introduced policy.

If the options are:

A. P-Q-R-S  
B. P-S-Q-R  
C. Q-P-S-R  
D. R-Q-P-S

You can eliminate **C and D** immediately because Q cannot come before the policy is introduced.

Then compare only A and B.

This is much faster than arranging all four sentences yourself.

---

### 3. Use the **pronoun rule**

Whenever you see:
**he, she, they, it, this, that, these, those, such, his, her, their**

ask:
> **Who/what does this word refer to?**

The noun usually appears earlier.

Example:
**A. He later became one of India's greatest scientists.**  
**B. C. V. Raman was born in 1888.**

Mandatory:
**B → A**

Never:
❌ A → B

because **He** needs an antecedent.

---

### 4. Use article clues: **A/An → The**

This is excellent for para-jumbles.

First mention:

> **A man entered the room.**

Later:

> **The man approached the officer.**

So:

**A man → The man**

Similarly:

**A problem → the problem**  
**An experiment → the experiment**  
**A theory → the theory**

Think:

> **A = introduction**  
> **The = already known**

---

### 5. Demonstratives usually point backward

Words like:

**this, that, these, those, such**

usually need something before them.

Example:

**A. Air pollution has increased dramatically in major cities.**  
**B. This problem has serious health consequences.**

A → B.

So **“This problem...”** is unlikely to be the opener.

---

### 6. Connectors tell you the relationship

Learn these groups:

| Connector                        | What must exist before it? |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| **However / Nevertheless / Yet** | contrasting idea           |
| **Therefore / Thus / Hence**     | cause/reason               |
| **Consequently / As a result**   | cause                      |
| **For example / For instance**   | general statement          |
| **Similarly / Likewise**         | comparable idea            |
| **Moreover / Furthermore**       | previous supporting point  |
| **Instead**                      | rejected/alternative idea  |

Example:

**A. Exercise improves cardiovascular health.**  
**B. Moreover, it reduces stress.**

A → B.

**Moreover** cannot normally introduce the entire topic.

---

## 7. General statement → explanation → example

This pattern appears constantly.

> **General idea → Explanation → Example → Conclusion**

Example:

**P:** Technology has transformed education.  
**Q:** Students can now access learning material from anywhere.  
**R:** For example, online platforms provide thousands of free courses.  
**S:** Therefore, education has become more accessible.

Natural flow:

**P → Q → R → S**

You don't even need deep comprehension.

Notice the signals:

**Topic → detail → “For example” → “Therefore”**

---

## 8. Cause must normally come before result

Look for:

**because, therefore, consequently, thus, hence, as a result**

Example:

**A. Heavy rainfall continued for three days.**  
**B. Consequently, several roads were flooded.**

A → B.

Memory:

> **Reason → Therefore → Result**

---

## 9. Chronological order

For historical/scientific passages, dates and stages are powerful.

Example:

**Born → educated → started career → achievement → death**

If you see:

**In 1947...**  
**A few years later...**  
**Eventually...**

you already have a structure.

Words like:

**Initially → Later → Subsequently → Eventually → Finally**

are almost a ready-made sequence.

---

## 10. The most important option-elimination method: compare positions

Suppose you have:

A. **P-Q-R-S**  
B. **P-R-Q-S**  
C. **Q-P-S-R**  
D. **R-S-P-Q**

Don't solve four sentences.

First ask:

**Can Q be first?** No → eliminate C.

**Can R be first?** No → eliminate D.

Now only:

A. P-Q-R-S  
B. P-R-Q-S

You only need to decide:

> **Q or R comes after P?**

A 4-sentence problem has become a **2-sentence problem**.

That's exactly how you should use options.

---

# Your exam technique

When you see a huge para-jumble, **don't start reading every sentence deeply**.

Use this sequence:

**Step 1:** Look at all options first.  
**Step 2:** Check which sentences are possible openers.  
**Step 3:** Eliminate impossible starting sentences.  
**Step 4:** Find **one mandatory pair**.  
**Step 5:** Eliminate options that break that pair.  
**Step 6:** Find another pair only if required.  
**Step 7:** Read the final sequence once to verify coherence.

Your goal is **not**:

> “I must arrange P, Q, R, S perfectly.”

Your goal is:

> **“What is definitely impossible?”**

That change in thinking makes para-jumbles much faster.

### Golden clues to memorize

**Pronoun → find its noun before it**  
**This/That → find what it refers to**  
**The → often already introduced**  
**However → contrast before it**  
**Therefore → reason before it**  
**For example → general statement before it**  
**Moreover → previous supporting point**  
**Later/Eventually → earlier event before it**  
**Question → answer/explanation afterward**

And one especially useful rule:

> **Don't solve the paragraph. Solve the OPTIONS.**

For a long SSC CGL Tier-2 para-jumble, very often **2–3 logical links are enough to eliminate the other options**; you don't need to understand every difficult word.
