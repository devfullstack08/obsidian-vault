- **Full stop (.)** → My idea is complete.
- **Comma (,)** → I need a small separation.
- **Semicolon (;)** → I have two closely related complete sentences.
- **Colon (:)** → I am about to explain, introduce or list something.

## **1. Full Stop (.)**

**Rule:** We use a full stop to end a complete statement or sentence.

A complete sentence normally expresses a complete thought.

**Examples:**

- I am preparing for the SSC CGL examination.
    
- India became independent in 1947.
    
- She is an intelligent student.
    

### **When to use it**

**1. At the end of a statement/sentence**

> I study English every day.
> Negative: He does not like coffee.
> Indirect question: She asked me where I lived.

Notice that this is an indirect question, so it ends with a full stop rather than a question mark.

**Important:** We do not normally use a full stop after a direct question or an exclamation.

- Where are you going? ✅
- What a beautiful place! ✅

**2.  After abreviations**.
 we need pears, apple, strawberries etc. to make jam.
 A European country: i.e Spain, France, Germany

**3. In numbers.**

**4. After titles in American English.**

Mr. Jones (AME.)   Mr Jones(BE.)
Dr. Sanger.              Dr Sanger

**5. Confusion with Inverted comas ".".**
- AME: At the end of a sentence in American English it always comes before the closing inverted coma.
	- He said, "Come back.".
- BE: In British English it depends whether you have a full sentence said inside the inverted comas or if you are ending the full sentence outside the inverted comas.
	- He said, "come back .
- If the inverted comas are in the middle of a sentence we always use a coma at the end, even if it a full sentence.
	- He said, "Please arrive at 10 o'clock," and then he told me that it didn't matter if I was a bit late.

**6. Confusion with brackets**
put the full stop inside the brackets when the word in brackets make a full sentence. put the full stop outside the bracets when the word in brackets are part of a sentece.

- i recommend you use fresh flowers(when possible.)
- I recommend you use fresh flowers.(this is not absolutely essential.)

## **2. Comma (,)**

**Rule:** A comma separates parts of a sentence without ending the sentence.
Comma = a small pause that separates ideas and prevents confusion.

This is called a **comma splice** because two independent clauses are incorrectly joined with only a comma.

**A. To separate items in a list**

> I bought books, pens, pencils, and notebooks.
> She is **smart, hardworking, and honest**.

The comma before _and_ is called the **Oxford comma**. It is optional in many styles but can improve clarity.

**B. Before coordinating conjunctions joining two independent clauses**

The main coordinating conjunctions are **FANBOYS**:

For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So.

> I wanted to go outside, but it was raining.
>  I wanted to go, **but** I was tired
>  She studied hard, **so** she passed.


**Rule:** Independent clause + comma + coordinating conjunction + independent clause.

But not usually:

- She studied hard **and passed**.  
    No comma needed because “passed” is not a separate full sentence.


**C. After an introductory phrase or clause**

> After completing my homework, I went outside.
> Although he was tired, he continued studying.
> However, I disagree.
> In the morning, we went for a walk.
> After finishing the work, he went home.

 **D. To add non-essential information**

> Mahatma Gandhi, who was born in 1869, led India's freedom struggle.
> My brother, **who lives in Delhi,** is a doctor.
> Mr Sharma, **our English teacher,** is absent today.

The information between the commas adds extra details. The main sentence still makes sense without it.

**E. To address someone directly**

> Rahul, please close the door. 
> Rahul, come here.
> Thank you, sir.
>  Please listen, **sir**.
>  Students, open your books.

**F. With yes, no, well, etc.**

> Yes, I understand.
> No, I don’t agree.
> Well, we can try again.

**G. In direct speech**

>He said, **“I am ready.”**
>"Come here", she said.

**H. To separate dates and places**

>September 2, 2026
>Surat, Gujarat, India
### **Important mistake**

❌ I am preparing for SSC CGL, I study every day.

✅ I am preparing for SSC CGL. I study every day.

✅ I am preparing for SSC CGL, and I study every day.

✅ I am preparing for SSC CGL; I study every day.
 ### Important point

A comma can change the meaning:

- **Let’s eat, Grandma.** ✅  
    = speaking to Grandma
    
- **Let’s eat Grandma.** 😄  
    = sounds like Grandma is the food

## **3. Semicolon (;)**

**Rule:** A semicolon mainly connects two closely related independent clauses without using a coordinating conjunction.

Think of it as a stronger separation than a comma but a closer connection than a full stop. (A semicolon is stronger than a comma but weaker than a full stop.)

 **A. To join two related complete sentences**

> I studied hard; I passed the examination.
> I was tired; I continued working.
> She loves reading; her brother prefers sports.

Both parts can stand independently.

1. I studied hard
2. I passed the examination.

However, the ideas are closely connected, so a semicolon is appropriate.

Compare:

- I studied hard. I passed the examination. ✅
    
- I studied hard; I passed the examination. ✅
    
- I studied hard, I passed the examination. ❌

Both sides can stand alone as full sentences.

**B.  Before conjunctive adverbs such as  however, therefore, nevertheless, moreover, consequently when they connect two complete sentences**

> He worked very hard; however, he failed the examination.
> I studied hard; **however,** I did not pass.
> He was ill; **therefore,** he stayed home.
> She was ill; nevertheless, she attended the examination.
> He did not prepare well; therefore, he failed.

Notice:  
semicolon + however + comma

Structure:
Independent clause + semicolon + conjunctive adverb + comma + independent clause.

**C. To separate complicated items in a list** when the items already contain commas**

When list items already contain commas, use semicolons for clarity.

> We visited Surat, Gujarat; Jaipur, Rajasthan; and Mumbai, Maharashtra.

Without semicolons, it may be difficult to distinguish the cities and states.
 
 ### Semicolon vs comma

❌ I was tired, I continued working.  
This is a **comma splice**.

✅ I was tired; I continued working.  
✅ I was tired, but I continued working.  
✅ I was tired. I continued working.


❌ Although he was tired; he continued studying.

✅ Although he was tired, he continued studying.

Why? _Although he was tired_ is a dependent clause, not an independent clause. Therefore, a comma is appropriate.

### Easy memory

**Semicolon = connects two complete, closely related thoughts.**

A useful test is:

> **Sentence 1; Sentence 2**

If both parts can be complete sentences by themselves, a semicolon may be correct.

## **4. Colon (:)**

**Rule:** A colon introduces information that explains, illustrates or expands on what comes before it.

 **A. To introduce a list**

> I need three things: books, pens, and notebooks.

**Important:** The words before the colon should normally form a complete clause.

✅ I bought three fruits: apples, bananas, and oranges.

❌ I bought: apples, bananas, and oranges.

The second example is incorrect in ordinary prose because the colon unnecessarily separates the verb _bought_ from its object.

 **B. To introduce an explanation**

> He had only one goal: to become an IAS officer.

The information after the colon explains what the goal was.


**C. To introduce an example**

> There is one major problem: unemployment.

**D. To introduce a quotation**

> The teacher gave us one instruction: "Always revise your notes."

**E. To emphasise a conclusion**

> The result was clear: hard work had paid off.
