## 1. Introduction

GOD - The **Somnath Temple** is an important Hindu temple dedicated to **Lord Shiva**. 
Geography - It is situated at **Prabhas Patan**, near Veraval, on the Arabian Sea coast in the Gir Somnath district of Gujarat.

Somnath is traditionally regarded as the **first among the twelve Jyotirlingas** of Shiva. The site has religious associations with the Moon god Chandra, Lord Krishna, the Puranas and the wider sacred landscape of Prabhas Kshetra.

Somnath is historically significant because it:

- developed into an important pilgrimage and temple centre;
- was attacked and reconstructed multiple times;
- was raided by Mahmud of Ghazni in 1025–26;
- became a symbol of cultural revival after Indian independence;
- generated an important debate about the meaning of secularism in the new Indian republic.

> [!summary]  
> Somnath represents the intersection of **religion, political authority, cultural memory, architecture, nationalism and secularism**.

---

# 2. Meaning of the Name Somnath

The word **Somnath** consists of:

- **Soma** — the Moon god;
- **Natha** — lord or protector.

Therefore, Somnath means:

> **Lord of the Moon**

According to Puranic tradition, Chandra—the Moon god—was cursed by his father-in-law Daksha because Chandra favoured his wife Rohini over Daksha’s other daughters. Chandra worshipped Shiva at Prabhas, and Shiva relieved him from the curse. Shiva was consequently worshipped here as **Somnath**.

This is a **religious tradition**, not a historically datable event.

---

# 3. Prabhas Patan

## 3.1 Location

Prabhas Patan is situated:

- near Veraval;
- in Gir Somnath district;
- on the western coast of Gujarat;
- close to the Arabian Sea.

Somnath Temple stands near the seashore at Prabhas Patan.

## 3.2 Other names

Prabhas Patan has also been associated with names such as:

- Prabhas Kshetra;
- Prabhas Tirtha;
- Dev Patan;
- Somnath Patan.

## 3.3 Religious importance

Prabhas Patan is a wider sacred landscape, not merely the location of one temple.

It is associated with:

- Somnath Jyotirlinga;
- Triveni Sangam
- Bhalka Tirth (The temple complex features an idol of Lord Krishna in a **semi-reclining position** (shayan mudra) with an arrow in his foot, alongside a statue of the hunter Jara.)
- Lord Krishna’s final journey;
- Dehotsarg or Golok Dham;
- Parashurama;
- the Pandavas;
- Balarama;
- ancient Shaiva traditions.

The Somnath Trust identifies Bhalka Tirth as the place where Krishna was struck by the arrow of the hunter Jara. According to tradition, Krishna later reached the area near the confluence of the Hiran, Kapila and Saraswati rivers and departed for his divine abode.

## 3.4 Triveni Sangam

The sacred confluence at Prabhas is traditionally linked with:

- Hiran River;
- Kapila River;
- Saraswati River;
- the Arabian Sea.

It is considered an important place for ritual bathing and ancestral rites.

## 3.5 Historical character of Prabhas Patan

Prabhas Patan developed as:

- a pilgrimage centre;
- a coastal settlement;
- a centre of temple activity;
- a place of trade and movement;
- an important cultural centre of Saurashtra.

The region contains older caves, traditional houses, gateways, temples and other heritage locations. The Somnath Trust identifies more than twenty historical and religious sites around Prabhas Patan.

---

# 4. Religious and Literary Traditions

Somnath and Prabhas are associated with texts and traditions including:

- Shiva Purana;
- Skanda Purana, especially Prabhas Khanda;
- Bhagavata Purana;
- Mahabharata traditions;
- regional sacred literature.

According to temple tradition:

1. Soma built a golden temple.
2. Ravana built a silver temple.
3. Krishna built a temple of sandalwood(ચંદન).
4. Later historical rulers constructed stone temples.

These accounts belong primarily to the realm(ક્ષેત્ર) of **sacred tradition and mythology**. They should not be presented as securely dated archaeological history.

> [!important]  
> In examination answers, clearly distinguish:
> 
> - **religious tradition**, based on Puranic accounts;
> - **historical evidence**, based on inscriptions, archaeology and chronicles.

# 5. Early Historical Development

The exact date of the first historical Somnath temple is uncertain.

However, the site was an established and prosperous pilgrimage centre by the early medieval period. Its wealth probably came from:

- royal grants;
- donations by merchants and pilgrims;
- land endowments;
- temple-related economic activity;
- its position in western India’s coastal network.

Somnath was not merely a place of worship. Like many major medieval temples, it likely performed wider functions as:

- a landholder;
- an employer;
- a centre of ritual;
- a receiver of donations;
- a cultural institution;
- a symbol of regional authority.

---

# 6. Early Temple Construction and Reconstruction

Different traditions and historical accounts refer to several phases of construction.

## 6.1 Early medieval rulers

The temple is often connected with rulers of western India, including:

- Maitrakas of Vallabhi;
- Pratiharas;
- Chaulukyas or Solankis;
- regional rulers of Saurashtra.

The exact sequence of the earliest temples is difficult to establish with complete certainty.

## 6.2 Bhima I and Bhoja

After Mahmud of Ghazni’s raid, the temple is traditionally said to have been restored under:

- **Bhima I**, the Chaulukya ruler of Gujarat;
- **Bhoja**, the Paramara ruler of Malwa.

Later traditions remember these rulers as major contributors to the revival of Somnath.

## 6.3 Kumarapala

The Chaulukya ruler **Kumarapala** is associated with a major restoration of Somnath during the twelfth century. Official accounts of Somnath’s reconstruction history identify Kumarapala as one of the important rulers who restored the shrine.

---

# 7. Mahmud of Ghazni

## 7.1 Background

Mahmud of Ghazni ruled the Ghaznavid Empire from 998 to 1030 CE.

His capital was at Ghazni in present-day Afghanistan.

He conducted repeated military expeditions into the Indian subcontinent. His objectives included:

- obtaining wealth;
- expanding political influence;
- controlling strategic territories;
- strengthening his prestige;
- presenting himself as a champion of Islam;
- financing his court, army and empire.

Therefore, his campaigns had interconnected:

- political;
- economic;
- military;
- religious dimensions.

## 7.2 Raid on Somnath

Mahmud marched towards Somnath in **1025 CE**, and the attack is commonly dated to late 1025 or January 1026.

He travelled through Multan and the desert routes of Rajasthan and Gujarat. Somnath reportedly offered resistance, but Mahmud’s forces captured and plundered the temple. The principal sacred image was broken, and substantial wealth was taken.

## 7.3 Why did Mahmud attack Somnath?

### Economic motive

Somnath was believed to possess:

- valuable donations;
- precious objects;
- accumulated temple wealth;
- offerings made by rulers, merchants and pilgrims.

Raiding such a wealthy institution could provide enormous resources.

### Political motive

The raid enhanced Mahmud’s reputation as a powerful conqueror.

It demonstrated that his forces could penetrate deep into western India.

### Religious and ideological motive

Persian chronicles celebrated Mahmud as an iconoclast who destroyed idols and attacked non-Islamic religious centres.

Religious language helped legitimise his campaigns before his court and supporters.

### Strategic prestige

Somnath was a famous and respected shrine. Capturing it carried symbolic prestige beyond ordinary territorial conquest.

> [!exam-note]  
> Avoid reducing Mahmud’s raid to a single motive.
> 
> A balanced answer should mention:
> 
> - economic plunder;
> - political prestige;
> - imperial ambition;
> - religious iconoclasm.

## 7.4 Was Mahmud trying to permanently conquer Gujarat?

Mahmud’s Somnath campaign was primarily a raid rather than a successful attempt to establish lasting direct rule over Gujarat.

He returned towards Ghazni with the acquired wealth. Britannica’s historical summary notes that many of Mahmud’s Indian campaigns greatly enriched Ghazni but did not necessarily produce permanent territorial control outside the north-western region.

## 7.5 Legends about the raid

Later narratives contain dramatic stories, including:

- an image filled with jewels;
- Brahmins offering Mahmud money to spare the image;
- fragments of the image being carried to Ghazni;
- huge and sometimes exaggerated casualty figures.

These stories must be treated carefully because:

- some were written long after the raid;
- chroniclers used literary exaggeration;
- later political communities reshaped the memory of the event;
- not all details are supported by contemporary evidence.

For example, the story that the Shiva linga was hollow and contained hidden jewels is generally regarded as a later dramatic legend rather than reliable evidence.

## 7.6 Historical significance

The raid became important not only because of what occurred in 1025–26, but because later generations interpreted it differently.

Somnath became:

- a symbol of Ghaznavid victory in Persian chronicles;
- a symbol of religious destruction in Hindu memory;
- a subject of colonial historiography;
- a symbol of cultural resilience in modern India;
- a political reference in debates over national identity.

# 8. Later Attacks and Reconstructions

After Mahmud’s raid, Somnath was rebuilt.

The later medieval history includes phases of destruction, damage, conversion, restoration and renewed worship.

## 8.1 Alauddin Khalji’s Gujarat campaign

In 1299, forces of Delhi Sultan **Alauddin Khalji**, led by commanders including Ulugh Khan, invaded Gujarat and attacked Somnath.

The temple was again damaged or plundered.

## 8.2 Chudasama reconstruction

The temple was restored under the Chudasama rulers of Saurashtra.

The Gujarat government’s district history records that:

- Mahipala I rebuilt the temple around 1308;
- his son Khengara restored the linga between approximately 1331 and 1351.

## 8.3 Zafar Khan

In 1395, Zafar Khan—then governor of Gujarat and later founder of the Gujarat Sultanate—is associated with another attack on Somnath.

## 8.4 Gujarat Sultanate period

Further damage is associated with later rulers of the Gujarat Sultanate.

The shrine’s condition and religious use changed across different political periods.

## 8.5 Mughal period

Aurangzeb is associated in later records with orders affecting the temple near the end of the seventeenth or beginning of the eighteenth century.

However, precise claims such as “Somnath was destroyed exactly seventeen times” should be used cautiously. Different lists count:

- raids;
- partial damage;
- demolition;
- conversion;
- rebuilding

in different ways.

> [!warning]  
> The popular statement that Somnath was “destroyed seventeen times” is widely repeated but should not be treated as an uncontested, precisely documented historical count.

---

# 9. Ahilyabai Holkar’s Reconstruction

## 9.1 Ahilyabai Holkar

Ahilyabai Holkar was the eighteenth-century Maratha ruler of Indore.

She sponsored the construction and restoration of many Hindu temples and pilgrimage centres across India.

## 9.2 Somnath temple built by Ahilyabai

In **1782–83**, Ahilyabai Holkar constructed a new Somnath shrine near the remains of the older temple.

This temple allowed continued worship during a period when the earlier monumental temple no longer functioned in its former condition. The Ahilyabai temple remains adjacent to the present temple complex.

## 9.3 Importance

Ahilyabai’s temple:

- preserved the continuity of worship;
- revived pilgrimage activity;
- connected Maratha patronage with sacred geography;
- demonstrated that religious institutions could survive political disruption.

---

# 10. Somnath under British Rule

During British rule, Somnath became an object of:

- archaeological curiosity;
- colonial historical writing;
- religious memory;
- political symbolism.

## 10.1 Colonial interpretation

British writers often presented Indian medieval history as a continuous conflict between:

- Hindus;
- Muslims.

The destruction of Somnath was sometimes used to support the colonial argument that British rule was necessary to maintain order between allegedly permanently hostile communities.

This interpretation oversimplified:

- regional politics;
- alliances across religious lines;
- economic motives behind temple raids;
- the changing role of temples;
- the diversity of medieval Indian society.

## 10.2 The “Gates of Somnath” controversy

In 1842, Governor-General **Lord Ellenborough** announced that wooden gates taken from the tomb of Mahmud at Ghazni would be brought to India as the supposed original gates of Somnath.

They were ceremonially carried to India but were later found unlikely to have been the original Somnath gates.

The episode illustrates how the colonial government used historical symbolism for political purposes.

---

# 11. Junagadh and the Political Context of 1947

Somnath was located within the princely state of Junagadh.

## 11.1 Junagadh accession(જોડાણ) crisis

At independence:

- Junagadh had a Muslim Nawab;
- the majority of its population was Hindu;
- the Nawab announced accession to Pakistan;
- the state was geographically surrounded by Indian territory except for its coastline;
- popular opposition developed;
- the administration eventually collapsed;
- Indian forces entered following an invitation from the Dewan;
- a plebiscite later confirmed accession to India.

Somnath’s reconstruction began in this atmosphere of:

- political integration;
- the end of princely rule;
- national consolidation;
- recovery of cultural confidence after colonial rule and Partition.

---

# 12. Reconstruction after Independence

## 12.1 Sardar Patel’s visit

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel visited the ruins of Somnath in November 1947, shortly after the resolution of the Junagadh crisis.

Official accounts give the visit as 12 or 13 November 1947. At Prabhas Patan, Patel publicly expressed the determination to reconstruct the temple.

## 12.2 Public funding rather than state funding

The initial idea included government involvement in reconstruction.

However, Mahatma Gandhi supported reconstruction on the condition that:

> the money should come from public donations rather than the state treasury.

Accordingly, reconstruction was primarily undertaken through:

- public subscriptions;
- donations;
- the Somnath Trust.

Munshi later recorded that Gandhi approved the proposal but advised that the temple should be rebuilt with public money.

## 12.3 Creation of the Somnath Trust

The Shree Somnath Trust was formed to:

- oversee reconstruction;
- receive donations;
- administer the temple;
- manage surrounding religious institutions;
- develop Prabhas Patan.

## 12.4 Foundation of the new temple

Important stages included:

- **19 April 1950:** ground-breaking ceremony associated with U. N. Dhebar, Chief Minister of Saurashtra;
- **8 May 1950:** foundation stone laid by Jam Saheb Digvijaysinhji of Nawanagar;
- **11 May 1951:** consecration performed by President Rajendra Prasad.

---

# 13. Role of Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel

## 13.1 Initiator of the modern reconstruction

Patel provided the decisive political and moral support for reconstruction.

After visiting the ruins, he viewed Somnath’s restoration as:

- the recovery of a major sacred site;
- an expression of national self-confidence;
- a correction of historical neglect;
- a symbol of cultural regeneration after foreign rule.

## 13.2 Relationship with integration of Junagadh

Patel’s Somnath initiative was closely connected with the integration of Junagadh into India.

The moment carried two parallel meanings:

1. territorial consolidation of the Indian Union;
2. cultural restoration of an ancient pilgrimage centre.

## 13.3 Patel’s approach to funding

Although Patel supported rebuilding, the project ultimately proceeded mainly through public donations rather than direct Union government financing.

This distinction allowed supporters to argue that:

- the state was not establishing a religion;
- citizens were voluntarily restoring a cultural and religious institution.

## 13.4 Patel’s death

Sardar Patel died on **15 December 1950**, before the consecration ceremony of May 1951.

Therefore, he did not witness the completion of the first major stage of reconstruction.

> [!summary]  
> Patel gave the reconstruction its **political momentum, national importance and organisational beginning**.

---

# 14. Role of K. M. Munshi

## 14.1 Background

**Kanhaiyalal Maneklal Munshi** was:

- a lawyer;
- freedom fighter;
- writer;
- member of the Constituent Assembly;
- Union minister;
- cultural thinker;
- founder of Bharatiya Vidya Bhavan.

He was born in Gujarat and had a strong interest in Indian history and culture.

## 14.2 Principal organiser

After Patel’s death, Munshi became one of the most important figures carrying the reconstruction project forward.

His contributions included:

- organising the reconstruction;
- supporting the formation and work of the Trust;
- collecting public support;
- coordinating with rulers and administrators;
- defending the project against criticism;
- presenting Somnath as a symbol of cultural continuity.

## 14.3 Munshi’s interpretation

Munshi did not see Somnath merely as a religious building.

For him, it represented:

- the vitality of Indian civilisation;
- recovery from conquest and colonialism;
- national dignity;
- the continuity of Indian culture;
- the right of society to restore its heritage.

## 14.4 _Somanatha: The Shrine Eternal_

Munshi wrote the book:

> **Somanatha: The Shrine Eternal**

The work presents:

- the sacred history of Somnath;
- its destruction and reconstruction;
- its role in cultural memory;
- the modern reconstruction movement.

It is valuable for understanding the perspective of the reconstruction’s strongest advocate, though it should be read alongside critical historical scholarship.

## 14.5 Munshi and Nehru

Nehru expressed discomfort with ministers associating the government with the rebuilding of a religious structure.

In a letter dated 20 July 1950, Nehru told Munshi that the government could not undertake the construction of religious edifices and warned that state involvement could create demands for favouring multiple religions.

Munshi defended the reconstruction as:

- a cultural act;
- an expression of public will;
- an act funded through voluntary contributions;
- compatible with Indian civilisation and national renewal.

> [!summary]  
> If Patel was the political initiator, **Munshi was the intellectual defender and principal organiser** of the reconstruction.

---

# 15. Role of Jam Saheb Digvijaysinhji

The Jam Saheb of Nawanagar, Digvijaysinhji:

- supported the reconstruction;
- served as an important office-bearer of the Somnath Trust;
- laid the foundation stone on 8 May 1950;
- contributed resources and prestige;
- helped supervise later construction.

The temple’s later ceremonial development continued under his guidance.

# 16. Architecture of the Present Temple

## 16.1 Architectural style

The present temple was constructed in the **Kailash Mahameru Prasad style**, part of the western Indian or Maru-Gurjara temple tradition.

## 16.2 Architect

The design was prepared by **Prabhashankar Sompura**, belonging to a traditional family of temple architects.

## 16.3 Main components

The major parts include:

- **Garbhagriha** — sanctum containing the Jyotirlinga;
- **Sabhamandapa** — assembly hall;
- **Nrityamandapa** — ceremonial or dance hall;
- **Shikhara** — tower above the sanctum;
- sculptural and architectural ornamentation.

The temple was completed in stages. The Trust records that the reconstructed temple was formally dedicated to the nation by President Shankar Dayal Sharma on **1 December 1995**.

## 16.4 Bāṇastambha

A pillar known as the **Bāṇastambha** stands near the seaward side of the temple.

An inscription associated with it conveys the traditional idea that there is no landmass in a straight line southwards between Somnath and the South Pole.

The pillar symbolises:

- the temple’s coastal location;
- India’s geographical imagination;
- the connection between sacred space and the ocean.

---

# 17. Dr Rajendra Prasad and the Consecration

## 17.1 Invitation to the President

India’s first President, **Dr Rajendra Prasad**, was invited to perform the consecration(પવિત્રતા) or _Pran Pratishtha_ ceremony.

Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru advised him against attending in his official capacity because Nehru feared that the President’s participation could make the ceremony appear to be a government-sponsored religious event.

Rajendra Prasad replied that:

- the temple had been built through private subscriptions;
- he visited religious institutions of different faiths;
- his attendance did not mean that the Indian state had adopted a religion.

Nehru ultimately did not constitutionally prevent him from attending. Their correspondence shows that the President participated despite the Prime Minister’s reservations.

## 17.2 Consecration ceremony

On **11 May 1951**, Rajendra Prasad performed the _Pran Pratishtha_ of the reconstructed Somnath Temple.

## 17.3 Meaning of his participation

Rajendra Prasad viewed Somnath as:

- a symbol of reconstruction;
- evidence that creative power is greater than destructive power;
- a sign of India’s cultural continuity;
- a restored national heritage site.

His participation represented a different understanding of secularism from Nehru’s.

For Rajendra Prasad:

- secularism did not require public officials to be personally detached from religion;
- the state should respect all faiths;
- a President could attend religious functions without establishing an official religion.

---

# 18. Jawaharlal Nehru’s Position

## 18.1 Nehru did not simply oppose private worship

Nehru’s central objection was not that private individuals should not rebuild Somnath.

His concern was that:

- the Union government;
- state governments;
- ministers;
- embassies;
- the President

should not create the impression that the Indian state officially sponsored one religion.

## 18.2 Letter to Munshi, July 1950

Nehru wrote that the government could not undertake building religious edifices because doing so would raise demands to favour many religions. He also considered large expenditure on a temple difficult to justify amid housing shortages and economic hardship.

## 18.3 Concern over the installation ceremony

Nehru objected when Indian embassies were reportedly asked to collect:

- river water;
- soil;
- natural objects

from foreign countries for the Somnath ceremony.

He feared that such involvement made a private religious event appear to be an official diplomatic and governmental activity.

## 18.4 Letter to Rajendra Prasad

In April 1951, Nehru wrote that Somnath was acquiring political and international significance. He described the ceremony as appearing “revivalist” and stressed that the Government of India had no official connection with it.

## 18.5 Government expenditure

Nehru also criticised reports that the Saurashtra government intended to spend ₹5 lakh on the installation ceremony.

His objection focused on:

- public expenditure;
- constitutional neutrality;
- the appearance of government sponsorship.

# 19. Debate over Secularism

The Somnath controversy became one of the earliest debates about the nature of secularism in independent India.

## 19.1 Nehruvian interpretation

Nehru’s approach emphasised:

- institutional distance between the state and organised religion;
- avoidance of official religious symbolism;
- equal citizenship;
- scientific temper;
- prevention of majoritarian dominance;
- caution against revivalist nationalism.

Under this view:

> The government may protect religious freedom and heritage, but it should not appear to sponsor the religious project of one community.

## 19.2 Patel–Munshi–Rajendra Prasad interpretation

This perspective emphasised:

- equal respect for all religions;
- cultural restoration;
- the civilisational importance of sacred institutions;
- voluntary public participation;
- distinction between supporting heritage and establishing a state religion.

Under this view:

> A secular state need not reject religion; it can respect and engage with different religious traditions without discrimination.

## 19.3 Public funds versus private funds

The funding question was central.

The temple itself was primarily reconstructed through:

- donations;
- public subscriptions;
- the Somnath Trust.

This reduced—but did not entirely eliminate—the controversy because:

- ministers were involved;
- the President attended;
- the Saurashtra administration had connections with the event;
- official offices and embassies were approached.

## 19.4 Positive secularism

India’s model of secularism is often described as different from a strict “wall of separation.”

It may involve:

- equal respect for all religions;
- state intervention in social reform;
- administration of some religious institutions;
- financial or legal support for different religious communities;
- protection of religious heritage.

Somnath revealed the tension between:

1. **state neutrality through distance**, and
2. **state neutrality through equal engagement**.

## 19.5 Was Nehru against reconstruction?

A balanced answer should state:

- Nehru did not prevent private reconstruction;
- he opposed direct government responsibility for constructing religious buildings;
- he objected to official expenditure and diplomatic involvement;
- he feared that the event could be seen as sectarian or revivalist;
- he advised Rajendra Prasad not to attend but did not prevent him from doing so.

## 19.6 Was Rajendra Prasad violating secularism?

Supporters argued that he attended:

- in a personal capacity;
- as a religiously inclined citizen;
- just as he attended places of worship belonging to different religions.

Critics argued that:

- the President’s office could not easily be separated from his person;
- his presence gave the event official legitimacy;
- the head of a secular republic should avoid identification with a particular religious revival.

## 19.7 Continuing relevance

The Somnath debate continues to influence discussions about:

- government participation in religious ceremonies;
- restoration of places of worship;
- public funding for religious heritage;
- constitutional secularism;
- cultural nationalism;
- the personal faith of constitutional authorities;
- the difference between religion and civilisation.

> [!mains-analysis]  
> The Somnath controversy was not simply a conflict between “religious” and “secular” leaders. It represented competing visions of Indian secularism:
> 
> - Nehru emphasised **institutional restraint**;
> - Munshi and Rajendra Prasad emphasised **cultural inclusion and equal respect**.

# 20. Somnath and Indian Nationalism

Somnath became a symbol with several meanings.

## 20.1 Cultural nationalism

For supporters of reconstruction, Somnath represented:

- recovery of civilisational dignity;
- resistance to historical destruction;
- revival after colonialism;
- national cultural self-confidence.

## 20.2 Constitutional nationalism

For Nehru and similar thinkers, the republic’s legitimacy came from:

- equal citizenship;
- secular institutions;
- modern development;
- avoidance of religious majoritarianism.

## 20.3 Multiple memories

Somnath can therefore be remembered as:

- a sacred Jyotirlinga;
- an architectural monument;
- a site of invasion and reconstruction;
- a symbol of resilience;
- a symbol of cultural nationalism;
- a test case for constitutional secularism.

---

# 21. Somnath in Contemporary India

The present temple is:

- an active place of worship;
- a major pilgrimage centre;
- a significant tourist destination;
- a symbol of Gujarat’s religious heritage;
- administered by the Shree Somnath Trust.

The temple complex and surrounding area include:

- Ahilyabai Temple;
- Bhalka Tirth;
- Triveni Sangam;
- Dehotsarg;
- Gita Mandir;
- Parashurama Temple;
- heritage routes;
- museums and visitor facilities;
- sound-and-light presentations.

The fully reconstructed temple was dedicated to the nation in 1995. In 2026, official commemorations marked **75 years since the 1951 consecration**.

## Material and design

The modern structure was created as a functioning temple rather than as an archaeological conservation of the earlier ruins.

This itself produced a heritage debate:

- Should the ruins have been preserved as historical evidence?
- Or should Somnath have been rebuilt as a living sacred institution?

Munshi strongly supported reconstruction as a living temple, while some archaeologists preferred preservation of the ruins.

# 25. Historical Interpretation: Important Cautions

## 25.1 Mythology versus history

Do not treat Puranic accounts as precisely dated historical events.

Write:

> “According to Puranic tradition…”

rather than:

> “It is historically proved that…”

## 25.2 Number of destructions

Avoid confidently stating that Somnath was destroyed exactly seventeen times unless the source and counting method are explained.

A safer statement is:

> “Somnath underwent repeated attacks, destruction, damage and reconstruction between the eleventh and eighteenth centuries.”

## 25.3 Mahmud’s motives

Avoid presenting his raid as:

- purely religious; or
- purely economic.

Use a combination of:

- wealth;
- imperial prestige;
- military strategy;
- political legitimacy;
- religious iconoclasm.

## 25.4 Nehru’s position

Avoid saying:

> “Nehru opposed the existence of Somnath Temple.”

A more accurate formulation is:

> “Nehru opposed direct state association with and expenditure on the reconstruction and consecration of a religious temple.”

## 25.5 Patel versus Nehru

Do not reduce the debate to a simple personal rivalry.

It reflected deeper differences about:

- secularism;
- national culture;
- state responsibility;
- postcolonial identity.

# 28. Model Mains Conclusion

> Somnath is not merely the history of a temple repeatedly destroyed and rebuilt. It is the history of a sacred landscape, changing political powers, social memory and India’s search for a modern identity. Its post-independence reconstruction represented cultural resilience, while the disagreement between Nehru, Munshi and Rajendra Prasad exposed the complexity of Indian secularism. Somnath therefore occupies an important place in religious history, Gujarat’s heritage and the constitutional history of modern India.

# 29. One-Minute Revision

> Somnath Temple is located at Prabhas Patan near Veraval in Gujarat and is traditionally regarded as the first Jyotirlinga. It became a wealthy pilgrimage centre and was famously raided by Mahmud of Ghazni in 1025–26. The temple underwent repeated attacks and reconstructions; Ahilyabai Holkar built a shrine there in the eighteenth century. After independence, Sardar Patel initiated its reconstruction, while K. M. Munshi became its principal organiser. The project was financed mainly through public donations. President Rajendra Prasad consecrated the reconstructed temple on 11 May 1951 despite Prime Minister Nehru’s concern that official participation could conflict with the secular character of the state. Somnath consequently became a symbol of cultural resilience and a major case study in the debate over Indian secularism.
