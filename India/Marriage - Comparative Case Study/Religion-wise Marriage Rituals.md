# Religion-wise Marriage Rituals

Back to [[Marriage - Master Case Study]]. Regional examples: [[India — State and Union Territory Marriage Atlas]].

> [!important]
> A religion gives a theological/legal framework; culture supplies much of the clothing, music, food and surrounding celebration. Sect, denomination and country matter. The descriptions below are comparative foundations, not universal scripts.

## 1. Hindu traditions — *Vivaha*

### Core idea

In many Hindu traditions, *vivaha* is a **samskara**—a life-cycle sacrament—and establishes *grihastha*, the householder stage. Hindu law does not impose one pan-Indian script: Section 7 of the Hindu Marriage Act recognises the **customary rites and ceremonies of either party**. Where those rites include *saptapadi*, marriage becomes complete on the seventh step.

### Common sequence

**Before:** family agreement/engagement, horoscope in some families, auspicious date, Ganesh or family-deity worship, turmeric, mehendi and music.

**Central ceremony:** welcome of groom, garland exchange, *kanyadan* in communities that practise it, joining hands, sacred fire (*homa/agni*), offerings, circumambulation or steps, vows, *saptapadi*, sindoor and/or mangalasutra according to custom.

**After:** blessings, meal, departure, home-entry and games.

### Seven steps—broad themes

Interpretations vary, but the steps commonly invoke nourishment, strength, prosperity, shared duties, family/welfare, health/seasons and enduring friendship/companionship.

### Internal variation

- Tamil weddings may emphasise tali-tying and *oonjal*.
- Telugu weddings may use *jeelakarra-bellam* and *talambralu*.
- Bengali weddings use *shubho drishti* and distinctive seated/circling customs.
- Maharashtrian weddings use *antarpat* and *mangalashtaka*.
- Some communities do not make sacred fire or saptapadi their customary constitutive rite.

### Evolution and debate

Vedic/domestic texts supplied influential forms, but local, caste, tribal and devotional customs continually reshaped practice. Today some couples reinterpret *kanyadan* (“gift of a maiden”), add reciprocal vows, include both mothers, reject dowry or use a civil ceremony.

## 2. Islamic traditions — *Nikah*

### Core idea

In Islamic jurisprudence, marriage is a solemn **contract/covenant** with moral and legal obligations, not a priestly sacrament. Schools of law differ on details.

### Constitutive elements

- free consent of the parties
- clear offer and acceptance—*ijab* and *qubul*
- witnesses according to the applicable school/legal system
- *mahr*—an obligatory marital gift/right owed by the groom to the bride
- guardian (*wali*) requirements differ by school and circumstance

An imam/qazi commonly conducts or records the ceremony, but the contract’s valid elements—not elaborate celebration—are central. A *nikahnama* records terms. A sermon (*khutbat al-nikah*), Qur’anic recitation and supplication are common. The *walima* is the wedding feast associated with the groom/couple.

### Cultural layers

Mehendi, sehra, processions, music, segregated seating, henna nights and regional dishes are cultural and differ from Morocco to Kerala or Indonesia. They should not be mistaken for universally mandatory Islamic rites.

### Sectarian/legal variation

Sunni schools differ regarding guardians and witness formulation; Shia jurisprudence has distinct contract rules. Local civil law may require registration in addition to religious validity.

## 3. Christian traditions

### Core idea

Christian marriage is generally understood as a covenant before God and community. In Catholic and Orthodox traditions it is sacramental; Protestant understandings vary. Denominational and national rules matter.

### Common Western sequence

- preparation/counselling and legal formalities
- entrance procession
- scripture readings, sermon/homily and prayers
- declaration of intent and consent
- vows
- exchange/blessing of rings
- ministerial blessing, witnesses and register
- Eucharist/Communion in some services
- recessional and reception

### Major variations

- **Roman Catholic:** consent exchanged in canonical form; nuptial Mass may include Eucharist.
- **Eastern Orthodox:** betrothal/rings followed by crowning, shared cup and ceremonial procession (“dance of Isaiah”); vows may be less central than in Western forms.
- **Protestant/Anglican:** scripture, vows, rings and blessing; exact liturgy varies.
- **Indian Christianity:** Syrian Christian, Goan Catholic, Tamil, Khasi, Mizo and other communities combine church liturgy with regional clothes, tali/minnu, feast, music or household customs.

White gown, bouquet toss and cake are widespread modern-cultural practices, not universal Christian requirements.

## 4. Jewish traditions

### Core idea

Jewish marriage joins legal covenant, sanctification and community. Orthodox, Conservative/Masorti, Reform and regional communities differ, especially regarding gender roles and eligibility.

### Representative sequence

- signing or arranging the **ketubah** (marriage contract)
- ceremony under the **chuppah** canopy
- blessings over wine
- *kiddushin/erusin*: sanctification, traditionally with a ring
- public reading/presentation of the ketubah
- *nissuin*: seven blessings—**Sheva Brachot**
- breaking a glass
- private seclusion (*yichud*) in communities that practise it
- festive meal and dancing

### Symbolism

The chuppah represents the new household and openness; the ketubah states obligations; the broken glass has several interpretations, prominently remembrance of the destruction of Jerusalem even in joy.

### Indian Jewish traditions

Bene Israel, Cochin and Baghdadi Jewish communities preserved Jewish legal cores while using Indian languages, foods, garments and musical forms.

## 5. Sikh traditions — *Anand Karaj*

### Core idea

*Anand Karaj* means “ceremony of bliss.” The spiritual centre is Guru Granth Sahib; the four **Lavan** hymns describe stages of the soul’s journey toward the Divine and frame marital companionship.

### Sequence

- assembly before Guru Granth Sahib
- *ardaas* and guidance to the couple
- four Lavan are sung; the couple circles Guru Granth Sahib once with each hymn
- concluding hymns, *Anand Sahib*, *ardaas* and *hukamnama*
- *karah prasad* and usually *langar*

Punjabi celebrations such as roka, mehendi, jaggo, bhangra or reception are cultural additions. The Sikh Rehat Maryada governs religious form; display of caste or dowry conflicts with Sikh egalitarian teaching, though social practice does not always match ideals.

## 6. Zoroastrian/Parsi traditions

### Core idea

Parsi marriage in India is called **lagan**. It joins Zoroastrian religious blessing with Gujarati-Parsi cultural forms. Indian law separately regulates Parsi marriage and divorce.

### Representative sequence

**Before:** *rupiya peravanu* (engagement/gift), *madhavsaro* planting ritual, *adarna*, purification baths and family celebrations; actual use varies.

**Ceremony:** the couple sits facing each other with a cloth screen. A cord is wound around them in **achumichu/ara antar**-type protective and uniting observances. Rice may be thrown competitively when the screen falls. Priests recite prayers; the **ashirvad** blesses the union. The couple may circle or be ritually linked seven times depending on described community form. Witnesses sign, followed by feast.

### Caution

Eligibility for a religious Parsi ceremony and recognition within community institutions are debated and can be stricter than civil marriage law. Do not generalise from one priestly or family practice.

## 7. Buddhist traditions

### Core idea

Marriage is generally a civil/social institution rather than a sacrament established by the Buddha. Monastics may bless a couple but usually do not “create” the marriage in the same theological way as a priestly sacrament.

### Common forms

- civil/customary formation of marriage
- taking refuge in Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha
- recitation of precepts or protective texts
- offering to monks/temple
- blessings, water, thread, scarves or lamps according to country
- family/community feast

Sri Lankan, Thai, Tibetan, Japanese and Ambedkarite Buddhist weddings differ substantially. Indian Navayana weddings may foreground the Three Refuges, Five Precepts, equality and rejection of caste.

## 8. Jain traditions

### Core idea

Jain marriage is a household and community institution guided by values such as non-violence, restraint and ethical partnership; monks and nuns do not marry and generally do not function as wedding priests.

### Representative Indian sequence

Engagement, temple/family worship, turmeric or regional celebrations, garlands, hand-joining, fire/customary rites, rounds and blessings may appear. Gujarati, Rajasthani, Digambara and Shvetambara communities differ. Much ceremonial form resembles neighbouring Hindu regional practice, while Jain prayers, dietary ethics and community identity give it a distinct frame.

---

## 9. Bahá’í marriage

Bahá’í marriage requires the free consent of the couple and, under Bahá’í law, consent of living parents; the couple recites the prescribed marriage verse before witnesses. Celebrations can adopt local culture provided they do not contradict Bahá’í principles. Simplicity, unity and equality are emphasised.

## 10. Indigenous and tribal traditions

There is no single “tribal religion” or wedding. Systems may include clan exogamy, bridewealth, bride-service, elopement recognised after settlement, community feasts, ancestor/local-deity rites, dance, rice beer, ceremonial capture symbolism, or matrilineal inheritance. Every claim must name the people—Santhal, Khasi, Garo, Gond, Naga group, Bhil, Toda, etc.—and historical period.

**Bridewealth** (transfer connected to the bride’s kin) is not automatically “buying a bride”; anthropologists analyse it as creating rights and obligations between groups. It can nevertheless become coercive, just as dowry can.

---

## 11. Civil/secular marriage

Civil marriage is formed through legal declaration, notice/registration procedures and witnesses rather than religious rites. Couples may add personal vows, rings, music or a reception. In India, the **Special Marriage Act, 1954** provides a civil form for eligible couples irrespective of religion, subject to its statutory process.

---

## Comparative synthesis

- **Sacramental/sacred-household emphasis:** many Hindu, Catholic and Orthodox understandings.
- **Contract strongly foregrounded:** Islamic *nikah* and Jewish *ketubah/kiddushin*, though both also have profound sacred meaning.
- **Scripture as ceremonial centre:** Sikh *Anand Karaj*.
- **Blessing of a socially/civilly formed union:** common in many Buddhist contexts.
- **State declaration:** civil marriage.

These labels are analytical aids, not boxes. Most traditions combine consent, obligation, sacred meaning, family and public witness in different proportions.
