# Marriage Around the World

Back to [[Marriage - Master Case Study]]. Religious cores: [[Religion-wise Marriage Rituals]].

> [!note]
> Countries contain many ethnicities and religions. These are representative ceremonial patterns, never a claim that every citizen marries this way.

## East Asia

### China

Classical ideals included family negotiation, matching, betrothal gifts, choosing an auspicious date, procession and bride’s transfer to the groom’s family. A tea ceremony honours parents/elders and incorporates the spouse into kinship. Modern China requires civil registration for legal marriage; couples may add red dress, door games, banquet, Western gown and photography. Red connotes joy and good fortune.

### Japan

A Shinto-style wedding may include purification, offerings, **san-san-kudo**—three exchanges of sake from three cups—vows and shrine blessing. Modern couples may instead hold Christian-style chapel ceremonies, Buddhist rites or civil registration. The popularity of chapel aesthetics illustrates global cultural borrowing without necessarily indicating Christian faith.

### Korea

Contemporary weddings often use a hall ceremony with vows, music and banquet. A smaller **paebaek** may follow: the couple bows to elders, offers dates/chestnuts and receives blessings symbolising descendants and family incorporation. Historical Confucian ritual placed strong emphasis on families and patrilineal continuity.

## Southeast Asia

### Indonesia

Legal and religious requirements vary by recognised religion, while Javanese, Sundanese, Balinese, Minangkabau and other *adat* traditions shape clothing and procession. Muslim ceremonies centre on *akad nikah*; Javanese customs may include symbolic meeting and parental rites. Minangkabau society offers an important matrilineal comparison.

### Thailand

Buddhist monks may chant and bless the couple, sacred thread may connect participants, and elders pour lustral water over joined hands. The marriage’s legal formation is civil; Buddhist blessing and local spirit/Brahmanical customs can coexist.

### Philippines

Catholic/Christian weddings are prominent: vows, rings, Mass/service and sponsors. Filipino additions may include veil, cord and coins (*arras*), symbolising unity and stewardship. Muslim Filipino and indigenous communities have other forms.

## Middle East and North Africa

Muslim marriages centre on contract, consent, *mahr* and witnesses, with region-specific henna, processions and feasts. In parts of the Arab world the celebratory **zaffa** procession publicly escorts or announces the couple. Christian Arab and Jewish communities use their own religious cores with shared regional music, food and dress.

### Iran

Persian weddings commonly feature the **sofreh-ye aghd**, a ceremonial spread containing mirror, candles, bread, herbs/spices, sweets and symbolic objects. The marriage contract and consent are central; sugar may be ground above the couple. Contemporary celebrations combine ancient Persian symbols, Islamic law and modern style.

## Africa

Africa cannot be represented by one wedding. Kinship and marriage exchanges vary enormously.

### Ghana and Nigeria

Many families hold both customary/traditional marriage and church, mosque or civil ceremonies. Introductions and gift lists publicly recognise relations between families. Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa, Akan and numerous other peoples use different clothing, prostration/bowing, kola nuts, bride identification, prayers and feasts.

### Kenya

Customary negotiations, bridewealth and family recognition may coexist with Christian, Muslim or civil ceremony. Kikuyu, Maasai, Luo and other communities differ; modern urban couples often combine multiple forms.

### South Africa

Civil, customary, Christian, Muslim, Hindu and other marriages coexist. **Lobola** negotiations can connect families and recognise obligations; they should not be reduced to a purchase. Zulu, Xhosa and other ceremonies may include distinctive dress, dance, exchange and ancestral acknowledgement.

## Europe

### Britain and Ireland

Church or civil ceremony commonly includes entrance, declarations, vows, rings, signatures and reception. “Something old, new, borrowed and blue,” white dress, speeches, cake and first dance are cultural conventions rather than universal Christian requirements. Handfasting has been revived in some Celtic/neopagan and secular ceremonies.

### Greece and Eastern Europe

Eastern Orthodox ceremonies commonly include betrothal/rings, crowning with *stefana*, a shared cup and ceremonial procession. Civil requirements vary by country. Folk music, bread, icon blessings and extended dancing surround the liturgy.

### France

Legal marriage is performed civilly by the mayor/authorised official; a religious celebration may follow but does not replace the civil act. The town-hall ceremony illustrates a strong separation between state validity and optional religious blessing.

## The Americas

### United States and Canada

Legal rules are jurisdictional. Ceremonies can be Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu, Indigenous, civil or personally designed. A common secular/Christian-style sequence is procession, readings, vows, rings, pronouncement, kiss and reception. Interfaith and same-sex weddings have expanded personalised and blended forms.

### Mexico and Latin America

Catholic nuptial Mass and civil marriage often coexist. Regional customs may include *arras* coins, *lazo* cord, godparents/sponsors, mariachi or community dance. Indigenous and Afro-descendant practices create additional diversity.

## Oceania and the Pacific

Australia and New Zealand combine civil/Christian and immigrant traditions. Māori weddings may include *karanga*, *haka/waiata*, blessings in *te reo Māori* and exchange involving families/whānau, depending on the couple. Pacific Island weddings often place strong emphasis on church, extended kin, textiles/mats, feast and dance.

## What international comparison teaches

Across societies, five recurrent processes appear:

1. **Consent or family authorisation**
2. **Transfer/exchange of obligations or gifts**
3. **Public witness and announcement**
4. **Symbolic transformation of status**
5. **Feast and incorporation into kin/community**

Globalisation does not simply erase tradition. It creates **hybridity**: civil registration + ancestral rite + global reception style can all occur in one wedding.
