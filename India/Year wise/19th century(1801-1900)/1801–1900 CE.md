# India During 1801–1900 CE — The 19th Century

The 19th century was the period when India transformed from a collection of powerful regional kingdoms into a territory dominated by British colonial rule, while Indian nationalism gradually emerged.

To understand this century, divide it into four major phases.

1801–1818 (British expansion)
Maratha power declines; the East India Company establishes political supremacy.

1818–1857 (Company rule)
British annexations, administrative reforms, economic changes and growing resistance.

1857–1858 (Turning point)
Revolt of 1857 and transfer of power from the Company to the British Crown.

1858–1900 (Crown rule)
British Raj, social reform, economic criticism and organised Indian nationalism.

## 1. What was India's situation in 1801 CE?

Imagine India in 1801. The Mughal emperor still sat in Delhi, but his empire had lost most of its political and military power.

There was no single Indian government controlling the entire subcontinent.

Historical map reference: India in the early 19th century, when British-controlled areas existed alongside major Indian kingdoms.

| Region                                  | Who held power around 1801?                                                |
| --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Delhi                                   | Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II, with limited effective authority              |
| Maharashtra and Central India           | Maratha Confederacy                                                        |
| Punjab                                  | Sikh misls; Ranjit Singh was consolidating power                           |
| Mysore                                  | Wodeyar dynasty under British influence after Tipu Sultan's defeat in 1799 |
| Hyderabad                               | Nizam, under a British subsidiary alliance                                 |
| Bengal, Bihar and much of eastern India | East India Company                                                         |
| Awadh                                   | Nawab under increasing British control                                     |
| Rajasthan                               | Various Rajput kingdoms                                                    |
| Gujarat                                 | Gaekwads, other regional powers, and expanding British influence           |

Important: The British had been in India for roughly two centuries as traders, but by 1801 they were already a major territorial and military power.

Their key earlier victories included:

- 1757: Battle of Plassey.
    
- 1764: Battle of Buxar.
    
- 1765: Company received Diwani rights in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.
    
- 1799: Tipu Sultan died during the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War.

Thus, the British were no longer simply merchants.

## 2. 1801–1818: How did the British become dominant?

The largest obstacle to East India Company expansion was the Maratha Confederacy.

The Marathas were not a completely unified empire. Powerful chiefs such as the Scindias, Holkars, Bhonsles and Gaekwads controlled different territories.

Major turning points

| --------- | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1802      | Treaty of Bassein        | Peshwa Baji Rao II accepted a British subsidiary alliance        |
| 1803–1805 | Second Anglo-Maratha War | British defeated major Maratha forces and occupied Delhi in 1803 |

In 1818, Peshwa Baji Rao II was defeated and pensioned off.

This was a decisive political shift. The British East India Company became the paramount power across much of India, although several princely states retained their rulers.

## 3. 1818–1857: The East India Company consolidates control

During these decades the British expanded their territory through wars, treaties and annexations.

Some important developments:

- 1824–1826: First Anglo-Burmese War.
    
- 1829: Sati was abolished under Governor-General Lord William Bentinck, supported by Indian reform efforts.
    
- 1833: Charter Act ended the Company's remaining commercial activities and strengthened central administration.
    
- 1835: Macaulay's Minute promoted English-language education.
    
- 1843: Sindh annexed.
    
- 1845–1846: First Anglo-Sikh War.
    
- 1848–1849: Second Anglo-Sikh War; Punjab annexed.
    
- 1856: Awadh annexed on grounds of alleged misgovernment.
    

Governor-General Lord Dalhousie (1848–1856) also used the Doctrine of Lapse, under which certain princely states could be annexed if the ruler died without a recognised natural male heir.

India was simultaneously experiencing major social and technological changes, including campaigns against harmful social practices, new educational institutions, telegraphs, postal reforms and the first passenger railway from Bombay to Thane in 1853.

However, British land revenue demands, annexation policies, and military grievances generated considerable discontent.

## 4. 1857–1858: The great turning point

In 1857, an uprising by Indian sepoys grew into a major rebellion across parts of northern and central India.

It began at Meerut on 10 May 1857, after escalating grievances, including controversy surrounding cartridges believed to contain cow and pig fat.

Important figures included:

| Leader             | Major centre  |
| ------------------ | ------------- |
| Bahadur Shah Zafar | Delhi         |
| Rani Lakshmibai    | Jhansi        |
| Nana Sahib         | Kanpur        |
| Begum Hazrat Mahal | Lucknow       |
| Kunwar Singh       | Bihar         |
| Tantia Tope        | Central India |

The rebellion was eventually suppressed. It was widespread but not supported uniformly throughout India.

In 1858, the Government of India Act transferred administration from the East India Company to the British Crown.

- Queen Victoria became the sovereign authority.
    
- Lord Canning became the first Viceroy of India.
    
- The Company ceased to govern India.
    
- The Mughal dynasty came to an end.

Queen Victoria's proclamation was issued on 1 November 1858.

This marked the beginning of direct Crown rule, commonly called the British Raj.

## 5. 1858–1900: British Raj and the rise of Indian nationalism

After 1858, India came under the direct authority of the British Crown, but Indian princely states continued to exist under British paramountcy.

This period saw an important contradiction: India became more connected through railways, education, newspapers and political institutions, while economic inequality, colonial restrictions and demands for self-government also grew.

### B. Economic situation

India remained largely agricultural, and most people lived in villages.

The British expanded transport, communications, andcommercial infrastructure. But land revenue demands, peasant indebtedness, une
al economic relations and famines created serious hardship.

Major famines

The Great Famine of 1876–1878 devastated several regions, particularly southern India. Severe famine also occurred in 1896–1897 and 1899–1900.

Colonial relief policies and their effectiveness became subjects of major criticism.

Indian thinkers such as Dadabhai Naoroji developed the **Drain of Wealth Theory**, arguing that colonial economic arrangements transferred substantial wealth from India to Britain.

### C. Social and religious reform movements

Indian society also experienced reform movements addressing caste discrimination, women's education, social customs and religious practices.

These reforms contributed to new forms of public debate and social awareness.

### D. The birth of organised Indian nationalism

In December 1885, the Indian National Congress held its first session in Bombay.

- A. O. Hume helped establish the organisation.
    
- W. C. Bonnerjee was its first president.
    
- It began with 72 delegates.
    
- Early leaders included Dadabhai Naoroji, Pherozeshah Mehta and Surendranath Banerjee.

At first, Congress leaders generally demanded administrative reform, greater Indian representation, and civil rights, rather than immediate complete independence.

By 1900, the foundations of a wider national movement had been established.

## Final understanding

The story of 19th-century India can be remembered through one sequence:

Regional kingdoms and declining Mughal authority

East India Company expansion

British territorial supremacy by 1818

Revolt of 1857

British Crown rule from 1858

Indian National Congress in 1885

Growing nationalism by 1900

For UPSC and GPSC: Remember that 1801–1900 is not only a story of British conquest. It is also the period of administrative transformation, social reform, economic change, peasant and tribal resistance, the emergence of modern political organisations, and the beginnings of the organised freedom struggle.

The next century, 1901–1947, would witness mass nationalist movements, Gandhian politics, revolutionary activities, constitutional negotiations, and ultimately Indian independence.
