1701–1707: The final years of Aurangzeb
Mughal authority extended over much of the subcontinent, but prolonged Deccan warfare, heavy expenditure and administrative tensions had weakened imperial stability.

Aurangzeb died in 1707, creating a major turning point.
1707–1740: Emergence of regional powers
The Marathas expanded under the Peshwas, while Bengal, Awadh and Hyderabad developed increasingly autonomous governments.
Delhi was no longer able to control the entire Mughal imperial system effectively.

1739–1761: Invasions and military struggles
Nadir Shah invaded Delhi in 1739. Ahmad Shah Abdali's invasions and the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761 further reshaped northern India's political balance.

1757–1765: The British breakthrough in Bengal
The victories at Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764), followed by the Diwani grant (1765), allowed the East India Company to acquire substantial political and revenue authority.

1765–1800: Expansion of Company power
The Company fought Indian powers such as Mysore and the Marathas, developed new administrative systems and expanded its influence through warfare and diplomacy.
Tipu Sultan died in 1799 during the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War.

The 18th century was not simply the collapse of the Mughals. It involved five connected developments.

Political transformation

India moved from a Mughal-centred imperial order to several competing regional states. The Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, Mysore, Hyderabad, Awadh and Bengal became increasingly important.

Military transformation

Indian states and European companies competed through warfare, military organization, diplomacy and alliances. Battles such as Plassey, Buxar and Panipat had long-lasting consequences.

Economic transformation

The East India Company increasingly combined trade, revenue collection and territorial control. Bengal's revenues became important to financing its growing political and military power.

Administrative transformation

British parliamentary legislation and Company land-revenue policies established institutions that shaped colonial administration in subsequent centuries.

Social and cultural developments

Regional courts, cities, commercial networks, intellectual traditions and artistic centres continued to develop. The century was a period of regional reorganization, not uniform decline.
