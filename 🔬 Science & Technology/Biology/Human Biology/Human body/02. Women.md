Women "all over the world are evaluated and oppressed by their appearances",including their **ages**, **skin tones**, or **sizes**.

Society often judges women by their **age, skin tone, body size, facial features and overall appearance** because physical appearance has historically been connected to social expectations about beauty, marriage, status and femininity.

But an important question is: **Why does society give so much importance to appearance rather than intelligence, character, abilities or achievements?**

There are several sociological and psychological reasons.

**1. First impressions and human psychology**

Human beings naturally notice visible characteristics before they learn about someone's personality.

For example, when we meet a stranger, we may immediately notice their face, clothing, age or body size. Understanding their intelligence, honesty or kindness takes much longer.

Psychologists call one related tendency the **halo effect**: people sometimes assume that an attractive person also possesses other positive qualities, even without evidence.

However, being attractive does not necessarily mean someone is intelligent, kind or capable.

**2. Patriarchy and traditional gender roles**

Historically, many patriarchal societies evaluated women according to expectations about marriage, motherhood, youth and physical beauty.

Men were more often evaluated through occupations, income, authority and achievements, while women faced stronger expectations about appearance and domestic roles.

For example, people may praise a man for his professional success but focus on a woman's appearance even when she has achieved something equally significant.

This is an example of **gender inequality** because the standards used to evaluate people are different.

**3. Beauty standards are socially constructed**

What society considers beautiful is not entirely natural or universal. Beauty standards change across cultures and historical periods.

Consider these examples:

- **Skin tone:** Colourism can lead people to favour lighter skin, particularly in societies influenced by historical hierarchies and discriminatory beauty standards.
    
- **Body size:** Some societies historically associated larger bodies with prosperity, while contemporary media often promotes thinness.
    
- **Age:** Women may experience greater pressure to appear youthful because ageing is sometimes unfairly associated with declining attractiveness or social value.

These differences demonstrate that **beauty standards are strongly influenced by culture, history and social power**.

**4. Media and the beauty industry**

Advertising, cinema, social media and the beauty industry can reinforce narrow standards of attractiveness.

For example, social media frequently presents edited photographs, beauty filters and carefully selected images.

When people repeatedly encounter these images, they may begin treating unrealistic appearances as normal.

This encourages **social comparison**, where individuals evaluate their own appearance against others.

However, media can also challenge stereotypes by representing diverse ages, skin tones and body types.

**5. Objectification of women**

In sociology, _objectification_ refers to treating a person primarily as an object to be looked at or evaluated rather than recognising them as a complete human being.

For example, imagine two women applying for the same job. Both have similar qualifications, but an employer gives preference to one because she is considered more physically attractive.

This is an example of appearance-based discrimination.

When women are repeatedly evaluated primarily by their physical characteristics, their skills, opinions and achievements may receive less attention.

**6. Socialisation — society teaches these expectations**

From childhood, girls and boys may receive different messages about what makes them valuable.

A girl might frequently hear:

> "You look so beautiful!"

While a boy might hear:

> "You are very intelligent!" or "You are very strong!"

Neither compliment is necessarily harmful. The problem develops when appearance becomes the primary measure of a girl's worth.

Over time, these repeated messages can influence how people evaluate themselves and others.

 **A sociological perspective**
Three sociological theories can help explain this phenomenon.

|Theory|Explanation|
|---|---|
|**Feminist theory**|Examines how patriarchal power structures and gender expectations contribute to the greater scrutiny of women's appearance.|
|**Symbolic interactionism**|Explains how everyday interactions, compliments, criticism and social labels shape people's understanding of beauty and identity.|
|**Social constructionism**|Examines how societies create, maintain and change standards of beauty through culture, institutions and social practices.|

## **My central conclusion**

Appearance matters in society partly because **it is immediately visible, while a person's character, intelligence and abilities require time and interaction to understand**.

But this psychological tendency does not fully explain why women experience especially strong appearance-related pressure. Historical gender hierarchies, cultural expectations, media and economic incentives help explain that difference.

There is also an important distinction between **noticing someone's appearance** and **judging their human worth by it**. The first is a common human experience; the second can become discrimination.

For your sociology studies, you could express the central argument this way:

> Society often evaluates women through their physical appearance not simply because beauty attracts attention, but because historical gender roles, cultural expectations and social institutions have made appearance an important measure of femininity. Such judgments can reinforce inequality by reducing women's identities to their age, skin tone or body size while overlooking their intelligence, abilities and individuality.
