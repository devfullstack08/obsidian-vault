## 1. Introduction

The **Indian Councils Act of 1909**, also known as the **Morley–Minto Reforms**, was passed by the British Parliament on **25 May 1909** to increase Indian participation in legislative councils while retaining British control over administration.

- **John Morley** — Secretary of State for India.
- **Lord Minto II** — Viceroy of India (1905–1910).
- **Main objective:** Expand legislative representation and provide limited political concessions without establishing responsible government.

> **Key point:** The Act expanded Indian participation but did not transfer real executive power to Indians.

## 2. Background — Why was the Act introduced?

The **Indian Councils Act of 1892** had increased legislative council membership and permitted budget discussions and questions. However, Indian representatives had very limited powers.

### Major developments

1. **1892 Act's limitations:** Members could discuss the budget but could not move budget resolutions or ask supplementary questions.
2. **Growth of nationalism:** The Indian National Congress demanded greater representation and political rights.
3. **Partition of Bengal (1905):** Triggered the Swadeshi and Boycott movements, intensifying nationalist demands.
4. **Simla Deputation (1906):** Muslim leaders led by Aga Khan met Lord Minto and demanded separate political representation.
5. **All-India Muslim League (1906):** Founded at Dacca, reflecting the growth of organized Muslim political representation.
**Result:** The British government introduced the Morley–Minto Reforms in 1909.

## 3. Main Provisions

### A. Expansion of Legislative Councils

- The size of the **Central and Provincial Legislative Councils** was substantially increased.
- The Central Legislative Council's membership increased **from 16 to 60** under the new arrangements.
- Provincial council membership also increased, but the numbers differed between provinces.
**Remember:** Both the 1892 and 1909 Acts increased council membership, but the expansion in 1909 was considerably larger.

### B. Official and Non-official Majority

| Legislative Council                 | Majority after 1909              |
| ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| **Central Legislative Council**     | Official majority retained       |
| **Provincial Legislative Councils** | Non-official majority introduced |

- **Official members:** Government officials.
- **Non-official members:** Members who were not government officials; they could be nominated or elected.
**Important:** A non-official majority did not mean an elected Indian majority or control over the executive. British authorities retained decisive administrative powers.

### C. Increased Powers of Legislative Councils

Members received additional opportunities to examine and criticize government policies.

- Could ask **supplementary questions** following an initial question.
- Could discuss the annual budget and **move budget resolutions**.
- Could move resolutions on matters of public interest, subject to restrictions.
- Could participate more extensively in legislative debates.
**Limitation:** Council resolutions were generally recommendatory rather than binding on the executive. Members did not gain full control over public expenditure.

### D. Expansion of the Electoral System

The Act expressly recognized elected members within the legislative councils and provided for a wider, though restricted, electoral system.

Representation included constituencies or bodies associated with:
- Municipalities and local boards.
- Universities.
- Chambers of commerce.
- Landholders and other specified interests.
- Muslim electorates.
Elections were largely indirect, with restricted eligibility.

**Important distinction:**
- **1892:** Limited indirect election through recommendations, without formally using the term election in the Act.
- **1909:** Election explicitly recognized and expanded, but no universal adult franchise.
### E. Separate Electorates for Muslims — Most Important Provision

The Act introduced **separate electorates for Muslims**, establishing communal representation in the electoral system.

**Meaning:** In constituencies reserved for Muslim representation, eligible Muslim voters elected Muslim representatives.

This did **not** mean every Muslim could vote. Voting eligibility was limited by the electoral rules.

**Example:**

- General electorate → elected representatives under its applicable rules. 
- Separate Muslim electorate → Muslim voters elected Muslim representatives.

**Significance:**

- Formally recognized Muslims as a separate political electoral category.    
- Institutionalized religious distinctions in legislative representation.
- Became a significant feature of subsequent constitutional and communal politics.

Lord Minto is sometimes referred to in examination material as the **Father of Communal Electorates**.

### F. Indians in Executive Councils

The reforms permitted greater Indian association with executive councils.

**Satyendra Prasanna Sinha (S. P. Sinha)** became the **first Indian member of the Viceroy's Executive Council in 1909**, serving as the **Law Member**.

**UPSC distinction:**

- **Legislative Council:** Discussed legislation, questions and government policies.
- **Executive Council:** Participated directly in executive administration.
Therefore, S. P. Sinha's appointment was important because he entered the higher executive machinery.

## 4. Indian Councils Act of 1892 vs 1909

|Feature|Act of 1892|Act of 1909|
|---|---|---|
|Central council size|10–16 additional members permitted|Enlarged to 60 members|
|Provincial councils|Expanded|Further expanded|
|Central official majority|Retained|Retained|
|Provincial non-official majority|Not introduced|Introduced|
|Budget|Discussion allowed|Resolutions permitted|
|Questions|Questions allowed|Supplementary questions allowed|
|Election|Limited indirect principle|Explicitly recognized and expanded|
|Separate Muslim electorates|No|**Introduced**|
|Indians in Viceroy's Executive Council|No such reform|**S. P. Sinha appointed**|
|Responsible government|Not introduced|Not introduced|

**Note:** The figures for 1892 and 1909 are not identical counting categories: the 1892 figure describes additional members, while the 1909 figure describes the expanded Central Legislative Council's membership.

## 5. Importance of the Act

1. **Expanded representation:** More Indians could participate in legislative councils.
2. **Strengthened legislative discussion:** Members could question government policies and move resolutions.
3. **Extended electoral institutions:** Formally expanded elected representation.
4. **Introduced communal electorates:** Separate Muslim electorates became part of British India's constitutional arrangements.
5. **Indian participation in executive administration:** S. P. Sinha entered the Viceroy's Executive Council.

## 6. Criticism and Limitations

### 1. No Responsible Government

The executive was not accountable to the elected members of the legislative councils. Ultimate power remained with the British colonial administration.

### 2. Restricted Franchise

Only limited categories of voters could participate in elections. The majority of Indians remained excluded.

### 3. Communal Representation

Separate Muslim electorates institutionalized political representation based on religion.

- The British government and supporters presented separate representation as a safeguard for Muslim political interests.
- Nationalist critics argued that it encouraged communal divisions and weakened the possibility of common political representation.
### 4. Limited Legislative Powers

The councils could debate and recommend measures but could not effectively control the colonial executive.

## 7. Historical Significance — UPSC Mains

The Indian Councils Act of 1909 represented a **limited constitutional advance without the introduction of responsible government**.

It increased Indian representation, expanded debate and established more formal electoral arrangements. However, British officials retained executive control, and the legislative councils lacked decisive authority over administration and public expenditure.

The introduction of separate Muslim electorates became one of the most consequential and controversial features of colonial constitutional development.

**Conclusion:** The reforms widened participation while preserving British authority and institutionalizing communal electoral representation.

## 9. Quick Revision

**Morley–Minto Reforms (1909)**

- **Morley:** Secretary of State for India.
- **Minto:** Viceroy of India.
- **Council expansion:** Central Council enlarged to 60 members.
- **Majorities:** Official at the Centre; non-official in provinces.
- **New powers:** Supplementary questions, budget resolutions and public-interest resolutions.
- **Elections:** Explicitly recognized and expanded, but restricted.
- **Most important feature:** Separate electorates for Muslims.
- **Executive Council:** S. P. Sinha became its first Indian member.
- **Limitation:** No responsible government.
