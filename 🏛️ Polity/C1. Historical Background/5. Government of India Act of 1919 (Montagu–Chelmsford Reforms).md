## 1. Introduction

The **Government of India Act, 1919**, also known as the **Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms**, was passed by the British Parliament to introduce limited political reforms and gradually increase Indian participation in administration.

- **Edwin Montagu:** Secretary of State for India.
- **Lord Chelmsford:** Viceroy of India (1916–1921).
- **Passed:** 1919.
- **Implemented:** 1921.
- **Main objective:** Gradual development of responsible government in British India.
> **Key Point:** The Act introduced **dyarchy in provinces** and **bicameralism at the Centre**, but real executive power remained largely with British authorities.

## 2. Background — Why was the Act introduced?

The Indian Councils Act of 1909 had increased Indian representation but failed to establish responsible government. 

Meanwhile:
1. **Growth of nationalism:** Indians increasingly demanded self-government.
2. **First World War (1914–1918):** Indian contributions increased expectations of political reforms.
3. **Home Rule Movement (1916):** Bal Gangadhar Tilak and Annie Besant demanded greater self-government.
4. **Lucknow Pact (1916):** Congress and the Muslim League presented constitutional demands.
5. **Montagu Declaration (20 August 1917):** The British government announced the gradual development of self-governing institutions and responsible government in India.
6. **Montagu–Chelmsford Report (1918):** Recommended reforms that became the basis of the 1919 Act.

**Result:** The Government of India Act, 1919 was enacted to introduce limited constitutional reforms while maintaining British supremacy.

## 3. Main Provisions

### A. Division of Central and Provincial Subjects

The Act classified government functions into **Central** and **Provincial** subjects.

| Central Subjects       | Provincial Subjects   |
| ---------------------- | --------------------- |
| Defence                | Education             |
| Foreign affairs        | Public health         |
| Railways               | Agriculture           |
| Posts and telegraphs   | Local self-government |
| Currency               | Land revenue          |
| Major central taxation | Police                |

**Purpose:** To transfer certain administrative responsibilities to provinces while retaining important all-India subjects under central control.

**Important:** Provinces were not fully autonomous. The British central government retained substantial supervisory powers.

### B. Dyarchy in Provinces — Most Important Provision

**Dyarchy = Dual Government (Rule of Two).**

Provincial subjects were further divided into two categories:

|Reserved Subjects|Transferred Subjects|
|---|---|
|Police|Education|
|Finance|Public health|
|Land revenue|Agriculture|
|Irrigation|Local self-government|
|Administration of justice|Industries|
|Prisons|Local sanitation|

**Reserved Subjects**

- Administered by the **Governor and his Executive Council**.
- Executive councillors were generally appointed officials.
- They were **not responsible to the Provincial Legislative Council**.

**Transferred Subjects**

- Administered by ministers selected from the elected members of the provincial legislature and appointed by the Governor.    
- Ministers were expected to be responsible to the Provincial Legislative Council.
- The Governor retained powers to override ministerial advice and remove ministers.
#### Example — Why was Dyarchy problematic?

Suppose an Indian minister wants to build **100 new schools**.

- **Education** is a transferred subject, so the Indian minister can propose new schools.
- **Finance** is a reserved subject, controlled by the Governor's administration.
- The minister may lack the necessary funding or face restrictions.

**Result:** Indian ministers had responsibilities but insufficient financial authority.

> **UPSC:** Dyarchy created a mismatch between responsibility and power, contributing to administrative difficulties.

### C. Bicameral Legislature at the Centre

The Act introduced a **bicameral Central Legislature**, consisting of two Houses:

|House|Position|Membership|Term|
|---|---|---|---|
|Council of State|Upper House|60|5 years|
|Legislative Assembly|Lower House|145 initially (membership figures changed)|3 years|

The Central Legislature could debate legislation and financial matters, but the Governor-General possessed extensive powers, including control over important legislative and budgetary decisions.

**Important:** Bicameralism was introduced at the **Centre**, while dyarchy was introduced in the **provinces**.

### D. Introduction of Direct Elections

- Direct elections were introduced into the legislative system.
- Voting rights were restricted by qualifications such as property, taxation and other eligibility requirements.
- Universal adult franchise was **not introduced**.
- Provincial legislative councils were expanded, with at least 70% of members to be elected.

**Difference from 1909:**

- **1909:** Electoral representation expanded, primarily through indirect arrangements.
- **1919:** Direct elections became a significant part of legislative representation.
### E. Expansion of Separate Electorates

The system of separate electorates, introduced for Muslims in **1909**, was extended to additional communities:

- Sikhs
- Indian Christians
- Anglo-Indians
- Europeans

**Meaning:** Specified communities received separate electoral representation under the colonial electoral arrangements.

**Criticism:** The system institutionalized political representation on communal lines.

### F. Separation of Provincial and Central Budgets

- Provincial budgets were separated from the central budget.
- Provincial legislatures received greater responsibility for considering their budgets.
- Provinces were allocated financial resources for provincial subjects.

**Limitation:** Governors retained special financial powers, so provincial financial independence remained restricted.

### G. Public Service Commission

The Act provided for the establishment of a **Public Service Commission** to assist with recruitment to public services.

- **1926:** The first Public Service Commission in India was established.
- **First Chairman:** Sir Ross Barker.
**UPSC Trap:** The Act provided for the commission in **1919**, but the commission was established in **1926**.

### H. High Commissioner for India

The Act provided for a **High Commissioner for India in London**.

The office took over certain commercial and administrative functions that had previously been performed by the Secretary of State for India.

### I. Indians in the Viceroy's Executive Council

The Act provided for **three of the six members** of the Viceroy's Executive Council, excluding the Commander-in-Chief, to be Indians.

This increased Indian participation at the highest level of colonial executive administration, although ultimate authority remained with British officials.

### J. Statutory Commission

The Act provided for a statutory commission to review constitutional progress after ten years.

- **1927:** The British government appointed the **Simon Commission**, earlier than the anticipated ten-year review.
- **Chairman:** Sir John Simon.
- **All seven members were British.**
- **1928:** The Commission arrived in India and faced widespread opposition because it had no Indian members.
## 4. Importance of the Act

1. **Dyarchy:** Introduced limited responsible administration in transferred provincial subjects.
2. **Bicameralism:** Established a two-house Central Legislature.
3. **Direct elections:** Increased electoral participation, though voting remained restricted.
4. **Decentralization:** Separated Central and Provincial subjects and finances.
5. **Indian representation:** Increased Indians' participation in the legislative and executive systems.
6. **Public services:** Provided for a Public Service Commission.
7. **Constitutional development:** Marked an important stage in British India's gradual constitutional reforms.

## 5. Limitations and Criticism

### 1. Dyarchy was administratively difficult

Transferred subjects depended on finances and administrative cooperation controlled through reserved departments.

### 2. Governors retained overriding powers

Indian ministers could be overruled or dismissed by the Governor.

### 3. No responsible government at the Centre

The Central Executive was not accountable to the elected Central Legislature.

### 4. Limited voting rights

Most Indians were excluded from the electorate.

### 5. Separate electorates continued

Communal representation was further expanded.

### 6. British control remained dominant

Defence, foreign affairs, police, major revenue matters and important executive powers remained in British hands.

## 7. Historical Significance — UPSC Mains

The Government of India Act of 1919 represented an important stage in the gradual introduction of representative institutions in British India.

Its most significant innovation was **provincial dyarchy**, under which transferred subjects were administered by Indian ministers who were expected to be responsible to provincial legislatures. It also established a bicameral Central Legislature, expanded direct elections and separated central and provincial finances.

However, the reforms did not establish complete responsible government. Governors retained considerable authority, financial control remained restricted, and the central executive was not accountable to the elected legislature.

The system of dyarchy revealed the difficulties of combining limited Indian ministerial responsibility with continued British executive supremacy.

**Conclusion:** The Act expanded constitutional participation but failed to satisfy demands for genuine self-government, leading to further constitutional debates and reforms.

### Important Examination Traps

- **Dyarchy at the Centre in 1919 — Incorrect.** It was introduced in provinces.
    
- **Bicameralism in all provinces in 1919 — Incorrect.** It was introduced at the Centre.
    
- **1919 established the first Public Service Commission — Incorrect.** It provided for it; establishment followed in 1926.
    
- **Universal adult franchise introduced in 1919 — Incorrect.**
    
- **Simon Commission appointed in 1928 — Incorrect.** Appointed in 1927; arrived in India in 1928.
    
- **1935 successfully introduced central dyarchy — Incorrect.** It proposed it, but the relevant federal provisions were not implemented.
    

## 9. Quick Revision

**Government of India Act, 1919**

- **Popular name:** Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms.
- **Main change:** Dyarchy introduced in provinces.
- **Reserved subjects:** Police, finance, land revenue, justice, irrigation.
- **Transferred subjects:** Education, health, agriculture, local government, industries.
- **At Centre:** Bicameral legislature — Council of State and Legislative Assembly.
- **Elections:** Direct elections with restricted franchise.
- **Separate electorates:** Expanded beyond Muslims.
- **Provincial budgets:** Separated from central budget.
- **Public Service Commission:** Provided in 1919; established 1926.
- **Simon Commission:** Appointed 1927 for constitutional review.
- **Limitation:** Real executive control remained with British authorities.
