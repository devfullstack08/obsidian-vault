The Constitution of India was framed by the **Constituent Assembly of India**.

It was formed under the **Cabinet Mission Plan, 1946**.

British Acts from 1773 to 1947 are in [[1. The Company Rule (1773–1858)|Historical Background]]. Those Acts were made in London. Indians also wrote their own constitutional drafts. Those drafts are the missing half of the timeline.

## Swaraj Bill, 1895

The **Constitution of India Bill, 1895** is also called the **Swaraj Bill**.

It was written when Indian nationalism was rising, but the demand was still for self-government **inside** the British Empire, not for complete independence.

- It is the **first non-official attempt** by Indians to draft a constitution.
- It is written in legal form and has **110 articles**.
- It asks for rights that later appear in the Constitution: freedom of speech, right to property, protection of the home, and equality before the law.
- It also sketches a structure of government and a separation of powers.
- Annie Besant later connected the document with **Bal Gangadhar Tilak**, the leader of the call for Swaraj. The author’s name is not settled. Do not write in an exam that Tilak definitely drafted it.

It influenced the Commonwealth of India Bill (1925) and the Nehru Report (1928).

Exam point: some questions call 1895 the first *glimpse* of Indians framing their own constitution. The first clear demand for a **Constituent Assembly** is still **M. N. Roy in 1934**.

## Commonwealth of India Bill, 1925

[[Annie Besant]] and Indian constitutionalists, including [[Tej Bahadur Sapru]], prepared a fuller draft.

- It asked for **dominion status** at once, with some powers kept by the Viceroy for a time (defence and foreign affairs).
- It contained a bill of rights: personal liberty, freedom of conscience, freedom of speech, and equality of the sexes.
- George Lansbury introduced it in the House of Commons on **9 December 1925**.
- It did not become law.

## Nehru Report, 1928

This is the document behind the line you remembered: **self-government, minority rights, and a demand that fell on deaf ears.**

### Why it was written

In **November 1927** the British appointed the **Simon Commission** to review the 1919 Act. Every member was British. No Indian was included. Indians boycotted it (“Simon Go Back”).

The Secretary of State, **Lord Birkenhead**, challenged Indian leaders to produce a constitution they could agree on. The reply was the Nehru Report.

- The Madras session of Congress (December 1927) asked for a Swaraj constitution, drawn up with other parties.
- The **All Parties Conference** met in 1928.
- In May 1928, at Bombay, it appointed a committee with **Motilal Nehru as chairman**.
- Jawaharlal Nehru was the secretary of that committee. He is not the author of the Report. **Motilal** is.
- The committee included [[Tej Bahadur Sapru]], Sir Ali Imam, Subhas Chandra Bose, M. R. Jayakar, M. S. Aney, N. M. Joshi, Shuaib Qureshi, G. R. Pradhan and Sardar Mangal Singh.
- The Report was published in **August 1928**.

### What it asked for

**Self-government**

- **Dominion status**, on the model of Canada or Australia: India governing itself, while remaining in the British Empire.
- Responsible government at the Centre and in the provinces. The Governor-General and the Governors were to act on the advice of Indian ministers.
- A parliamentary system, with a bicameral central legislature.
- A Supreme Court.
- No official state religion.

**Minority rights**

- **Joint electorates.** It rejected separate electorates.
- Reservation of seats only where a community was in a **minority**:
  - Muslims in provinces where Muslims were a minority;
  - non-Muslims in the North-West Frontier Province.
- The reserved seats were to match that community’s share of the population, and those members could also contest other seats.
- **No reservation in Punjab and Bengal**, where Muslims were the majority.
- Full protection of the religious and cultural rights of minorities.
- A list of **fundamental rights** (nineteen in the standard exam account).

### Why the demand fell on deaf ears

Three things killed it. Only one of them was the British.

1. **The British did not accept it.** In October 1929 Lord Irwin said dominion status was the goal, but he would not promise that the coming Round Table Conference would actually frame a dominion constitution. On **23 December 1929**, Gandhi, Motilal Nehru, Vithalbhai Patel, Sapru and Jinnah met the Viceroy. He still would not give that assurance. The call for self-government on Indian terms was refused.
2. **The Muslim League rejected the minority formula.** In **1929** Jinnah issued his **Fourteen Points**. The points that clash with the Report are: separate electorates, one-third of the seats at the Centre for Muslims, and residuary powers with the provinces.
3. **Younger Congress leaders wanted more than dominion status.** Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose wanted complete independence.

The Calcutta Congress (1928), with Motilal Nehru as president, accepted the Report but gave Britain **one year**. If dominion status was not granted, Congress would ask for complete independence.

At the **Lahore session (December 1929)**, Jawaharlal Nehru presiding, Congress declared that the Nehru Report scheme had **lapsed**, and that Swaraj meant **Purna Swaraj** (complete independence). **26 January 1930** was observed as Independence Day. That date is why the Constitution later commenced on 26 January.

## Karachi Resolution, 1931

The Karachi session (March 1931) was presided over by **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**. The resolution on Fundamental Rights and the National Economic Programme was drafted mainly by **Jawaharlal Nehru**, with Gandhi’s approval.

It joined two ideas:

- civil liberties (speech, press, assembly, equality);
- economic promises (a living wage, free primary education, protection of workers, the right to form unions).

It is the bridge between the rights in the Nehru Report and the later split into **Fundamental Rights** and **Directive Principles**.

## Sapru Committee Report, 1945

A non-party committee chaired by **Sir Tej Bahadur Sapru** published its proposals in 1945.

Its exam point is the division of rights into:

- **justiciable** rights — a person can go to court;
- **non-justiciable** rights — the state should follow them, but a court cannot enforce them.

That division is the ancestor of Part III and Part IV of the Constitution.

## M. N. Roy

The idea of a Constituent Assembly for India was first put forward in **1934** by **[[M. N. Roy]]**.

The Swaraj Bill and the Nehru Report came earlier. They were Indian drafts. They were not yet a demand that an elected Constituent Assembly, free of British interference, should write the Constitution. That specific demand begins here.

He was:

- a revolutionary;
- a political thinker;
- a pioneer of the communist movement in India;
- later associated with Radical Humanism.

## Indian National Congress

In **1935**, the Indian National Congress officially demanded that the Constitution of India should be framed by a Constituent Assembly elected by Indians.

## Jawaharlal Nehru’s declaration

In **1938**, Jawaharlal Nehru declared on behalf of the Congress:

> The Constitution of free India must be framed, without outside interference, by a Constituent Assembly elected on the basis of adult franchise.

## British acceptance

The British government gradually accepted the idea.

### August Offer, 1940

For the first time, the British government recognised in principle that Indians should frame their own Constitution.

### Cripps Proposals, 1942

The Cripps Mission proposed the establishment of a constitution-making body after the Second World War.

The proposal was rejected because:

- India was not immediately granted independence;
- provinces were given the option to remain outside the proposed Union;
- princely states were not to be represented democratically;
- the British retained control over defence during the war.

### Cabinet Mission Plan, 1946

The Cabinet Mission Plan finally provided for the formation of the Constituent Assembly.

The Cabinet Mission consisted of three British Cabinet ministers:

1. **Lord Pethick-Lawrence** — Secretary of State for India
2. **Sir Stafford Cripps** — President of the Board of Trade
3. **A. V. Alexander** — First Lord of the Admiralty

It arrived in India in March 1946.

It aimed to:

- discuss the transfer of power;
- establish a constitutional framework;
- create a Constituent Assembly;
- preserve Indian unity;
- resolve differences between the Congress and Muslim League.

It proposed:

- a Union of India;
- a weak central government;
- provincial autonomy;
- grouping of provinces;
- a Constituent Assembly;
- an interim government.
