## 1. Introduction: How did the Marathas become a major power?

The Marathas rose in western India, especially the Maharashtra region, during the decline of the Deccan Sultanates and the long Mughal struggle for control of the Deccan.

Under Chhatrapati Shivaji Maharaj, a regional military power developed into a sovereign kingdom. After his death, the Marathas survived decades of Mughal warfare. During the eighteenth century, political power increasingly shifted from the Chhatrapati to the Peshwas and powerful regional Maratha chiefs.

The Marathas became one of the strongest powers in India after the weakening of the Mughal Empire, but internal rivalries and a series of wars with the British East India Company eventually ended Peshwa supremacy in 1818.

### Understand the two phases

| Feature             | Maratha State, 1674–1720                                      | Peshwa-led Maratha expansion and Confederacy, 1720–1818                         |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Main authority      | Chhatrapati                                                   | Increasingly Peshwa and regional chiefs                                         |
| Political centre    | Raigad, later Satara and rival centres                        | Pune, with Satara retaining symbolic legitimacy                                 |
| Principal objective | Establishment and survival of Swarajya                        | Expansion, revenue rights and regional supremacy                                |
| Nature              | Kingdom centered on the Chhatrapati                           | Increasingly decentralized political-military network                           |
| Key personalities   | Shivaji, Sambhaji, Rajaram, Tarabai, Shahu, Balaji Vishwanath | Baji Rao I, Nanasaheb, Madhavrao I, Mahadji Scindia, Nana Fadnavis, Baji Rao II |
| Major enemies       | Bijapur, Mughals, neighbouring powers                         | Nizam, Afghans, Mysore, British, regional rivals                                |
| Outcome             | Survival and consolidation despite Mughal pressure            | Expansion across India, later fragmentation and defeat                          |

Important historical qualification: The year 1720 is a convenient division for studying, because Baji Rao I became Peshwa. It was not the formal creation date of a completely separate confederacy. The decentralized Maratha confederacy emerged gradually during the eighteenth century, particularly as major Maratha families acquired extensive regional power.

# PART I — MARATHA STATE (1674–1720)

## 2. Background: The rise of Shivaji before 1674

Before studying Shivaji's coronation, understand why he was able to build an independent state.

### Political background

In the seventeenth century, the Deccan was contested by:

- Mughal Empire: Expanding southwards.
    
- Bijapur Sultanate: Controlled substantial parts of the western Deccan.
    
- Golconda Sultanate: Another important Deccan kingdom.
    
- Local Maratha families: Served different Deccan rulers as military chiefs, officials and landholders.
    

Shivaji's father, Shahaji Bhonsle, served the Ahmadnagar and later Bijapur political establishments.

Shivaji gradually established control over forts and surrounding territories in western Maharashtra.

- Rugged hills and narrow passes.
    
- Strong hill forts and difficult approaches.

### Challenge 1. Why was coronation necessary?

Although Shivaji had controlled territories before 1674, he needed formal recognition as an independent sovereign.

Actions:

- Underwent coronation ceremonies at Raigad.
    
- Claimed independent royal authority rather than the status of a subordinate to Bijapur or the Mughals.
    
- Strengthened the legitimacy of his administration and revenue collection.
    

Significance: Shivaji transformed his military conquests into an explicitly sovereign kingship.

### Challenge 2. Administration of the kingdom

Shivaji developed a council of eight important ministers called the Ashta Pradhan.

|Office|Main function|
|---|---|
|Peshwa|Prime minister; general administration|
|Amatya / Mazumdar|Finance and accounts|
|Sachiv|Royal correspondence and records|
|Mantri / Waqia-navis|Intelligence, court records and affairs|
|Senapati / Sari-naubat|Military command|
|Sumant / Dabir|Diplomatic affairs|
|Nyayadhish|Judicial matters|
|Panditrao|Religious affairs and charities|

UPSC distinction: Shivaji's Ashta Pradhan council should not be confused with a modern cabinet. The ministers operated under the king's authority, and not every office had equal responsibility or independent political power.

### Challenge 3. Revenue administration

Shivaji sought more systematic land-revenue assessment and supervision of local officials.

Two important terms are commonly associated with Maratha finances:

|Term|Meaning|Exam significance|
|---|---|---|
|Chauth|Generally one-fourth (25%) of the assessed revenue demanded as tribute from specified territories|Instrument of fiscal and political pressure|
|Sardeshmukhi|Additional claim generally amounting to 10% of assessed revenue|Claimed on the basis of superior hereditary fiscal rights|
|Swarajya|Core territories under direct Maratha authority|Different from areas primarily paying tribute|

Example: If the recognized revenue base was ₹100, a chauth demand calculated at 25% would be ₹25; an additional sardeshmukhi claim at 10% would be ₹10.

This is an illustration, not proof that both were collected uniformly from every village.

Historical nuance: Chauth was not invented entirely by Shivaji, and its collection varied according to political circumstances. Shivaji expanded its use, while later Peshwas greatly extended Maratha fiscal claims.

### Challenge 4. Military organization

Shivaji's military strength rested on a combination of:

- Fort-based defence: Control of strategic hill forts.
    
- Mobile cavalry: Rapid movement and attacks.
    
- Ganimi Kava: A style of irregular warfare involving surprise, mobility and exploitation of terrain.
    
- Intelligence gathering: Information about enemy movements.
    
- Infantry and artillery: Particularly important for forts and defensive operations.
    
- Naval development: Protection of coastal interests and resistance to maritime threats, including the Siddis and European powers.
    

Avoid saying that the Marathas fought only through guerrilla warfare. They also conducted conventional campaigns, sieges and field battles.

### Challenge 5. Deccan and southern expansion

During his later years, Shivaji campaigned in Karnataka and the eastern Deccan.

His southern expedition of 1676–1678 extended Maratha influence and secured important territories and forts, including Gingee.

This later became crucial when the Mughals pressed the Marathas in Maharashtra: Rajaram would use Gingee Fort as a major centre of resistance.

### Assessment of Shivaji

Shivaji's most important achievement was not simply capturing territory. He built institutions capable of preserving political power, collecting revenue and sustaining warfare against much larger enemies.

Sambhaji, Shivaji's eldest son, eventually succeeded him after a struggle for succession.

### Major challenges

1. Mughal pressure: Aurangzeb personally concentrated on the Deccan campaigns from 1681 onwards.

2. Multiple enemies: Sambhaji faced Mughal operations as well as conflict involving the Siddis and Portuguese.

3. Survival of Maratha territory: The empire lacked the resources of the Mughal state and had to defend widely dispersed forts.

He was executed on Aurangzeb's orders in 1689.

Why important? Aurangzeb expected the death of the ruler to weaken or destroy Maratha resistance, but the Marathas reorganized under Rajaram.

## 5. Rajaram (1689–1700): Survival through dispersed resistance

Rajaram, Shivaji's younger son, became the next Chhatrapati.

### The central problem

After Sambhaji's death, the Mughals intensified their military pressure and captured important Maratha forts, including Raigad in 1689.

### Rajaram's strategy

Rajaram escaped to Gingee (Jinji) Fort, in present-day Tamil Nadu, from where he maintained resistance.

Maratha commanders, notably Santaji Ghorpade and Dhanaji Jadhav, conducted mobile operations against Mughal forces.

The Mughals besieged Gingee for years and eventually captured it in 1698, but Rajaram had escaped before its fall.

Result: Mughal forces had to fight across a broad geographical area, stretching their resources and supply lines.

Rajaram died in 1700.

After Rajaram's death, his widow Tarabai became regent on behalf of her young son, Shivaji II.

### Her achievements

- Organized continued resistance to Aurangzeb.
    
- Coordinated important Maratha chiefs and military campaigns.
    
- Kept Maratha opposition active despite Mughal territorial advances.
    
- Maintained political legitimacy for Rajaram's line.
    

Aurangzeb died in 1707, after decades of expensive warfare in the Deccan.

Historical significance: Tarabai was not merely a symbolic queen. She was an active political and military leader during one of the most difficult periods of Maratha history.

## 7. Shahu (1707–1749): Succession struggle and the rise of the Peshwas

Although Shahu reigned beyond 1720, his early political decisions are essential to understanding the transition.

### Challenge 1. Rival claims to the throne

Shahu, the son of Sambhaji, had been held in Mughal captivity since childhood.

He was released in 1707, after Aurangzeb's death.

Problem: Tarabai upheld her son's claim to the Maratha throne, creating a political division.

Shahu defeated Tarabai's forces at the Battle of Khed (1707) and established his principal seat at Satara. He was crowned in 1708.

A separate Maratha royal line gradually emerged at Kolhapur.

### Challenge 2. Uniting powerful nobles

The Maratha state had become reliant on military chiefs controlling substantial territories and resources.

Shahu needed capable officials who could win over influential sardars, arrange revenue collection and negotiate with the Mughal government.

One such figure was Balaji Vishwanath.

## 8. Balaji Vishwanath (Peshwa, 1713–1720)

Balaji Vishwanath was appointed Peshwa by Shahu in 1713.

He helped stabilize Shahu's government and increased the political importance of the Peshwa's office.

### His major achievements

1. Consolidation of Shahu's authority: He negotiated with and won over influential Maratha chiefs.

2. Mughal diplomacy: During the political turmoil at Delhi, the Marathas cooperated with the powerful Sayyid Brothers.

3. Imperial recognition of revenue claims (1719): Mughal grants recognized important Maratha claims to swarajya, chauth and sardeshmukhi under negotiated arrangements.

4. Establishing Peshwa influence: His success made the office increasingly powerful and prepared the way for his son, Baji Rao I.

1720: Balaji Vishwanath died; Baji Rao I became Peshwa.

## 9. Baji Rao I (1720–1740): Expansion beyond Maharashtra

Military reputation: Highly effective mobile cavalry commander

### Why was Baji Rao I important?

Baji Rao recognized that the Mughal Empire was becoming increasingly weak after Aurangzeb's death in 1707.

Instead of restricting Maratha power to Maharashtra, he pursued campaigns in the Deccan and northern India.

His strategy emphasized cavalry mobility, rapid advances, control of important routes and opportunities created by Mughal political weakness.

### Major achievements

|Year|Event|Importance|
|---|---|---|
|1728|Battle of Palkhed|Defeated and strategically outmaneuvered Nizam-ul-Mulk|
|1729–1731|Campaigns involving Bundelkhand, Gujarat and Malwa|Expanded Maratha influence|
|1737|Advance towards Delhi|Exposed weakness of Mughal authority|
|1737–1738|Bhopal campaign|Forced the Nizam to negotiate|
|1740|Baji Rao I died|Succeeded by Balaji Baji Rao|

### Battle of Palkhed (1728)

Problem: Nizam-ul-Mulk challenged the expansion of Maratha influence and intervened in Deccan politics.

Action: Baji Rao used rapid cavalry movements to disrupt the Nizam's supplies and manoeuvred him into an unfavourable military position.

Result: The Nizam was compelled to negotiate.

UPSC significance: Palkhed illustrates the effectiveness of mobile warfare against a slower army dependent on supply routes and heavy equipment.

### Rise of regional Maratha families

As campaigns extended beyond Maharashtra, various commanders acquired substantial influence in their territories.

These included the ancestors or leading figures of the Scindia, Holkar, Gaekwad and Nagpur Bhonsle houses.

Their growing independence would eventually shape the confederacy.

## 10. Balaji Baji Rao / Nanasaheb (1740–1761): Expansion and the Panipat disaster

Balaji Baji Rao, popularly known as Nanasaheb, succeeded his father Baji Rao I.

### Major developments

1. Political centralization at Pune: The Peshwa's authority grew relative to the Chhatrapati.

2. Death of Shahu (1749): The Chhatrapati retained royal legitimacy, but practical administrative and military power became increasingly concentrated with the Peshwa and powerful sardars.

3. Northern expansion: Maratha armies extended their influence into Malwa, Delhi and Punjab.

4. Increasing decentralization: The territorial growth strengthened powerful regional chiefs who controlled military resources and revenue.

### Why did the Marathas clash with Ahmad Shah Abdali?

The Marathas expanded towards Punjab and northwestern India, entering territories contested by Ahmad Shah Durrani (Abdali) of Afghanistan.

Both sides sought influence over Delhi and northern India.

The Marathas also faced opposition from certain regional powers, including the Rohilla chiefs, while obtaining only limited cooperation from some potential allies.

### Why did the Marathas lose?

1. Extended supply lines: Their army operated far from its principal Deccan base.

2. Isolation: They were unable to secure enough effective support from important regional powers.

3. Food shortages: Afghan forces disrupted supply routes during the prolonged confrontation.

4. Large camp: The Maratha army was accompanied by a substantial non-combatant population, complicating logistics.

5. Battlefield losses: Important leaders and fighting units suffered catastrophic casualties.

- The Peshwa's political prestige was badly damaged.

Madhavrao I became Peshwa at a young age after Nanasaheb's death.

### What challenges did he inherit?

- Severe casualties and damaged prestige after Panipat.
    
- Financial and administrative problems.
    
- Rivalries among influential nobles.
    
- Intervention by the Nizam and other powers.
    
- Conflict with his uncle Raghunathrao.
    

### Major actions and achievements

1. Restored administration: Worked to revive revenue collection, political discipline and military effectiveness.

2. Defeated the Nizam: Maratha forces won the Battle of Rakshasbhuvan (1763).

His reign proved that the 1761 defeat at Panipat had not ended Maratha power.

However, Madhavrao died in 1772, and subsequent succession disputes weakened the Peshwa government again.

## 13. Rise of the Maratha Confederacy

The Maratha Confederacy was a political-military system in which powerful hereditary chiefs exercised extensive authority over particular regions while remaining connected, to varying degrees, to the Peshwa and the wider Maratha political order.

### Five major centres of power

Chhatrapati — Satara

Dynastic legitimacy, increasingly limited executive power

Peshwa — Pune

Leading political and coordinating authority

Scindia / Shinde

Gwalior / Malwa

Holkar

Indore / Malwa

Gaekwad

Baroda / Gujarat

Bhonsle

Nagpur / Berar

Simplified political structure. These chiefs were not always subordinate in practice and often pursued their own policies.

|House|Main regional centre|Key personalities|
|---|---|---|
|Peshwa|Pune|Baji Rao I, Madhavrao I, Baji Rao II|
|Scindia / Shinde|Ujjain, later Gwalior|Ranoji Shinde, Mahadji Scindia, Daulat Rao Scindia|
|Holkar|Indore / Malwa|Malhar Rao Holkar, Ahilyabai Holkar, Yashwantrao Holkar|
|Gaekwad|Baroda (Vadodara), Gujarat|Pilaji Gaekwad, Damaji Gaekwad|
|Bhonsle|Nagpur / Berar|Raghuji Bhonsle and successors|

Important: These five houses were the principal political centres, not the only Maratha families. Other sardars and ruling houses also exercised significant authority.

### Advantages of the confederacy

- Allowed extensive territorial expansion.
    
- Enabled local military mobilization and regional governance.
    
- Provided commanders with resources and incentives.
    
- Made it difficult for one defeat to destroy the entire political network.
    

### Weaknesses

- Rivalries between chiefs.
    
- Unequal political and financial contributions.
    
- Difficulty coordinating military strategy.
    
- Increasing dependence on individual leaders.
    
- Opportunity for the British to negotiate separately with different Maratha houses.
    

UPSC Mains concept: The Marathas demonstrated substantial capacity for expansion, but territorial reach did not automatically create a uniform, centralized administration.

## 14. Nana Fadnavis and Mahadji Scindia: Preservation of Maratha power

### Nana Fadnavis (1742–1800)

Nana Fadnavis became one of the most influential political figures at Pune.

Following the murder of Peshwa Narayanrao in 1773, he helped organize a regency associated with the infant Madhavrao II (Sawai Madhavrao).

He was important for:

- Negotiating among rival chiefs.
    
- Preserving the Peshwa government.
    
- Managing diplomacy with the British and Indian powers.
    
- Opposing attempts by Raghunathrao to monopolize the Peshwaship.
    

### Mahadji Scindia (c. 1730–1794)

Mahadji Scindia became a dominant Maratha leader in northern India.

He reconstructed Maratha military influence after Panipat, expanded his authority over Delhi politics and developed infantry forces trained along European lines.

His influence over the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II was especially important.

### Why these leaders matter

During the later eighteenth century, the Maratha Confederacy depended increasingly on powerful regional leaders who could command armies, revenue and diplomatic networks.

Their achievements strengthened Maratha power, but also illustrated its decentralization.

## 15. The Three Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1818)

The British East India Company gradually became the strongest competitor of the Maratha Confederacy.

Unlike Mysore, which had a relatively concentrated territorial centre, the Maratha Confederacy consisted of several powerful regional houses. This complicated Maratha cooperation and enabled British diplomatic intervention.

### First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782)

Cause: A succession dispute over the Peshwaship after Narayanrao's murder.

Raghunathrao sought British support to secure power.

Important events:

- Treaty of Surat (1775): Raghunathrao allied with the Bombay government of the Company.
    
- Treaty of Purandar (1776): Attempted to resolve the dispute under different arrangements.
    
- Battle/Convention of Wadgaon (1779): British forces suffered a serious setback.
    
- Treaty of Salbai (1782): Ended the war.
    

Result: The British failed to impose Raghunathrao as Peshwa. Salbai brought a period of relative peace between the two sides.

### Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805)

Cause: Conflict between Peshwa Baji Rao II and rival Maratha chiefs, particularly Yashwantrao Holkar.

After being defeated near Pune in 1802, Baji Rao II sought British protection.

He signed the Treaty of Bassein on 31 December 1802.

### Why was the Treaty of Bassein important?

It required the Peshwa to accept a British subsidiary force and substantial restrictions on independent diplomacy.

Why did the British want it? Control over the Peshwa offered political legitimacy and an opportunity to expand British influence throughout western and central India.

Other major Maratha chiefs opposed the resulting British intervention.

Major battles and treaties:

|Year|Event|Result|
|---|---|---|
|1803|Battle of Assaye|British victory under Arthur Wellesley|
|1803|Battle of Argaon|British victory over Nagpur Bhonsle forces|
|1803|Battle of Laswari|British victory against Scindia forces|
|1803|Treaty of Deogaon|Nagpur Bhonsle ceded territories|
|1803|Treaty of Surji-Anjangaon|Scindia made territorial concessions|
|1805|Settlement with Holkar|Hostilities eventually ended|

UPSC significance: The Second Anglo-Maratha War greatly increased British control over Indian political affairs.

### Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818)

Background: British expansion, intervention in Maratha politics and campaigns against the Pindaris intensified tensions.

Major events:

- 1817: Peshwa Baji Rao II fought the British near Khadki (Kirkee), Pune.
    
- 1817: British forces fought the Nagpur Bhonsle army at Sitabuldi.
    
- 1818: British forces fought and defeated Maratha armies in several engagements, including Ashti.
    
- 1818: Baji Rao II surrendered, and the Peshwaship was abolished.
    

Result: The British annexed much of the Peshwa's territory.

The surviving Maratha royal and princely states continued under varying forms of British control. Satara, for example, survived as a separate princely state until its later annexation in 1848.

Important distinction: 1818 ended the Peshwa-led Maratha political system as a major independent power, not the existence of every Maratha-ruled state.

### Was Panipat in 1761 the main cause of the Maratha collapse in 1818?

Not by itself.

Panipat was a severe setback, but the Marathas recovered significant influence under Madhavrao I and Mahadji Scindia.

Their eventual defeat resulted from the combined effects of internal political rivalries, decentralized military-fiscal arrangements, British military strength and Company diplomacy.

Mains takeaway: The defeat of the Maratha Confederacy was a prolonged process, not the consequence of a single battle.

## 19. UPSC Mains model answer

Question: “The Maratha Confederacy's extensive territorial expansion was accompanied by structural weaknesses that facilitated British supremacy.” Examine. (250 words)

The Marathas emerged as a major regional power under Chhatrapati Shivaji, whose coronation in 1674 marked the consolidation of independent Maratha kingship. During the eighteenth century, the Peshwas expanded Maratha influence across large parts of India, while powerful regional houses such as the Scindias, Holkars, Gaekwads and Bhonsles acquired extensive territories.

The confederate structure possessed important strengths. It enabled rapid military mobilization, decentralized administration and flexible alliances. Under Baji Rao I, Maratha influence expanded into Malwa and northern India. Following the setback at Panipat in 1761, Madhavrao I and Mahadji Scindia restored significant political and military authority.

However, expansion created structural problems. Regional chiefs increasingly maintained independent armies, controlled revenue sources and pursued competing political interests. The Peshwa lacked an effective mechanism to ensure permanent cooperation among them.

These weaknesses were exploited by the British East India Company. Succession disputes contributed to the First Anglo-Maratha War. The Treaty of Bassein (1802), signed by Baji Rao II, undermined the political independence of the Peshwa and precipitated opposition from other Maratha chiefs.

British victories in the Second and Third Anglo-Maratha Wars progressively destroyed the confederacy's military and political autonomy.

Thus, the decline of the Maratha Confederacy resulted not merely from battlefield defeats but also from the interaction of decentralized authority, internal rivalry, financial fragmentation and British diplomatic expansion.
