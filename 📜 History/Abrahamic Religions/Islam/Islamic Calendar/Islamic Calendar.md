The Islamic calendar is the **Hijri calendar**.

Its years are counted from the **Hijrah**, the migration of Prophet Muhammad from Makkah to Madinah.

Read this note first. Then open [[The Twelve Months]] and [[Festivals and Holy Days]].

## Say these three dates separately

People often collapse three different moments into one sentence. Keep them apart.

|     | What happened                               | Date                                                                                                |
| --- | ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | The calendar's Year 1, Day 1                | **1 Muharram 1 AH = Friday, 16 July 622 CE** (Julian)                                               |
| 2   | The Hijrah journey itself                   | **Rabi' al-Awwal 1 AH**, about September 622. Traditional arrival in Madinah: **12 Rabi' al-Awwal** |
| 3   | The calendar is adopted for official dating | Caliph **Umar**, about **17 AH** (some reports say 16 AH), around **638 CE**                        |

On 1 Muharram 1 AH, the migration has not happened yet. That day is the first day of the lunar year in which the migration later takes place.

The journey comes in the third month of that year. Histories place Prophet Muhammad's arrival in Madinah on 12 Rabi' al-Awwal. On the calculated calendar, that day is 24 September 622 in the Julian calendar, which is 27 September 622 on the Gregorian calendar. Early reports differ by a day or two about the exact Monday of the journey. They agree on the month: Rabi' al-Awwal.

The background of the Hijrah is in [[C2. Foundation of Islam]].

## Why Umar began it

During Umar's caliphate, official letters had no year on them. A report says Abu Musa al-Ash'ari, governor of Basra, asked for dates because he could not tell which letter was earlier.

Umar consulted the companions. The reports, including those preserved by al-Tabari and later scholars such as Ibn Hajar, describe a discussion with several proposals:

| Proposal | What it would have counted from |
| --- | --- |
| The Prophet's birth | About 570 CE |
| The first revelation | About 610 CE |
| The Hijrah | 622 CE |
| The Prophet's death | 632 CE |

They chose the **Hijrah**. In one famous wording, Umar says the Hijrah separated truth from falsehood. Another report says Ali ibn Abi Talib proposed dating from the Hijrah.

Then they chose the first month of the year. Some suggested Ramadan. Some suggested Rajab. Some suggested the month of the journey. The decision was **Muharram**, because it was already a sacred month and the month when pilgrims returned home after Hajj.

So the calendar is named from the journey, and the year opens in Muharram.

## How a month begins

![[hilal-new-month.png]]

The new month begins when the **hilal**, the thin crescent, is seen in the west after sunset.

| Rule | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Crescent seen after the 29th day | Tonight is the 1st |
| Crescent not seen | The month runs to 30 days, then the next month begins |
| The day starts at sunset | The night of a date comes before its morning |
| Local sighting | Two countries can begin Ramadan on different civil dates |

A **tabular**, or calculated, Hijri calendar fixes every date in advance. Governments and computers use it. Religious start-days for Ramadan and the two Eids follow a moon-sighting announcement, so they can fall a day away from the table.

This medieval diagram shows the same idea from inside Islamic science. It is a manuscript drawing of the Moon's phases, preserved in the tradition of the scholar **al-Biruni** (973–1048). The Moon orbits, and the bright edge we see changes through the month.

![[lunar-phases-al-biruni.jpg]]

## Length of the year

| | Days |
| --- | --- |
| One lunar month | 29 or 30 |
| Twelve lunar months | **354**, or **355** in a leap year |
| Gregorian year | 365, or 366 in a leap year |
| Difference | About **10 to 11 days** every year |

In the calculated calendar, the odd months have 30 days and the even months have 29. In 11 years of every 30-year cycle, Dhul Hijjah gains one extra day. Those are the leap years.

Over about **33 Gregorian years**, a Hijri date comes back to the same season.

![[lunar-year-drift.png]]

The month names were taken from seasons in old Arabia: spring, dry ground, heat. The Qur'an fixed the year at twelve lunar months and rejected *nasī'*, the pre-Islamic habit of inserting or postponing a month to keep the sacred months in a chosen season. After that, the names kept their old seasonal meanings, and the months themselves began to walk through summer and winter.

## The four sacred months

The Qur'an (9:36) says there are twelve months, and four of them are sacred.

| Month | Place in the year |
| --- | --- |
| **Dhul Qa'dah** | 11th |
| **Dhul Hijjah** | 12th |
| **Muharram** | 1st |
| **Rajab** | 7th |

Dhul Qa'dah, Dhul Hijjah, and Muharram stand in a row around Hajj. Rajab stands alone, in the middle of the year.

In the sacred months, fighting was forbidden. People used the peace to travel, trade, and perform Hajj and Umrah.

## The week

Friday is **Jumu'ah**, the day of the congregational prayer. The Islamic week has a gathering day. It does not work as a full day of rest in the way the Jewish Sabbath does.

The day changes at sunset.

| Arabic | English |
| --- | --- |
| al-Ahad | Sunday |
| al-Ithnayn | Monday |
| ath-Thulatha' | Tuesday |
| al-Arbi'a | Wednesday |
| al-Khamis | Thursday |
| al-Jumu'ah | Friday |
| as-Sabt | Saturday |

## Where this date falls now

Today in this note's writing is **3 October 2026**.

On the calculated Hijri calendar, that day is about **20 Rabi' al-Thani 1448 AH**.

A moon-sighting calendar can place the same civil day one Hijri date earlier or later.

The festivals of the year 1448 AH, and the ones just before it, are listed in [[Festivals and Holy Days]].

## A second calendar that also says "Hijri"

Iran and Afghanistan use a **Solar Hijri** calendar for ordinary civil life. It also counts from the Hijrah, and it follows the Sun. Its new year is Nowruz, at the spring equinox. Its months are Farvardin, Ordibehesht, and the rest.

Ramadan, Hajj, Eid al-Fitr, and Eid al-Adha follow the **lunar** Hijri calendar on this page. They do not follow the Iranian solar months.
