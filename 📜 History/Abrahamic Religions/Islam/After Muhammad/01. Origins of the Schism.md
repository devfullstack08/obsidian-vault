# Origins of the Schism

Chapter 1. Next: [[02. From Muhammad to Medieval India]]

**Open with this.** In 632 the new Muslim community had to answer one question: who leads after Muhammad? The two answers became **Sunni** and **Shia**. In that same century the state he left behind grew from Arabia to Spain in the west and to Central Asia and Sindh in the east.

---

## 1. The line, then the map

> His followers subsequently built an empire that would stretch from Central Asia to Spain less than a century after his death.

Read the line once. Then put the map on the screen and point in this order:

1. **Darker orange, Arabia.** This is 632. Muhammad has died. Power is consolidated here, not yet in Spain or Central Asia.
2. **Lighter orange, the wide band.** This is about a century later. One caliphate, not seven separate kingdoms. The gaps of sea are only the Mediterranean and the Red Sea.
3. **Finger from right to left.** Bukhara and Samarkand (Central Asia) → Iran → Arabia → Egypt → North Africa → Cordoba in Spain. That is the sentence.
4. **Two red dots are limits, not conquests.** Tours, 732: a raid into France was stopped. Constantinople: besieged, not taken.
5. **Sindh is on this map on purpose.** Debal, 712. The eastern end also touches Sindh. Who sent that army is [[04. Umayyads and the Road to Sindh|chapter 4]], not this page.

![[early-caliphate-spain-to-central-asia.png]]


### What “less than a century” means in dates

Muhammad died in **632**. A century later is **732**.

| Year        | What to say                                                              | Years after 632 |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------- |
| 632         | Death. Arabia consolidated. The succession fight begins.                 | 0               |
| 711         | Muslim armies enter Spain. Cordoba becomes the western city to remember. | 79              |
| 712         | Muhammad bin Qasim takes Debal in Sindh.                                 | 80              |
| by the 710s | Bukhara and Samarkand, in Central Asia, are under the caliphate.         | about 80        |
| 732         | Advance into France stopped at Tours.                                    | 100             |

So the line is true as a picture of reach: **Spain on one side, Central Asia and Sindh on the other, inside about one hundred years.** The capital of that century is **Damascus** (Umayyads), not Baghdad. Baghdad is founded in **762**, after this map.

## 2. The schism, in the order you should teach it

The empire grew. The community had already split over the leader. Draw the family before the two answers. Ali only makes sense when the student can see both lines at once.

![[situation-632-two-claims.jpg]]

Fictional scene, not a portrait. The cushion is empty because Muhammad has died and named no successor both sides accept. The men on the left are choosing a leader from the community. The family standing apart is the bloodline claim. No face in this picture is a historical likeness.

![[muhammad-ali-family-line.png]]

**How to teach the tree, top to bottom.**

1. **Hashim → Abd al-Muttalib.** Stop. He has two sons who matter here.
2. **Left son, Abdullah.** His son is **Muhammad**. Abdullah died before Muhammad was born.
3. **Right son, Abu Talib.** His son is **Ali**. So Ali is Muhammad’s cousin.
4. **Fatimah**, Muhammad’s daughter, **married that same Ali**. So Ali is also the son-in-law. One man, two relationships. Do not draw two Alis.
5. Their sons are **Hasan** and **Husayn**. Husayn is the one killed at Karbala in 680. Shia leadership follows this bloodline. Sunni leadership does not require it.

Two names repeat. **Abdullah** the father is not **Abdullah** the son. **Zaynab** and **Umm Kulthum** are both daughters of Muhammad and, later, daughters of Fatimah and Ali. Say “grandmother and granddaughter” once, or a student will merge them.

There is no photograph of any of these people. They lived in the seventh century, before cameras. A face on the internet is a later painting or a guess. Do not put that in front of a class as if it were evidence.

![[sunni-shia-schism-diagram.png]]

### Before the split

- **610.** Muhammad first reveals the message at Mecca. *Unveiled* in the old sentence means **revealed**, shown for the first time. It does not mean a monument.
- The faith is **Islam**, submission to God. It is **monotheistic** (એકેશ્વરવાદી): one God.
- It stands in the same prophetic line as Judaism and Christianity — Abraham, Moses, Jesus — and then adds its own law, covering worship and public life, including who holds authority.
- **622.** Hijra to Medina. **630.** Mecca comes under Muslim control. **632.** He dies at Medina.

### 632 — two answers

He left no successor in a form that both sides later accepted.

|                         | Sunni answer                                     | Shia answer                                                     |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Who should lead?        | A qualified member of the community              | A man from Muhammad’s family                                    |
| First leader they count | **Abu Bakr**, a companion, elected at **Saqifa** | **Ali ibn Abi Talib**, cousin and son-in-law, married to Fatima |
| Name                    | **Sunni**, from *sunna*, the way of Muhammad     | **Shia**, from *shi‘atu Ali*, partisans of Ali                  |
| What they reject        | Leadership fixed only by bloodline               | The caliphs who were chosen ahead of Ali                        |

Abu Bakr became the **first caliph** (khalifa, successor in leadership). Caliph does **not** mean the next prophet. Muslims hold Muhammad to be the last prophet.

The two camps were not finished political parties on one afternoon in 632. The names, doctrines and communities took shape over the next generations. Say that, or the student will think Sunni and Shia were born as modern blocs on a single day.

The four early caliphs, in Sunni memory the **Rashidun** (“rightly guided”), are Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Ali. Detail of each reign is in [[03. Succession and the Rashidun Caliphs]].

### 656, 661, 680 — three dates after the first choice

- **656.** Ali becomes caliph. He rules about five years, from **Kufa** in Iraq, and is assassinated in **661**.c
- **661.** The caliphate passes to the **Umayyad** family at Damascus. Shias do not accept these rulers. The dynasty itself is [[04. Umayyads and the Road to Sindh|chapter 4]].
- **680.** At **Karbala**, in modern Iraq, soldiers of **Yazid** (the second Umayyad caliph) kill Ali’s son **Husayn** and many of his companions.

Karbala is the story Shias return to: the family of the Prophet killed by the ruling caliph. Sunni caliphs feared that the Shia Imams — the descendants of Ali and Husayn, taken as the legitimate leaders — could turn that memory into a revolt. The result was further pressure on, and marginalisation of, Shias.

**Word trap.** Sunni *imam* usually means the man who leads prayer in a mosque. Shia *Imam* means the rightful leader from Ali’s line. Same English word, different job.

### Shias then split among themselves

They agreed that leadership belongs in Ali’s line. They disagreed on which son inherited it.

| Branch                       | Where the line breaks           | Where to place it                                  | One fact                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Zaydi**                    | At the **5th** Imam             | Mostly Yemen                                       | Imam rule in parts of Yemen lasted until the 1960s                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Ismaili**                  | At the **7th** Imam             | South Asia, plus a diaspora                        | Most Ismailis revere the **Aga Khan** as the living representative of their Imam. The **Fatimid** dynasty in Egypt was Ismaili.                                                                                 |
| **Twelver** (*Ithna Ashari*) | Twelve Imams, then a hidden one | Majority of Shias; Iran and the eastern Arab world | The 12th Imam entered **occultation** (hiddenness) around **941** (some accounts say 939) and will return at the end of time. Until then, authority rests with senior scholars, **ayatollahs** (“sign of God”). |

### Why many non-Arab converts became Shia

In the early centuries, many Christian, Jewish and Zoroastrian converts chose Shia rather than Sunni. The early caliphates were Arab empires and often treated non-Arabs as second-class. Becoming Shia was, for many of them, also a protest against that. Their older religious habits then marked Shia practice as distinct in ritual and belief. Do not say “all Shias were non-Arab converts.” Say that this was one important stream.

### From 1501 to the map students see today

For about nine centuries, Sunni dynasties held most of the ruling power. The clear exception is the Shia **Fatimids**.

- **1501.** The **Safavid** dynasty in Persia makes Twelver Shiism the state religion.
- For two centuries the Safavids fight the **Ottomans**, who hold the Sunni caliphate.
- By the seventeenth century those wars have roughly drawn the later borders of **Iran** and **Turkey**.

**Where the majority sits now**

- **Shia majority:** Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan, Bahrain.
- **Shia plurality** (the largest group, not more than half): Lebanon.
- **Sunni majority:** more than forty countries, from Morocco to Indonesia.

Sunnis **triumphed** (વિજય મેળવ્યો) politically for most of those centuries. That political victory is not the same thing as “Shias disappeared.”

## 3. If a student asks

**Did Muhammad conquer Spain?** No. He died in 632 with Arabia consolidated. Spain is 711, the work of later followers.

**Is the orange one country with one law everywhere?** No. It is the reach of the caliphate. Rule was tighter in some provinces than in others.

**Did Ali refuse Abu Bakr and start a war in 632?** He and his supporters objected to being passed over. The full civil war, the First Fitna, comes in 656, in Ali’s own reign. Do not collapse 632 and 656 into one battle.

**Are Sunni and Shia two religions?** No. Both are Muslims. The split is over legitimate leadership after the Prophet, and over the authority of the Imams. It later grew into differences of law, ritual and politics.

## 4. Do not mix these up

| Easy to confuse                       | Keep them apart                                                                    |
| ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 632 and 711                           | Death and the succession split, versus the entry into Spain                        |
| Sunni *imam* and Shia *Imam*          | Prayer leader, versus leader from Ali’s line                                       |
| Umayyad Damascus and Abbasid Baghdad  | 661 Damascus; Baghdad is 762, after the first century                              |
| Karbala 680 and Ali’s death 661       | Ali is killed in 661. His son Husayn is killed at Karbala in 680                   |
| Twelver, Ismaili, Zaydi               | 12th Imam; 7th Imam; 5th Imam                                                      |
| Safavid 1501 and the origin of Shiism | Shiism begins with the succession argument. 1501 is when Iran’s state becomes Shia |
