# The short story: 632 to 1206

Back: [[08. Recall Questions and Exam Traps]] · Order: [[02. From Muhammad to Medieval India]]

Read this once after the chapters. It is the same road, with nothing new added. The pictures are fictional scenes, not portraits and not photographs.

## 632 — who leads?

After Prophet Muhammad died in Medina in **632**, the Muslim community had to decide who should lead. That choice became the four **Rashidun** caliphs: **Abu Bakr (632–634)**, **Umar (634–644)**, **Uthman (644–656)**, and **Ali (656–661)**.

![[situation-632-two-claims.jpg]]

The empty place is the situation, not a historical object. One side chose a leader from the community. The other side held that leadership belonged with Ali and Muhammad’s family. Sunnis later accepted the first four caliphs as legitimate. Shia tradition held that Ali and the family had the stronger claim. The split was not finished on one day in 632. It became much deeper when **Husayn ibn Ali**, Muhammad’s grandson, was killed at **Karbala in 680**.

## The Rashidun take the armies outside Arabia

During those four reigns, Muslim armies expanded into **Syria, Palestine, Egypt, Iraq and Persia**.

![[situation-rashidun-expansion.jpg]]

One column stands for that whole advance. It is not a map of one march, and the faces are not the caliphs.

## The Umayyads, and Sindh long before Ghazni

After Ali’s death, the **Umayyad Caliphate (661–750)** ruled from **Damascus**. Muslim rule then reached from **North Africa into Spain in 711**, eastward into **Central Asia** (Bukhara and Samarkand), and into **Sindh in 711–712** under **Muhammad bin Qasim**.

![[situation-712-sindh-port.jpg]]

This is the port at the end of that eastern road. Qasim was a commander, not a caliph. The Arab conquest of Sindh happened centuries before Mahmud of Ghazni.

## Abbasids, then kingdoms of their own

In **750** the **Abbasids** defeated the Umayyads. They later ruled mainly from **Baghdad**, founded in **762**. Over time their political control weakened, and independent Muslim kingdoms appeared. One of them was the **Ghaznavid** state.

![[situation-distant-caliph.jpg]]

The city and the hill fort are drawn together so the situation is visible. They were not neighbours. A far caliph could still be named, while a local ruler held the army and the revenue.

## Mahmud, then Ghori, then Delhi

**Mahmud of Ghazni (998–1030)** repeatedly invaded northern and western India. Exam accounts commonly count about **17** expeditions, including the raid on **Somnath in 1025–26**. The campaigns were raids and also territorial expansion in the northwest. He did not establish the Delhi Sultanate.

About a century later came **Muhammad Ghori**. **Prithviraj Chauhan** defeated him at the **First Battle of Tarain (1191)**. Ghori returned and defeated Prithviraj at the **Second Battle of Tarain (1192)**. That victory established lasting Ghurid power in northern India.

![[situation-tarain-plain.jpg]]

Cavalry against elephants is the kind of meeting at Tarain. It is not the exact layout of either battle. Ghori lost the first and won the second.

Ghori left much of his Indian administration to his general **Qutb al-Din Aibak**. After Ghori’s death in **1206**, Aibak became independent and founded the **Mamluk (Slave) dynasty**, the conventional beginning of the **Delhi Sultanate**. Iltutmish later consolidated that state.

![[situation-1206-delhi.jpg]]

The seated man is not a portrait of Aibak, and the city is not a photograph of Delhi in 1206. The letter is the news that the master is dead, and the general is about to rule on his own.

## Say it in one breath

**632** Abu Bakr → Umar → Uthman → Ali, and the Sunni–Shia split, deepened at **Karbala in 680**. **661** Umayyads at Damascus; **711–712** Spain and Sindh. **750** Abbasids. **998–1030** Mahmud, not the Delhi Sultanate. **1191** Ghori loses, **1192** Ghori wins. **1206** Aibak.
