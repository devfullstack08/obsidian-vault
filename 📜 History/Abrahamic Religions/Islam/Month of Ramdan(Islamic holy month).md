Ramadan is mainly an **Islamic holy month**. It is **not a Christian or Jewish month**, but Christianity and Judaism also have important traditions of **fasting, prayer, charity, repentance, and spiritual discipline**. So we can compare Ramadan with similar ideas in Christianity and Judaism.

## Basic Introduction

**Ramadan** is the **ninth month** of the Islamic lunar calendar.

It is the holiest month in Islam because Muslims believe that the **Qur’an began to be revealed** to Prophet Muhammad during this month.

Muslims observe Ramadan by fasting, praying, reading the Qur’an, giving charity, controlling desires, and increasing good deeds.

Ramadan is a month of:

- Fasting
- Prayer
- Qur’an
- Charity
- Self-control
- Repentance
- Mercy
- Forgiveness
- Spiritual purification

---

## Meaning of Ramadan

The word **Ramadan** comes from an Arabic root connected with **intense heat or burning**.

Spiritually, it is understood as a month in which sins are burned away through fasting, worship, repentance, and good actions.

---

## Position in Islamic Calendar

Ramadan is the **ninth month** of the Islamic calendar.

The Islamic calendar is a **lunar calendar**, meaning it is based on the moon.

Because of this, Ramadan comes around **10 to 11 days earlier each year** in the Gregorian calendar.

## Why Ramadan Is Important in Islam

Ramadan is important because:

1. The Qur’an began to be revealed in this month.
2. Fasting in Ramadan is one of the Five Pillars of Islam.
3. Muslims believe this month brings mercy, forgiveness, and spiritual purification.
4. Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power, occurs in Ramadan.
5. It teaches self-control, patience, compassion, and discipline.
6. It increases charity and concern for poor people.
7. It ends with Eid al-Fitr, a major Islamic festival.

---

## Qur’an and Ramadan

Ramadan is strongly connected with the Qur’an.

Muslims believe that the first revelation came to Prophet Muhammad in the **Cave of Hira** through Angel **Jibril**.

The first revealed word was:

**Iqra** — meaning “Read” or “Recite.”

This event is connected with **Laylat al-Qadr**, also called the **Night of Power**.

## Laylat al-Qadr

**Laylat al-Qadr** means the **Night of Power** or **Night of Decree**.

Muslims believe that the Qur’an began to be revealed on this blessed night.

It is considered better than a thousand months.

The exact date is not fixed with full certainty.

Many Muslims search for Laylat al-Qadr in the **last ten nights of Ramadan**, especially the odd nights:

- 21st night
- 23rd night
- 25th night
- 27th night
- 29th night

Many communities especially observe the **27th night**, but the exact night is not universally agreed upon.

---

## Important Point About 21st Ramadan

The 21st night of Ramadan can be one of the possible odd nights of Laylat al-Qadr.

But it is not correct to say that the first revelation definitely happened on the 21st of Ramadan.

The better statement is:

**The first revelation happened in Ramadan on Laylat al-Qadr, and Muslims search for Laylat al-Qadr in the last ten nights, especially the odd nights.**

## Fasting in Ramadan

Fasting in Ramadan is called **Sawm**.

It is one of the **Five Pillars of Islam**.

Muslims fast from **dawn to sunset**.

During the fast, Muslims avoid:

- Food
- Drink
- Smoking
- Sexual relations
- Bad language
- Anger
- Lying
- Wrong actions

Fasting is not only about hunger.

It is about controlling the body, mind, tongue, and desires.

## Suhoor and Iftar

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Suhoor | Pre-dawn meal before fasting begins |
| Iftar | Meal eaten at sunset to break the fast |

Muslims usually eat **suhoor** before dawn.

They break the fast at sunset with **iftar**.

Many Muslims break the fast with dates and water, following the practice of Prophet Muhammad.

---

## Daily Ramadan Routine

| Time | Practice |
|---|---|
| Before dawn | Suhoor |
| Dawn | Fasting begins |
| Daytime | Fasting, prayer, work, self-control |
| Sunset | Iftar |
| Night | Taraweeh prayer, Qur’an recitation, worship |

---

## Taraweeh Prayer

**Taraweeh** is a special night prayer performed during Ramadan.

It is usually prayed after the Isha prayer.

In many mosques, the Qur’an is recited over the nights of Ramadan.

Taraweeh is especially associated with Ramadan.

---

## Charity in Ramadan

Charity is very important in Ramadan.

Muslims are encouraged to help the poor and needy.

Important charity terms:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Zakat | Obligatory charity |
| Sadaqah | Voluntary charity |
| Zakat al-Fitr | Charity given before Eid al-Fitr |

---

## Zakat al-Fitr

**Zakat al-Fitr** is a special charity given before Eid al-Fitr.

It helps poor people celebrate Eid.

It is given before the Eid prayer.

---

## Eid al-Fitr

Ramadan ends with **Eid al-Fitr**.

Eid al-Fitr means the **Festival of Breaking the Fast**.

It is celebrated on the first day of **Shawwal**, the month after Ramadan.

On Eid al-Fitr, Muslims:

- Pray Eid prayer
- Give Zakat al-Fitr
- Wear clean or new clothes
- Meet family and friends
- Share food
- Thank Allah for completing Ramadan

---

## Main Purpose of Ramadan

The main purpose of Ramadan is to develop **taqwa**.

**Taqwa** means God-consciousness, piety, and awareness of Allah.

Ramadan teaches Muslims to control desires and live a disciplined moral life.

## Who Must Fast?

Fasting is required for adult Muslims who are physically and mentally able.

Some people are exempt or allowed to delay fasting.

| ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |

| Sick people         | May delay or compensate                                     |

| Pregnant women      | May delay if fasting is harmful                             |

## Things That Break the Fast

Common things that break the fast include:

- Eating intentionally
- Drinking intentionally
- Smoking
- Sexual relations
- Intentional vomiting in many rulings

The exact legal details may differ among Islamic schools of law.

---

## Things That Reduce the Reward of Fasting

Even if the physical fast is not broken, bad actions reduce its spiritual reward.

Examples:

- Lying
- Backbiting
- Anger
- Fighting
- Cheating
- Using abusive language
- Looking at forbidden things
- Wasting time in sinful activities

---

## Ramadan and Jihad al-Nafs

Ramadan is connected with **jihad al-nafs**, meaning the struggle against one’s own lower desires.

It teaches a person to fight against:

- Greed
- Anger
- Ego
- Laziness
- Bad habits
- Sinful desires

## Ramadan and Christianity

Ramadan is not a Christian month.

Christians do not observe Ramadan as a religious obligation.

However, Christianity also has traditions of fasting, prayer, repentance, and charity.

The closest comparison is **Lent**.

---

## Lent in Christianity

**Lent** is a period of fasting, prayer, repentance, and self-discipline before Easter.

It lasts about 40 days in many Christian traditions.

During Lent, Christians may give up certain foods, habits, or pleasures.

The purpose is spiritual preparation, repentance, and remembering the suffering of Jesus Christ.

---

| Ending Festival | Eid al-Fitr | Easter |

## Ramadan and Judaism

Ramadan is not a Jewish month.

Jews do not observe Ramadan as a Jewish religious month.

However, Judaism also has important fasting days and repentance traditions.

The most important Jewish fast day is **Yom Kippur**.

---

## Yom Kippur in Judaism

**Yom Kippur** means the **Day of Atonement**.

It is the holiest day in Judaism.

Jews observe it with fasting, prayer, repentance, and asking God for forgiveness.

It is different from Ramadan because it is one day, not a full month.

---

## Important Difference

Ramadan is specifically Islamic.

Christians and Jews do not celebrate Ramadan as part of their religion.

But fasting, prayer, charity, repentance, and self-control are common values in Islam, Christianity, and Judaism.

## Short Summary

Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic lunar calendar and the holiest month in Islam. Muslims believe that the Qur’an began to be revealed to Prophet Muhammad during Ramadan on Laylat al-Qadr. Fasting in Ramadan is one of the Five Pillars of Islam. Muslims fast from dawn to sunset, pray, read the Qur’an, give charity, and seek forgiveness. Ramadan teaches taqwa, self-control, patience, compassion, and spiritual purification. Ramadan ends with Eid al-Fitr. Ramadan is not a Christian or Jewish month, but Christianity and Judaism also have fasting and repentance traditions, such as Lent in Christianity and Yom Kippur in Judaism.
