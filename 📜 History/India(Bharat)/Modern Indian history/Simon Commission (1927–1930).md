Imagine India in 1928. The country was under British rule, and Indians had been demanding greater control over their own government.

The British government announced that it would send a commission to India to examine how the country was being governed and recommend future political reforms.

But there was one major problem:

**The commission had seven British members and not a single Indian member.**

Indian nationalists asked an important question:

_How can seven British politicians decide the political future of millions of Indians without giving Indians a place on the commission?_

This disagreement sparked protests across India, where people carried black flags and shouted:

**"SIMON GO BACK!"**

To understand the full event, we need to start in 1919.

## **1. Background — Why was the Simon Commission created?**

### **1919 — Government of India Act**

After World War I, the British introduced the Government of India Act 1919, based on the Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms.

It introduced **dyarchy (dual government)** in British Indian provinces.

|Reserved subjects|Transferred subjects|
|---|---|
|Police|Education|
|Law and order|Public health|
|Finance|Agriculture|
|Controlled by Governor and his executive councillors|Administered by Indian ministers, subject to the Governor's overriding powers|

In simple words, Indians received some responsibility, but the British government retained control over several crucial departments.

The Act also provided for a statutory commission to review how these reforms were functioning after ten years.

Therefore, a review was expected around **1929**, but the British government appointed the commission earlier, in **November 1927**.

## **2. November 1927 — Appointment of the Simon Commission**

![[sir-john-simon.jpg]]

![[sir-john-simon’s-bombay-departure.png]]


**Sir John Simon**

- Official name: Indian Statutory Commission
- Appointed: November 1927
- Appointed by: British Conservative government under Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin
- Chairman: Sir John Simon
- Members: 7, all British parliamentarians
- Purpose: Review the Government of India Act 1919 and recommend constitutional reforms

One interesting fact: Clement Attlee, who later became British Prime Minister during India's independence in 1947, was also a member of the Simon Commission.

### **Why did Indians oppose it?**

There were three major reasons.

1. **No Indian representation:** Every member was British, despite the commission being responsible for examining India's constitutional future.
    
2. **Question of self-government:** Indian nationalists believed Indians should participate in decisions about their own political rights.
    
3. **Disappointment with the 1919 reforms:** Many Indian leaders wanted responsible government and greater self-rule, not another review controlled entirely by British politicians.


The Indian National Congress decided to boycott the commission at its December 1927 Madras session. Important sections of the Muslim League and other political organisations also opposed it.

However, opposition was not universal. Certain political groups and leaders cooperated with the commission or presented evidence to it.

## **3. 3 February 1928 — Simon Commission arrives in India**

On **3 February 1928**, the Simon Commission arrived at Bombay (present-day Mumbai).

Instead of a welcoming reception, the commissioners encountered demonstrations.

Protesters:

- Carried black flags as symbols of opposition.
    
- Organised strikes (_hartals_) and public demonstrations.
    
- Shouted **"Simon Go Back!"**
    
- Boycotted the commission's proceedings.

The slogan meant:

_We reject a commission that will decide India's constitutional future without Indian representation._

The slogan is commonly credited to the nationalist and socialist leader **Yusuf Meherally**.

The protests were not confined to Bombay. Demonstrations followed the commission to other Indian cities, including Lahore and Lucknow.

## **4. 30 October 1928 — The Lahore protest**

This is the most important event connected with Lala Lajpat Rai.

On 30 October 1928, the Simon Commission reached Lahore, then part of British India and now in Pakistan.

Nationalist leaders organised a procession towards Lahore railway station to protest against the commissioners' arrival.

The procession included prominent leaders such as Lala Lajpat Rai, Madan Mohan Malaviya, Maulana Zafar Ali and others.

**Lala Lajpat Rai**, widely known as _Punjab Kesari_ or the Lion of Punjab, led the demonstration.

Protesters carried black flags and shouted "Simon Go Back."

British police had erected barricades to prevent the demonstrators from reaching the station.

When the procession approached the barricades, a confrontation developed between police and protesters, and police officers struck demonstrators with lathis (batons).

Lala Lajpat Rai was among those struck and injured.

### **Who was responsible for the police action?**

![[historical_officers_group_original.jpg]]

The police superintendent associated with the incident was **James A. Scott**.

Lajpat Rai publicly accused Scott of striking him. Several other nationalist leaders were injured in the police action.

This incident later became central to the revolutionary activities of Bhagat Singh and his associates.

Later that day, Lala Lajpat Rai addressed a public gathering.

He is remembered for declaring that the blows inflicted upon him would become the last nails in the coffin of British imperialism.

## **5. 17 November 1928 — Death of Lala Lajpat Rai**

Lala Lajpat Rai died in Lahore, 18 days after the police lathi charge.

His death was widely attributed by Indian nationalists to the injuries he received during the demonstration.

The British authorities disputed that the police blows caused his death. The question of medical causation was therefore contested at the time.

This was a major moment in the Indian independence movement. News of his death provoked anger and mourning, particularly among young revolutionaries.

## **6. 17 December 1928 — Bhagat Singh and the Saunders killing**

![[john-poyntz-saunders.png]]

Following Lala Lajpat Rai's death, several revolutionaries associated with the **Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA)** decided to retaliate against the police officer they considered responsible.

Their intended target was **James A. Scott**, the police superintendent associated with the lathi charge.

However, on **17 December 1928**, a case of mistaken identity occurred.

Bhagat Singh and Shivaram Rajguru shot **John P. Saunders**, an Assistant Superintendent of Police, after confusing him with Scott. Saunders died. During their escape, Chandra Shekhar Azad also fatally shot Indian police head constable Chanan Singh, who was pursuing them.

This event became known as the Saunders assassination or Lahore Conspiracy Case.

Remember the distinction for examinations:

- **Scott** — intended target, associated with the police action against Lala Lajpat Rai.
- **Saunders** — police officer actually killed on 17 December 1928.

## **7. What happened to the Simon Commission afterward?**

Despite the boycott, the commission continued its work and eventually published its report in **1930**.

Some of its important recommendations were:

|Recommendation|Meaning|
|---|---|
|Abolish provincial dyarchy|End the division between reserved and transferred provincial subjects|
|Introduce greater provincial autonomy|Give provincial governments more responsibility|
|Develop a federal structure|Explore a federation of British Indian provinces and princely states|
|Retain important safeguards|Preserve certain powers for British-appointed authorities|

The commission's recommendations were subsequently considered as part of broader constitutional discussions, including the Round Table Conferences of 1930–1932. These discussions eventually contributed to the **Government of India Act 1935**, although that Act was not simply a direct implementation of the Simon Report.

## **8. How did Indians respond politically? — Nehru Report, 1928**

The Indian response was not limited to street protests.

British Secretary of State for India Lord Birkenhead had challenged Indian political leaders to produce a constitutional proposal on which they could agree.

An All Parties Conference appointed a committee under **Motilal Nehru**, which produced the Nehru Report in August 1928.

It proposed, among other things, Dominion Status and a constitutional framework for India.

This was significant because Indian leaders were presenting their own vision of constitutional government rather than merely reacting to proposals coming from Britain.

The political disagreement over Dominion Status versus complete independence helped shape later developments, including the **Purna Swaraj resolution at the Lahore Congress session in December 1929**.

## **9. Complete chronological timeline**

1. **1919 — Government of India Act**
    
    Provincial dyarchy introduced; provision made for a constitutional review after ten years.
    
2. **November 1927 — Simon Commission appointed**
    
    Seven British members under Sir John Simon, with no Indian member.
    
3. **December 1927 — Congress boycott resolution**
    
    Indian National Congress decides to oppose the commission.
    
4. **3 February 1928 — Arrival in Bombay**
    
    Nationwide protests and the slogan "Simon Go Back."
    
5. **August 1928 — Nehru Report**
    
    Indians put forward their own constitutional proposals.
    
6. **30 October 1928 — Lahore lathi charge**
    
    Lala Lajpat Rai injured during the anti-Simon demonstration.
    
7. **17 November 1928 — Death of Lala Lajpat Rai**
    
    His death becomes a major rallying point in the freedom movement.
    
8. **17 December 1928 — Saunders killed**
    
    Bhagat Singh and associates target Scott but mistakenly kill Saunders.
    
9. **December 1929 — Purna Swaraj**
    
    Congress adopts complete independence as its goal.
    
10. **1930 — Simon Commission Report**
    
    Recommendations published; constitutional negotiations continue.
    
11. **1935 — Government of India Act**
    
    Major constitutional reforms enacted, including provincial autonomy.
