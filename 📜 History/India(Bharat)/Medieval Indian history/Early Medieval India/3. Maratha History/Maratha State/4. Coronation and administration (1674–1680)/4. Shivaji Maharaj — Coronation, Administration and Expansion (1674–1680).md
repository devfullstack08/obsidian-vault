Central question: Shivaji had already defeated powerful armies and taken important forts. Why did he need a coronation in 1674, and how did he turn those conquests into a kingdom?

Chapter 3 ends with the Treaty of Purandar (1665), the escape from Agra (1666), and the recovery of territory between 1670 and 1673. This chapter is the state he built. Those battles are not repeated here.

## 1. Why a coronation in 1674

By 1674 Shivaji held forts and settlements in western India. He collected revenue, kept an army, and dealt with other rulers.

His formal rank was still open to argument.

- **Bijapur** could treat him as a rebel officer from Shahaji's family, a man who had served the Adil Shahi government.
- **The Mughals** could point to the Treaty of Purandar, in which he had accepted a subordinate position.
- **Deshmukhs** held hereditary rights, and could dispute a ruler whose rank had never been declared.

The coronation was meant to give him a royal rank that did not depend on Bijapur or the Mughals.

What it was for:

1. Independent sovereign authority.
2. A recognised place for his family as a dynasty.
3. A firmer right to appoint officers and collect revenue.
4. A formal basis for royal orders, grants and ceremonies.
5. Legitimacy among the men whose support he needed.
6. A kingdom that other powers had to treat as a kingdom.

The coronation is a step in state formation. It is a political act, and also a ritual one.

---
## 2. Coronation at Raigad — 6 June 1674

Raigad was his capital, a hill fort in the Western Ghats. It was hard for a large army to take. It stood on the routes into the Konkan. It had room for the court and the offices.

The principal coronation was on **6 June 1674**.

**Gaga Bhatt**, a learned Brahmin associated with Varanasi, conducted the ceremony. Shivaji was recognised as **Chhatrapati**. The rite also settled, for the ceremony, the question of Kshatriya status and who was eligible to be crowned.

**Chhatrapati** is the title of a sovereign. *Chhatra* is the royal umbrella.

There were two ceremonies. The principal Vedic coronation was in **June 1674**. A second ceremony, in a different ritual tradition, followed in **September 1674**.

For Prelims, the date to write is **6 June 1674**, at **Raigad**.

-----
## 3. How the kingdom was governed

The Chhatrapati was the authority. Ministers and officers worked under him. The government used older Deccan practice, and tightened the king's own supervision over it.

-----
## 4. Ashta Pradhan — eight ministers

| Minister       | Other title    | Work                                |
| -------------- | -------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| **Peshwa**     | Mukhya Pradhan | General administration              |
| **Amatya**     | Mazumdar       | Finance and accounts                |
| **Sachiv**     | Surnavis       | Royal papers and correspondence     |
| **Mantri**     | Waqia-navis    | Court records and intelligence      |
| **Senapati**   | Sari-naubat    | Military command                    |
| **Sumant**     | Dabir          | Foreign affairs                     |
| **Nyayadhish** | —              | Justice                             |
| **Panditrao**  | —              | Religious grants, charity, ceremony |

Men associated with these offices:

- **Moropant Trimbak Pingle** — Peshwa.
- **Annaji Datto** — revenue, and the Sachiv's office.
- **Ramchandra Pant Amatya** — Amatya late in the reign. He matters more in the next period.
- **Hambirrao Mohite** — Senapati in the later reign.

The eight offices did not stay frozen, with the same man and the same work, for the whole reign.

This was not a modern cabinet. The ministers were not collectively responsible to a legislature. They served the king. The decisions that mattered stayed with Shivaji. Most of the eight kept records or ran a department. Panditrao and Nyayadhish were the specialised pair.

**Prelims trap.** The Peshwa under Shivaji is a minister under the Chhatrapati. The hereditary Peshwas who later lead the Maratha confederacy are a later office. Do not swap the two.

## 5. Province and village

| Term               | Meaning                                                |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Prant**          | A large territorial division                           |
| **Pargana / Tarf** | The revenue division below that                        |
| **Mauza**          | A village                                              |
| **Subhedar**       | Provincial supervision                                 |
| **Havaldar**       | Fort command, or a local charge, depending on the post |
| **Karkun**         | A clerk                                                |
| **Patil**          | Village headman                                        |
| **Kulkarni**       | Village accountant                                     |

Deshmukhs had armed retainers, revenue networks, and authority in their country. A disloyal deshmukh cost the king both money and men. Shivaji put important work under royal officers and limited what a hereditary chief could do on his own.

He did not abolish the village officers. Patil and kulkarni stayed. The change was stronger royal supervision of institutions that already existed.

## 6. Revenue

The army, the forts and the garrisons needed a regular income. Plunder could not be the treasury.

The state drew on agriculture, trade, customs, tribute, assignments of land, and the gains of campaigns.

### Land revenue

In the districts he administered directly, the land was measured and the produce estimated. Officers collected the revenue, and the aim was to cut what the intermediaries could skim.

Textbook accounts put the demand at about **two-fifths** of the produce. The rate was not one figure in every village for the whole reign. Region, soil and older practice changed it.

### Chauth and sardeshmukhi

|                  | **Chauth**                                        | **Sardeshmukhi**                          |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Plain sense      | A fourth                                          | A claim tied to a superior deshmukh right |
| Rate to remember | **25%**                                           | **10%**                                   |
| Where it falls   | Often on territory he did not administer directly | An additional claim, on a different right |
| What it did      | Carried Maratha pressure beyond the kingdom       | Asserted a higher fiscal authority        |

On a revenue base of 1,00,000, chauth is 25,000 and sardeshmukhi is 10,000. Both were not charged together on every district. The figures only fix the rates.

Shivaji did not invent chauth. Demands of this kind already existed on the western coast. He widened the political use of it. Collection across large regions becomes a Peshwa-period subject. That belongs in a later chapter.

- **Land revenue** — from territory under his own administration.
- **Chauth** — often from territory outside that administration.
- **Sardeshmukhi** — a further claim, on another basis.

Chauth is not another name for the land revenue.

## 7. The army

### Cavalry

| Term                    | Meaning                                            |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **Bargir**              | The horse and the equipment came from the state    |
| **Silahdar / Shiledar** | The cavalryman brought his own horse and equipment |

### Infantry

Infantry held forts, worked the passes, guarded supplies, and carried assaults. Cavalry was the arm of rapid movement. One did not replace the other.

### Ganimi kava

*Ganimi kava* is the name for mobile war: surprise, a strike at a weak point, an attack on the baggage and the food of a larger army in the hills.

The Marathas also fought pitched battles and conducted sieges.

**Prelims trap.** Shivaji did not fight only as a guerrilla.

---
## 8. Forts

| Fort           | Place in the system                                |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| **Raigad**     | Capital and royal seat                             |
| **Rajgad**     | The earlier principal stronghold                   |
| **Pratapgad**  | Watched Javli and the approaches through the hills |
| **Sindhudurg** | Sea fort on the Konkan coast                       |

A fort stored arms and grain, housed officers, held a pass, and could be defended by a small garrison when a large army entered the country. A town on open ground could not do that. The fort still needed food, water, powder and a road for messages.

A fort commonly had three officers: **Havaldar**, **Sabnis** and **Karkhanis**. Command, accounts and stores were split, so one man did not hold the whole fort in his hands.

---
## 9. The navy

The Konkan coast was contested by the **Portuguese** at Goa, the **English** at Bombay, and the **Siddis of Janjira**. A land army could not deal with an island fort or with ships on that coast.

The fleet was for coastal defence, for pressure on those rivals, and for the revenue of ports. **Sindhudurg**, near Malvan, was built in the **1660s** as the sea fort of that policy. The ships included **ghurabs** (grabs) and **galbats** (gallivats). The fleet did not command the Arabian Sea.

**Kanhoji Angre** becomes the great Maratha admiral after Shivaji's death, in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth century. He was not the admiral of the 1674 coronation.

---
## 10. Justice and religion

The **Nyayadhish** was the minister for justice. Disputes were also settled in the village, by hereditary officers, and by custom. The court at Raigad was not the only court.

The **Panditrao** handled religious grants and charity. The coronation used Hindu kingship to state a sovereign claim. The administration and the army also included men of other communities, as the Deccan of that century did.

The state was a seventeenth-century monarchy. It was not a modern secular democracy, and the wars were not one long religious conflict. Prelims and Mains both want the political fact: authority, alliance, revenue and legitimacy together.

## 11. Diplomacy

| Power             | What Shivaji had to settle           |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| Mughals           | Their pressure and their advance     |
| Bijapur           | The western Deccan and its forts     |
| Golconda          | Money and a southern understanding   |
| Siddis of Janjira | The coast and the island             |
| Portuguese        | Trade and coastal disputes           |
| English Company   | Trade, supplies, and the settlements |

A war on every side at once was beyond the treasury. A truce with one power bought time against another. The same ruler could be an enemy in one year and a party to a negotiation in the next.

---
## 12. The southern expedition — 1676–1678

This is the main territorial gain after the coronation.

Three pressures pushed it:

1. Forts and a standing army were expensive.
2. The Mughals and Bijapur still blocked an easy advance in the western Deccan.
3. Bijapur's hold on the south was loose, and local commanders were divided. Shahaji had also held a position in Karnataka, so the family already had a stake there.

Shivaji sought an understanding with **Abul Hasan Tana Shah** of Golconda.

| Year          | What happened                                                                |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **1676–1677** | The expedition is prepared and moves toward the eastern Deccan and Karnataka |
| **1677**      | He meets the Qutb Shahi ruler and secures military and financial help        |
| **1677**      | **Gingee** (Jinji), in present-day Tamil Nadu, is taken                      |
| **1678**      | **Vellore** falls in **August**, after a siege                               |

Gingee is a hill fortress, far from the western Maharashtra forts. A Mughal invasion of Maharashtra did not, by itself, take the southern holdings. Under Rajaram, Gingee becomes a centre of the war. That is Chapter 6.

The campaign was not a peaceful annexation. It used force, revenue demands and negotiation. By 1678 the kingdom had a position on both sides of the Deccan plateau.

----
## 13. The last years — 1678–1680

The southern forts needed commanders, revenue and a line of communication. Mughal and Bijapur power had not gone away.

**Sambhaji**, the eldest son, was at odds with his father. In **1678–1679** he went over to the Mughals for a time, and then returned. The succession crisis that follows the death belongs to Chapter 5.

---
## 14. Death — 3 April 1680

Shivaji died at **Raigad** on **3 April 1680**. He was about fifty. The accounts do not fix a medical cause that can be stated with certainty.

He left a crowned monarchy, a chain of forts, a revenue system, cavalry, infantry and a coastal fleet, and territory in the west and in the south. The kingdom still depended on unity, on revenue, and on holding off stronger neighbours.

His death is not the end of the Maratha state.
