Central question: Why did the Treaty of Bassein (1802) lead to the Second Anglo-Maratha War, how did the British defeat major Maratha armies in 1803, and why did the war continue against Yashwantrao Holkar until 1805?

This chapter is especially important for understanding how the British East India Company transformed itself into the dominant power in much of India before the final defeat of the Peshwa in 1818.

## 1. Where did Chapter 14 end?

We studied how Maratha politics became increasingly divided after the deaths of Mahadji Scindia in 1794 and Nana Fadnavis in 1800.

The Peshwa Baji Rao II had aligned himself with Daulat Rao Scindia against Yashwantrao Holkar.

On 25 October 1802, Holkar defeated the combined Peshwa–Scindia forces near Pune.

Baji Rao II fled and sought British military support.

On 31 December 1802, he signed the Treaty of Bassein (Vasai).

### What did Baji Rao II agree to?

- Accept a British subsidiary military force.
    
- Assign territory yielding approximately ₹26 lakh annually for its maintenance.
    
- Restrict his independent foreign relations.
    
- Accept British mediation in important political disputes.
    
- Receive British assistance in recovering the Peshwaship.
    

This was a major reversal.

Previously, the Peshwa had been the principal political coordinator of the Maratha Confederacy.

Now he had accepted substantial dependence on the Company.

The Second Anglo-Maratha War developed from the opposition of other Maratha powers to this new arrangement, combined with Lord Wellesley's expansionist policy.

## 2. India in 1803 — understand the political situation

The British East India Company already possessed major territorial and financial resources in Bengal and southern India.

It had defeated Tipu Sultan in the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War in 1799.

But the Maratha Confederacy still controlled or influenced extensive territories across western, central and northern India.

### Important powers

|Power|Leading figure|Political position|
|---|---|---|
|British East India Company|Lord Wellesley|Pursued expansion and subsidiary alliances|
|Peshwa at Pune|Baji Rao II|Accepted British protection|
|Scindia of Gwalior|Daulat Rao Scindia|Opposed increased British interference|
|Bhonsle of Nagpur|Raghuji II Bhonsle|Defended regional autonomy|
|Holkar of Indore|Yashwantrao Holkar|Rival of the Peshwa and Scindia; remained independent|
|Gaekwad of Baroda|Anand Rao Gaekwad|Had an established relationship with the Company|
|Mughal emperor|Shah Alam II|Remained at Delhi under shifting political protection|
|Hyderabad|Nizam Ali Khan|British subsidiary ally|

### The critical problem

The Marathas possessed considerable combined military resources.

However, they were not acting as one united political and military organization.

Their leaders had conflicting interests and rivalries.

The British could therefore deal with individual Maratha houses separately rather than face a fully coordinated confederacy.

### Scindia's concerns

Daulat Rao Scindia feared that British control over the Peshwa's diplomacy would undermine his own influence.

He had inherited Mahadji Scindia's powerful military establishment and extensive interests in northern India.

A British-protected Peshwa could challenge those interests.

### Bhonsle's concerns

Raghuji II possessed important political and revenue interests in central and eastern India.

He also feared the consequences of a British military establishment operating through the principal Maratha court.

### Did every Maratha chief oppose Bassein?

No.

This is essential for UPSC.

He therefore had little reason to subordinate himself to Scindia's leadership.

## 4. Lord Wellesley and the British expansionist policy

![Legacy Characters_India — Rose Gan](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/ICe02DYzict2jUBI1345fIjcqPDD8lK1S3oZJTLB87eJ2MWPQbFPPYx_2mQlXc1_lWOiTWWEGWul_vEXLwmucVeE8oEVbIIs2eVCNCw5bnDM865uNphjpME3MAs6AkQ9vPpiCdZOPABwWQNEdwdz0za53p-uWLKtd8TqOnyfxCM?purpose=inline)

### Who was Lord Wellesley?

Richard Wellesley served as Governor-General of Bengal from 1798 to 1805.

He pursued an assertive policy intended to expand Company influence and reduce the independence of other major Indian states.

### What was a subsidiary alliance?

A subsidiary alliance generally required an Indian ruler to accept Company military protection while undertaking financial and diplomatic obligations.

The specific terms differed between treaties, but such alliances often involved:

- Maintaining British troops at the ruler's expense.
    
- Restrictions on independent diplomatic relations.
    
- A British Resident at court.
    
- Exclusion of rival European military personnel.
    
- Dependence on the Company for external defence.
### Why did Wellesley want the Peshwa as an ally?

Because the Peshwa occupied an important position within Maratha politics.

If the Company controlled the Peshwa's external relations and military protection, it could gain influence far beyond the territory immediately governed from Pune.

### Did Wellesley seek only to defend Baji Rao II?

No.

Restoring Baji Rao was an immediate objective, but Company policy also pursued strategic territory, commercial security and regional supremacy.

UPSC Mains point: The war resulted from the interaction of internal Maratha rivalry and deliberate British imperial expansion—not from Maratha divisions alone.

## 5. British restoration of Baji Rao II (May 1803)

![Shaniwar Wada: The Spooky Side of Bajirao Mastani’s Epic Fort](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/bFlyCqOBOvBtV_Pdshs_SNsdZvsRS85VQ1QSEyXUGwdkL3ezVkXhMt_39NKZ5tZHgxIvBr1Re5Jr6JBSdHbSjSYWzDvh3Cau7eukYcRiLC3qbJ-xqKSHTCPaRMSSZAaMrkbtD5UXTSia7zqpDRa-ZoN2pt2R802SUycMEC-U_8s?purpose=inline)

### What happened after Bassein?

The Company assembled troops to restore Baji Rao II to Pune.

Major-General Arthur Wellesley, the younger brother of Governor-General Richard Wellesley, commanded important British forces in the Deccan.

British troops advanced towards Pune.

Holkar's forces withdrew rather than directly confront the advancing Company army in that campaign.

### Result

Baji Rao II was restored to the Peshwaship in May 1803, under British military protection.

### Why was this important?

The Company had demonstrated that it could determine who occupied the Peshwa's office.

This was a substantial political intervention.

However, the restoration did not secure acceptance from the other Maratha houses.

### What did the British demand next?

Wellesley sought assurances that Scindia and Bhonsle would not threaten the new British–Peshwa arrangement.

Both Maratha leaders maintained substantial forces.

Their failure to reach an acceptable settlement with the Company increased tensions.

Open war followed in August 1803.

## 6. Two principal military theatres — north and south

Unlike the First Anglo-Maratha War, the conflict in 1803 involved major coordinated British offensives across distant regions.

British military strategy, 1803

Northern theatre
**General Gerard Lake**
Aligarh, Delhi, Agra, Laswari
Against Scindia's northern forces

Deccan theatre
**Arthur Wellesley**
Ahmadnagar, Assaye, Argaon
Against Scindia and Bhonsle forces

### Why divide the operations?

Scindia's military establishment extended across central and northern India.

Bhonsle controlled major interests farther east.

A single British army could not easily defeat all these forces in one campaign.

Therefore, the Company pursued offensives on multiple fronts.

UPSC distinction:

- Arthur Wellesley: Assaye and Argaon.
    
- General Gerard Lake: Delhi and Laswari.

The British Library preserves correspondence and official records of the campaigns against Scindia and the Nagpur Bhonsle establishment.

## 7. Capture of Ahmadnagar (August 1803)

### Why was Ahmadnagar strategically important?

Ahmadnagar was a fortified centre in the Deccan, situated on routes connecting Pune, Hyderabad and central India.

Control of the fort could protect British military movements and weaken Scindia's southern positions.

### What happened?

Arthur Wellesley attacked Ahmadnagar in August 1803.

British troops captured the town's defensive positions and secured the fort after negotiations.

### Why was this an important first victory?

It helped Wellesley establish a secure position from which to conduct further operations.

It also demonstrated the vulnerability of Scindia's southern fortifications.

But the principal Maratha field armies had not yet been destroyed.

They remained capable of presenting a major military challenge.

## 8. Battle of Assaye — 23 September 1803

![Battle of Assaye - World History Encyclopedia](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/iybm85Z61Ll-HBpm8y8VQb0ZCsynt1gVGAG2I1b5Q5ol597TJNmep5ZuTyGNEhxiwC68a8DKEBECKsHVXODzjVUvoriIacaU7B9D8xgbCtx494cxkjqV01eHLoRsW0tI97mDehpx6vWk7XxuAOUqozjOFrAqRd-00-0KCrj9Ssw?purpose=inline)

This is the most important battle of the Second Anglo-Maratha War for UPSC Prelims.

### Where was Assaye?

Assaye is a village in the region of present-day Jalna district, Maharashtra, near the confluence of the Kaitna and Juah rivers.

### Who fought?

| British-led forces             | Maratha forces                                      |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Major-General Arthur Wellesley | Troops of Daulat Rao Scindia and Raghuji II Bhonsle |
| Company sepoys                 | Regular infantry trained under European officers    |
| British infantry and cavalry   | Maratha cavalry                                     |
| Supporting artillery           | Substantial Maratha artillery                       |

### Why were the Maratha forces formidable?

Do not imagine that this was simply a battle between British regular troops and irregular Maratha horsemen.

Scindia had inherited a professional army developed under Mahadji Scindia.

His forces included disciplined infantry and significant artillery.

These units were capable of fighting European-style battles.

### What happened?

1. Wellesley discovered the Maratha position.
    
    The opposing army was deployed near river barriers, with artillery defending important approaches.
    
2. British forces crossed the river.
    
    Wellesley chose to attack through a crossing that allowed his troops to approach the Maratha position.
    
3. Maratha artillery inflicted heavy losses.
    
    The battle became extremely costly for the British-led force.
    
4. British and Company infantry advanced.
    
    Disciplined formations attempted to capture the artillery positions and break the Maratha line.
    
5. Intense fighting followed.
    
    Both sides suffered severe casualties in artillery, infantry and cavalry engagements.
    
6. Wellesley's forces eventually prevailed.
    
    The Maratha army withdrew, leaving significant artillery behind.
    

### Why did Wellesley win?

Important factors included:

- Aggressive manoeuvring across the river.
    
- Effective infantry discipline during heavy fire.
    
- Coordination of infantry and cavalry.
    
- The ability to exploit weaknesses in the Maratha deployment.

However, the Maratha soldiers offered determined resistance.

It was not an effortless British victory.

### The historical result

Arthur Wellesley achieved one of his most important early military successes.

He later considered Assaye among his hardest-fought battles.

The National Army Museum records that his army numbered approximately 4,500, confronting a much larger force, and suffered very heavy casualties. These figures should be read as estimates rather than perfectly exact totals.
### UPSC takeaway

Assaye — 23 September 1803 — Arthur Wellesley — victory over forces of Scindia and Bhonsle.

Do not confuse Arthur Wellesley with Lord Richard Wellesley.

Richard was the Governor-General; Arthur was the field commander who later became the Duke of Wellington.

## 9. General Lake's campaign in northern India (1803)

![Aligarh’s French Connection](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/OuV8VVmMjiqP68WDyig8VBU1Wvwbn1WYwB5Cx3OBkadKcUnc_Wl9V0GZXQjZIPN-_WyUTnxriJFPODJ3YvTdAYm1jdfG79-QPjZjbmxjkTGflaLsOgu9fZOftGzkloz6JfIx39VUMlJgTRPxPlZs4vihFM0g1SNnBmqDWbZruBg?purpose=inline)

While Arthur Wellesley was fighting in the Deccan, General Gerard Lake attacked Scindia's northern military establishment.

### Why was northern India important?

Scindia controlled or influenced strategic positions across the Ganga–Yamuna Doab, Delhi, Agra and adjoining regions.

His forces also exercised considerable political influence over the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II.

If the British defeated Scindia in the north, they could acquire both valuable territories and the prestige of controlling the Mughal imperial capital.

### 4 September 1803 — Aligarh

Lake's forces captured Aligarh Fort, a key stronghold of Scindia's northern army.

The fort had been defended by troops associated with the European officers serving Scindia.

Its loss weakened the defensive network protecting Delhi.

### Why was Aligarh important?

It opened the route towards the Mughal capital and undermined Scindia's ability to organize resistance in the Doab.

## 10. Battle of Delhi — 11 September 1803

![Red Fort, Lahori Gate, west elevation | Delhi, India | imaginoso](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/rkldqnlg9qreziXG2Me5LeOkT5hRlVTpgDUW7GfI5RoRTwd1Z6PJAXhMpuk1TwFwgR7sB4BFb-FMDFoD2iuAVGLJvaMGc_I8XG3EaqCuP98Cw-GzJtLD2BrdEIqyqqsr_ENYjlMSjlf203MLprZL2LHRM7vHqo-m1Tu2Sn1dwBY?purpose=inline)

### Who controlled Delhi before this battle?

The Mughal emperor Shah Alam II still occupied the imperial throne.

But Scindia's military organization exercised substantial influence over the capital.

### What did General Lake do?

On 11 September 1803, British forces defeated Scindia's northern troops near Delhi.

The defending forces were associated with European-trained regular infantry.

### What happened afterward?

Lake entered Delhi.

The Mughal emperor came under British protection.

### Why was this more significant than an ordinary military victory?

For centuries, Delhi had represented imperial authority in much of northern India.

Even though the Mughal emperor no longer governed the empire of Akbar or Aurangzeb, his title retained considerable symbolic prestige.

By securing Delhi, the Company gained a powerful source of political legitimacy.

### Was Shah Alam II removed from the throne?

No.

He remained Mughal emperor.

But his political position became heavily dependent on the British.

## 12. British operations in Odisha and eastern India (1803)

![Explore Barabati Fort: Cuttack's Gem | Incredible India](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/Mcw_o3ksdWU7zTTv_jpKUoQkVjJkRbGw_yc86GR5b0ION3VTqD8Cl_sn5NrhzxBHbKF0HLHNwNmxW4VEYaxqfOK10LHJxvoRYSLa51_knrBNfRWkhcWmr--BIdoAaW_JeNN8G3K4VtRBDVd8QIw2FHSh3ExCks_Yr1MwrnGAh1I?purpose=inline)

Remember Chapter 10.

The Nagpur Bhonsle establishment had acquired important interests in Odisha through the eastern Maratha campaigns of the mid-eighteenth century.

These territories connected Bengal with the southeastern coast.

### Why did the British want Odisha?

The Company already possessed Bengal and the Northern Circars.

Control over the Odisha coast would improve communication between its eastern and southern territories.

It would also secure important coastal trade routes.

### What happened?

British forces under Lieutenant-Colonel George Harcourt advanced into Odisha during 1803.

They captured important positions in and around Cuttack.

The operations culminated in the British occupation of key coastal territories, including areas associated with Balasore and Cuttack.

### Why was this important?

Raghuji II Bhonsle's military interests extended far beyond Nagpur.

By attacking Odisha, the Company placed pressure on the Bhonsle establishment from another direction.

The territorial results were later formalized in the Treaty of Deogaon.

## 13. Battle of Argaon — 29 November 1803

![This is a natural and landscape scene](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/27678-l3Q91jxYl1yPOkQ7cJTBdX_BK6EljaNBUi810Ma8cImda2rqBEi9qo_shLfRRZfmoSYD66U45wQRRwbuDD78iIhxag8Ul5fgGU3FNbO5DIuW_NPemoCmrw_0L9ol9Z357oHv9qplEMnk6zXBGq82vw69fbe808L_FnK5znbtHmyZ0VZ85HnGP-AImP?purpose=inline)

### Where was Argaon?

Argaon (Argaum) is associated with the Vidarbha region of present-day Maharashtra, near the Akot–Ellichpur area.

### Who fought?

The British-led army commanded by Arthur Wellesley confronted forces associated with Raghuji II Bhonsle and Daulat Rao Scindia.

### Why did fighting continue after Assaye?

The defeat at Assaye had weakened the Marathas but had not destroyed their ability to regroup.

Important fortresses and regional armies remained under Maratha control.

The British therefore continued military operations to secure a decisive political settlement.

### What happened?

On 29 November 1803, Wellesley's forces attacked the Maratha army near Argaon.

Parts of the British-led force experienced confusion under heavy fire, but Wellesley restored order and pressed the attack.

The Maratha force was defeated.

### Why was this significant?

Argaon further weakened Raghuji II Bhonsle's ability to resist the Company.

It also made the defence of the remaining fortified positions in Berar more difficult.

### Capture of Gawilgarh — December 1803

![Gawilghur fort (also Gawilgarh or Gawilgad) was a well-fortified mountain stronghold at Chikhaldar](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/tZcGYhPQrG2ySXdAFgA6799ZXZsBjuVR1gV5w7FGfwoVBF4ZCp1UqZ4GW0sbq7KRSD5zfO1_NLrlirYPz7twKMPzcCdR8DOkwAgw_kyl0MkTGOcka-QAerXsPcboBI89lIzeWaPRCvBH_bdYKKT34igXlo2Z6g3Y-IfqZQnM4_RP3LhAOCtb15cTgtsc0xOK?purpose=inline)

Gawilgarh (Gavilgad) was a formidable hill fortress in the Satpura region.

British forces under Colonel James Stevenson, cooperating with Wellesley's campaign, captured it in December 1803.

The loss of Gawilgarh placed further pressure on the Nagpur Bhonsle government.

Raghuji II sought peace shortly afterward.

The National Army Museum identifies Assaye, Argaon and Gawilgarh as the principal achievements of Wellesley's 1803 campaign.
## 14. Treaty of Deogaon — 17 December 1803

This is one of the most important treaties of the war.

### Who signed it?

The treaty was concluded between Raghuji II Bhonsle of Nagpur and the British East India Company.

Negotiations were conducted on the Company's behalf by Mountstuart Elphinstone under Wellesley's authority.

### Why did Bhonsle agree?

His forces had suffered important defeats.

The British had captured strategic territory in Odisha and Berar.

Further military resistance threatened additional losses.

### Major provisions

|Provision|Meaning|
|---|---|
|Cuttack and Balasore|Transferred to the Company|
|Other Odisha territories|Company authority was established over important eastern holdings|
|Territory west of the Wardha|Significant concessions benefiting Hyderabad and the British alliance|
|Foreign relations|Bhonsle accepted restrictions affecting hostile European personnel and certain diplomatic arrangements|
|Political settlement|British–Nagpur relations were reorganized after the military defeat|

### Why was the loss of Odisha especially important?

The Company gained a more continuous strategic connection along the eastern coast.

It reduced the independent territorial reach of the Nagpur Bhonsles.

### Did the British annex Nagpur in 1803?

No.

Raghuji II remained ruler of Nagpur.

He suffered territorial and political losses, but his dynasty continued.

The complete British annexation of Nagpur occurred much later, in 1853, under the Doctrine of Lapse.

UPSC fact: Deogaon — 17 December 1803 — Raghuji II Bhonsle — Cuttack and Balasore.

## 15. Treaty of Surji-Anjangaon — 30 December 1803

![Gwalior Fort: Man Mandir Palace](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/oROA302lfdNlT0SV3zBbzuKHkdBJNMLD4MLncabZBnc3rM6ZBaNPoYzSLPDR9gwtykmSIi9bfWut4LP5-vUX-pQWa7ckJIGAYkkpDOoabRZ4JKKmnWYIt2uVpStoAztQpJoSgpefFhg0at6TIpL2fVsS6yrqDAxGWUaNvAb1VWe10pftsRROQY2_l6KGL3kl?purpose=inline)

This was the second major peace treaty concluded in December 1803.
### Who signed it?

Daulat Rao Scindia and the British East India Company.

### Why did Scindia agree?

His armies had suffered defeats in both principal theatres:

- Delhi and Laswari in the north.
    
- Assaye and related campaigns in the Deccan.
    

He had lost important fortified centres and much of his influence over the Mughal emperor.

### Major provisions

|Provision|Meaning|
|---|---|
|Ganga–Yamuna Doab|Scindia surrendered major territorial claims|
|Delhi and Agra region|Scindia relinquished authority over important northern territories|
|Bharuch (Broach)|Important western coastal territory ceded|
|Ahmadnagar interests|Important territorial concessions affecting his Deccan possessions|
|Bundelkhand and other claims|Several territorial and political rights were surrendered|
|Mughal emperor|Scindia relinquished his dominant position at the imperial court|
|Relations with other powers|British influence over Scindia's political arrangements increased|

### Why was this a major British success?

It substantially reduced Scindia's ability to challenge Company authority across northern India.

The Company acquired important territory and influence over Delhi.

### Did Scindia cease to exist as a political ruler?

No.

Daulat Rao Scindia retained a substantial territorial establishment.

But his ability to act independently in major matters had been weakened.

### Additional treaty in 1804

On 27 February 1804, Scindia concluded a further defensive and subsidiary alliance at Burhanpur with the British.
 
This must be distinguished from the main territorial settlement of December 1803.

British Library records separately identify both treaties and their dates.
### Deogaon versus Surji-Anjangaon

|Treaty|Date|Maratha ruler|Key loss|

## 16. Why did the war continue after December 1803?

You might ask:

If Scindia and Bhonsle signed peace treaties in December 1803, why is the war dated 1803–1805?

Because Yashwantrao Holkar remained outside the settlement.

### What was Holkar's position?

Yashwantrao Holkar

**Principal base:** Indore and Malw
**Important earlier victory:** Defeated Peshwa–Scindia forces near Pune in 1802
**Position in 1803:** Not part of the Scindia–Bhonsle coalition
**Conflict with the British:** Intensified in 1804

### Why did Holkar fight the British?

Holkar opposed British interference in the political affairs and territorial interests of the Maratha houses.

As British influence expanded after the defeat of Scindia and Bhonsle, relations with Holkar deteriorated.

Disagreements over territory, political recognition and military demands helped bring about open hostilities in 1804.

### Why was Holkar difficult to defeat?

He commanded highly mobile cavalry forces with experience operating across Malwa and northern India.

His troops could threaten extended British communications and supply routes.

The Company had already achieved major victories, but those victories did not automatically eliminate every independent Maratha army.

## 17. Monson's Retreat (1804) — a major British failure

This is a very important event for understanding why the Second Anglo-Maratha War was not a continuous series of British successes.

### Who was Monson?

Lieutenant-Colonel William Monson commanded a British-led force operating against Holkar in Rajasthan and Malwa.

### What happened?

During the summer of 1804, Monson advanced southwards, increasingly far from reliable support.

Holkar's cavalry threatened his forces.

Monson then attempted to retreat.

### Why did the retreat become disastrous?

- The army was far from secure bases.
    
- Holkar's cavalry harassed its movements.
    
- Heavy monsoon rains obstructed transport.
    
- River crossings became dangerous.
    
- Supplies deteriorated.
    
- The retreating force lost men, artillery and equipment.

### Result

Monson's force suffered heavy losses during its withdrawal towards Agra.

### Why was it significant?

British forces had defeated major Maratha armies in 1803, yet Holkar showed that the Company's army could still be highly vulnerable to mobile warfare and difficult terrain.

### What happened after Monson's Retreat?

Holkar continued operating in northern India.

His forces threatened British positions and communication routes.

General Lake launched operations against him.

### British counterattacks

The Company achieved important victories over Holkar's forces, including engagements at:

- Deeg (November 1804).
    
- Farrukhabad (November 1804).

However, Holkar still retained military resources and influential allies.

### Why was Bharatpur important?

Raja Ranjit Singh of Bharatpur, a Jat ruler, supported Holkar.

Bharatpur possessed formidable fortifications surrounded by defensive works and a moat.

General Lake decided to attack it.

### Siege of Bharatpur — January–April 1805

British forces besieged the fortress and carried out several major assaults.

The defenders repeatedly repelled the attacks.

The British suffered heavy casualties.

### Did the British capture Bharatpur in 1805?

No.

They failed to storm the fortress and eventually negotiated.

This was a serious setback for British military prestige.

### Was Bharatpur a Maratha capital?

No.

It belonged to the Jat state of Bharatpur, ruled by Ranjit Singh.

Its defence mattered to the Anglo-Maratha conflict because its ruler supported Holkar.

## 19. Why did British policy change in 1805?

Lord Wellesley's expansionist policy had produced substantial territorial gains.

But prolonged wars created financial and political problems.

### The Company's difficulties

- Military campaigns were extremely expensive.
    
- Monson's Retreat damaged British prestige.
    
- Bharatpur could not be captured.
    
- Holkar remained active.
    
- The Company faced growing criticism over aggressive warfare and expenditure.
### Changes in leadership

Lord Wellesley left office in 1805.

Lord Cornwallis returned briefly as Governor-General but died in October 1805.

Sir George Barlow subsequently directed Company policy.

### What did the new approach emphasize?

The Company increasingly sought to reduce expensive commitments and reach settlements with remaining opponents.

This encouraged negotiations with Holkar and modifications of some earlier arrangements.

Historical interpretation: British expansion was influenced not only by battlefield victory but also by the financial and political limits of imperial warfare.

## 20. Treaty of Rajghat (1805) — ending the conflict with Holkar

The final major settlement of the war is commonly known as the Treaty of Rajghat (Rajpur Ghat).

### When was it concluded?

The principal agreement is usually dated 24 December 1805, although some histories use 25 December or refer to subsequent confirmation and implementation in 1806.

### Who were the parties?

Yashwantrao Holkar and the British East India Company, through the negotiations conducted under General Lake.

### Major provisions

- Holkar gave up certain claims to territories north of the Chambal and to areas associated with the Peshwa and Bundelkhand.
    
- The Company recognized or restored substantial territories to Holkar.
    
- Restrictions were placed on certain political and military relationships.
    
- Hostilities were to cease.

Some concessions and restrictions were subsequently modified under the Company's less aggressive policy.

### Did Holkar surrender all his territory?

No.

The Holkar establishment survived.

Unlike the Peshwa in 1818, Holkar was not abolished as a ruling house.
### Why was this treaty important?

It ended the principal remaining hostilities of the Second Anglo-Maratha War.

But it did not create a permanent, stable political arrangement for all Maratha states.

The Company emerged with vastly greater power, while important Maratha houses retained enough resources to remain politically significant.

UPSC fact: Rajghat — 1805 — Yashwantrao Holkar — British East India Company.

### Were the Marathas militarily inferior in every respect?

No.

Scindia's regular infantry and artillery fought strongly at Assaye and Laswari.

Holkar's cavalry inflicted serious defeats during Monson's Retreat.

The defenders of Bharatpur successfully resisted repeated British attacks.

The Company's success arose from an advantageous combination of political alliances, strategic coordination, resources and military victories, rather than universal superiority in every engagement.

## 22. Consequences of the Second Anglo-Maratha War

### 1. British territorial expansion

The Company acquired or consolidated important territories in:

- The Ganga–Yamuna Doab.
    
- Delhi and the Agra region.
    
- Odisha, including Cuttack and Balasore.
    
- Bharuch and other western territories.
    
- Parts of Bundelkhand and the Deccan.

### 2. Mughal emperor under British protection

Shah Alam II remained emperor at Delhi, but his political position became dependent on the Company.

This strengthened Britain's symbolic authority in northern India.

### 3. Scindia and Bhonsle weakened

Both rulers lost significant territories and political influence.

However, neither ruling house was abolished.

### 4. Peshwa's dependence increased

Baji Rao II remained in office, but the Treaty of Bassein restricted his independent military and diplomatic position.

### 5. Holkar survived

Yashwantrao Holkar retained substantial influence following the 1805 settlement.

### 6. Conditions for future conflict

The British had become the dominant external power in Maratha politics, but they had not eliminated the principal Maratha houses.

Tensions over autonomy, military obligations and territorial claims continued.

These contributed to the Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818).

### Important distinction

1803–1805: British dominance greatly expanded.

1817–1818: The Peshwaship was abolished and the Maratha political order was fundamentally transformed.

Do not confuse the consequences of the two wars.

## 25. UPSC Mains — model answer

Question: “The Second Anglo-Maratha War established British supremacy in Indian politics but did not completely destroy Maratha power.” Examine. (250 words)

The Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805) marked a decisive stage in the expansion of British power in India. It arose from internal divisions within the Maratha Confederacy and the East India Company's intervention through the Treaty of Bassein in 1802.

Political divisions: Peshwa Baji Rao II accepted British protection after his defeat by Yashwantrao Holkar. However, Daulat Rao Scindia and Raghuji II Bhonsle opposed the arrangement, while Holkar initially remained outside their coalition. This prevented united Maratha resistance.

British military strategy: The Company conducted coordinated campaigns under Arthur Wellesley in the Deccan and General Lake in northern India. Victories at Assaye, Delhi, Laswari and Argaon weakened Scindia and Bhonsle.

Territorial consequences: Through the Treaties of Deogaon and Surji-Anjangaon in December 1803, the Company acquired important territories, including Odisha, the Ganga–Yamuna Doab and influence over Delhi.

Limits of British success: Yashwantrao Holkar continued resistance during 1804–1805. His forces inflicted serious losses during Monson's Retreat, while British assaults on Bharatpur failed.

Political settlement: The Treaty of Rajghat in 1805 ended the principal remaining hostilities. Major Maratha houses survived, although their independence and regional influence were weakened.

Thus, the war established British predominance across much of India but did not abolish the Peshwaship or the Maratha ruling houses. Their final major confrontation with the Company occurred during the Third Anglo-Maratha War of 1817–1818.
