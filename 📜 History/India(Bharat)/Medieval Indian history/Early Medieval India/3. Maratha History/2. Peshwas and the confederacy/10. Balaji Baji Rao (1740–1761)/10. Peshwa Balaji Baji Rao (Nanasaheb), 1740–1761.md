Central question: How did the Marathas reach the height of their political influence under Nanasaheb, yet suffer a catastrophic defeat at the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761?

This chapter covers three connected stories:

- 1740–1750: Nanasaheb strengthens the Peshwa's authority.
    
- 1750–1758: Maratha expansion across India and growing rivalry with regional powers.
    
- 1759–1761: Ahmad Shah Abdali's return, the road to Panipat, and the consequences of defeat.

The central lesson is that expanding military influence across a vast region is not the same as being able to administer, finance and defend it permanently.
## 1. India in 1740 — the situation Nanasaheb inherited

In Chapter 9, we studied how Baji Rao I defeated the Nizam at Palkhed, expanded towards Malwa and Bundelkhand, threatened Delhi, and strengthened the Peshwa's position.

When he died on 28 April 1740, the Marathas were no longer confined to Maharashtra.

They possessed important territorial and revenue interests in central and northern India.

However, this expansion also created problems.

### What was Nanasaheb's biggest challenge?

His father had secured influence over territories stretching far beyond the original Maratha kingdom.

But many areas were controlled by powerful local chiefs, not by officers directly supervised from Pune.

Nanasaheb had to solve two problems simultaneously:

Expand Maratha influence while strengthening the administration and finances needed to maintain that expansion.

Stewart Gordon describes this period as a transition from conquest towards administration, while emphasizing the growth of largely autonomous Maratha regional powers.
## 2. Nanasaheb becomes Peshwa (1740)

![File:Shaniwar wada (pune).jpg - Wikimedia Commons](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/yG3-Oo4VynNqbFVSbh_UusQT85yewT0753HkFplW2OwDH60iXZzGC6h1e8c4fKRRxGK5uATP-7-v1IE63raDZyy8OqJ_NeL-WiSO9UdvP6bF9ZKtWIgLa7z5vlnJUcZqp3sHolFMVA2ZTlksK_gsozzkFWfrpn-BJq67GIuBuGM?purpose=inline)

After Baji Rao I's death, a question arose: Who would become the next Peshwa?

His son Balaji Baji Rao, popularly called Nanasaheb, was approximately nineteen years old.

Some influential figures were not enthusiastic about another young member of the Bhat family inheriting the office.

However, Chhatrapati Shahu appointed Nanasaheb as Peshwa in 1740.
### How was Nanasaheb different from Baji Rao I?

| Baji Rao I                                    | Nanasaheb                                                          |
| --------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Famous for rapid cavalry campaigning          | Especially associated with administration and political management |
| Expanded Maratha military influence           | Tried to organize a much larger fiscal and territorial system      |
| Personally led several major expeditions      | Relied extensively on generals, relatives and regional chiefs      |
| Strengthened the Peshwa's military reputation | Greatly increased the Peshwa's practical political authority       |

This is a comparison of their principal historical roles, not a claim that Nanasaheb lacked military experience.
### Was Nanasaheb already king?

No.

Shahu remained the Chhatrapati at Satara.

Nanasaheb was the Peshwa operating principally from Pune.

But during his reign, the balance between royal authority and the Peshwa's government would change dramatically.

## 3. Expansion towards Bengal and Odisha (1741–1751)

![Explore Barabati Fort: Cuttack's Gem | Incredible India](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/Mcw_o3ksdWU7zTTv_jpKUoQkVjJkRbGw_yc86GR5b0ION3VTqD8Cl_sn5NrhzxBHbKF0HLHNwNmxW4VEYaxqfOK10LHJxvoRYSLa51_knrBNfRWkhcWmr--BIdoAaW_JeNN8G3K4VtRBDVd8QIw2FHSh3ExCks_Yr1MwrnGAh1I?purpose=inline)

One of the most important developments during Nanasaheb's reign occurred in eastern India.

But remember an important distinction:

The eastern expansion was principally associated with Raghuji Bhonsle of Nagpur, not simply a direct conquest personally led by Nanasaheb.

### Who was Raghuji Bhonsle?

Raghuji Bhonsle was an influential Maratha chief whose authority was centred on Nagpur and Berar.

He sought additional revenues and territorial influence towards Odisha and Bengal.
### Why did Bengal become a target?

Bengal was an extremely wealthy region with important agricultural production, textiles, inland trade and commercial networks.

In 1740, Alivardi Khan had seized power as Nawab of Bengal after defeating Sarfaraz Khan.

The resulting instability created opportunities for neighbouring military powers.
### The invasions

From 1742 onwards, Maratha forces repeatedly entered Bengal and Odisha.

A commander associated with these attacks was Bhaskar Ram (Bhaskar Pandit).

In Bengali historical memory, the Maratha raiders are often referred to as Bargis.

The invasions caused widespread suffering, disrupted agriculture and commerce, and imposed heavy financial pressure on Alivardi Khan's government.

### Did Nanasaheb and Raghuji always cooperate?

No.

There were disputes over who should receive the revenues and political benefits of the eastern campaigns.

Shahu had to mediate between important Maratha interests.

This illustrates an increasingly serious problem:

Maratha expansion could create rivalry between Maratha commanders themselves.
### Settlement of 1751

After years of warfare, Alivardi Khan reached an arrangement with Raghuji Bhonsle's establishment.

The settlement involved:

- Recognition of Maratha authority in much of Odisha.
    
- A recurring payment associated with Maratha chauth claims in Bengal and Bihar, conventionally recorded as ₹12 lakh annually.
    
- Arrangements concerning Mir Habib, an important participant in the conflict.

The precise implementation was complicated, but Odisha came under Nagpur Bhonsle influence.

UPSC significance: These campaigns demonstrate both the eastward reach of Maratha power and the severe social costs of revenue-driven warfare.

## 4. Death of Chhatrapati Shahu (1749) — a major constitutional turning point

![Satara Tourism (2026) > Tourist Places, Things to Do](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/Uc86De_kGy05SQhSEGkN3QSHrXpQDQM9wKPO65zYalByHUL1wbfHT3LFxxqpPx_zEBb06CARLvfzVwjvO8lDQiVSxjg6-kOOFmuhGtELmR30S0Sme_3EgkdZxZmkSSHA02JCROdblEeD8J8IcFiKMLptx54b-VfY5YzFoBPjDz8?purpose=inline)

This is one of the most important sections of Chapter 10.
### Why did Shahu's death create a problem?

Shahu had no surviving biological son to succeed him.

He had ruled from Satara since 1708 and had long served as an important source of royal legitimacy.

But the Peshwas had become increasingly powerful.

The succession to the Chhatrapati's throne could determine whether the royal court or the Peshwa would dominate future Maratha politics.
### Tarabai returns to the story

Remember Tarabai from Chapters 6 and 7.

She had once resisted Shahu, and later she had been displaced from power in the Kolhapur establishment.

By the 1740s, she again became influential in dynastic politics.

She introduced a young man whom she represented as a grandson of Rajaram.

This person became known as Rajaram II or Ramraja.
### Was Ramraja definitely Rajaram's grandson?

His ancestry was disputed.

Tarabai later repudiated his royal identity during a renewed conflict with the Peshwa.

For UPSC, remember that his contested legitimacy was central to the power struggle, rather than treating every dynastic claim as independently proven.
### What did Shahu do?

Shahu accepted arrangements for Ramraja's succession.

He died on 15 December 1749.

Rajaram II subsequently became Chhatrapati.
### Why was this important?

The new Chhatrapati lacked Shahu's political authority and independent support.

Nanasaheb, who controlled substantial military and administrative resources, was now positioned to become the principal effective ruler.

The institution of the Chhatrapati survived, but practical political power increasingly shifted towards the Peshwa.
## 5. The Sangola settlement (1750) — power shifts from Satara to Pune

### What was the Sangola settlement?

In 1750, political arrangements commonly associated with the Sangola settlement further concentrated executive authority in the Peshwa's hands.

The Chhatrapati retained dynastic and ceremonial significance, but decisions concerning major affairs increasingly came from the Peshwa's government.
### Why did this happen?

Three reasons are important.

1. Weak royal succession: Ramraja lacked Shahu's authority and military support.

2. Peshwa resources: Nanasaheb commanded extensive administrative, financial and military networks.

3. Powerful regional chiefs: The system needed an effective political centre capable of coordinating distant commanders.

### What happened to Tarabai?

Tarabai later clashed with Nanasaheb and Ramraja.

In 1750, she detained Ramraja, and the dispute contributed to further political conflict.
### Did the Maratha monarchy end in 1750?

No.

The Chhatrapati remained an important source of formal legitimacy.

But real control over major military and governmental affairs was increasingly exercised by the Peshwa.

UPSC distinction: The Sangola settlement represented a major shift in effective power, not the abolition of the Chhatrapati's office.

## 6. Growth of the Maratha Confederacy

During Nanasaheb's reign, the Marathas exercised influence across many regions, but they did not govern everything through a single uniform administration.

### Five principal centres

| House             | Principal centre     | Historical role                                   |
| ----------------- | -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Peshwa            | Pune                 | Principal political and administrative leadership |
| Scindia / Shinde  | Malwa, later Gwalior | Northern and central Indian campaigns             |
| Holkar            | Indore / Malwa       | Military and revenue interests in central India   |
| Gaekwad           | Baroda / Gujarat     | Expansion and consolidation in Gujarat            |
| Bhonsle of Nagpur | Nagpur / Berar       | Expansion into Odisha and eastern India           |
### Why did these houses become powerful?

Maratha commanders needed resources to maintain cavalry, infantry and administrative establishments.

They often acquired rights to collect revenues from particular regions.

Gradually, some commanders developed hereditary political and military establishments.
### Strengths of the system

- Able to mobilize forces in distant regions.
    
- Local commanders understood regional politics.
    
- Territorial expansion was not dependent on one army.
    
- Military and revenue networks could survive setbacks.
### Weaknesses

- Chiefs competed over revenue rights.
    
- The Peshwa could not always compel cooperation.
    
- Armies sometimes operated with conflicting objectives.
    
- Distant territories were costly to defend.
    
- Regional rulers could negotiate separately with powerful Maratha chiefs.

UPSC Mains concept: The decentralized structure made expansion possible but complicated the establishment of a coordinated empire-wide fiscal and military system.

## 7. Conflict with Hyderabad and the Battle of Udgir (1760)

![Udgir Fort, Maharashtra, India.](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/j6xrunDM2PU-YEaAG0V_rcr-KOcZtbmlUyApzOIushfXZQ0miVof_KygScBkh3hOv01w7ZOsJWDwvtzmuIdYBKZWFwIX-RJXFHm7WZGMiKu2TjDOCM_j5OJiYb9dA4zLsJjOskd6on9GqjBj9dLzyCvXiZm3XBLniA-kR9nBByBy7WNQRXjEYryJo45yslzJ?purpose=inline)

### Why did conflict with Hyderabad continue?

The Nizam and Marathas had overlapping claims over Deccan territories and revenues.

The first Nizam, Asaf Jah I, died in 1748.

Succession disputes followed among members of his family.

The Marathas intervened in regional conflicts to strengthen their position.

### Role of the French

French military officers, especially Marquis de Bussy, became influential in Hyderabad politics during the 1750s.

This made the Deccan political situation more complex.

European companies were no longer only commercial actors: their trained troops and artillery were increasingly important in Indian regional warfare.
### Battle of Udgir — February 1760

A major confrontation occurred between Maratha forces led principally by **Sadashivrao Bhau** and the forces of Nizam Ali Khan.

The Marathas won a decisive victory.
### What did the Marathas gain?

The subsequent settlement involved extensive territorial and financial concessions by the Nizam's establishment.

These substantially strengthened the Maratha position in the Deccan.

### Why is Udgir important?

It took place only a few months before Sadashivrao Bhau led the northern expedition that ended at Panipat.

The Maratha military leadership had just achieved a major success in the Deccan.

Yet the northern campaign presented very different geographical and logistical problems.

Prelims trap: Udgir (1760) was fought against the Hyderabad/Nizam forces, not Ahmad Shah Abdali.

## 8. The Mughal political crisis and Ahmad Shah Abdali

To understand Panipat, we need to return to northern India.

### What happened after Nadir Shah's invasion in 1739?

Nadir Shah's invasion had demonstrated how vulnerable the Mughal Empire had become.

The empire still had an emperor, but major provinces operated with substantial autonomy.

Different powers now competed to influence Delhi:

- Maratha chiefs.
    
- Rohilla Afghan leaders.
    
- Awadh's ruling establishment.
    
- Mughal nobles.
    
- The expanding Durrani state based in Afghanistan.
    

### Who was Ahmad Shah Abdali?

Ahmad Shah Abdali, also known as Ahmad Shah Durrani, founded the Durrani Empire in 1747.

He established his principal power base in Afghanistan and repeatedly intervened in northwestern India.

### Why did Abdali want influence in India?

The Punjab and adjoining territories were economically and strategically valuable.

They provided agricultural revenues, trade routes and access between Afghanistan and northern India.

Abdali also sought to preserve his political influence over territories formerly associated with Mughal authority.

### Key events

|Year|Event|
|---|---|
|1747|Ahmad Shah Durrani established his empire|
|1748|First major Indian invasion; Mughal forces checked him near Manupur|
|1752|Mughal authorities conceded important Punjab claims to Abdali|
|1757|Abdali invaded Delhi and the surrounding region|
|1758|Maratha forces expelled Afghan representatives from much of Punjab|
|1759|Abdali returned to recover lost influence|
|1761|Third Battle of Panipat|

### Why did conflict with the Marathas become inevitable?

The Marathas wanted to expand northward and influence Mughal imperial politics.

Abdali sought to preserve his authority over Punjab and northwestern India.

Both powers were competing for influence over the same broad region.

Their collision was intensified by alliances with local Indian chiefs.

## 9. Maratha expansion into Punjab (1757–1758)

![Alamgiri Gate in Lahore fort, Punjab province, Pakistan](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/dWywnyWkVTvzjQH_9HA-fP-vJlULY78To6U77vfNAciEr-7DqLhV6thun3nZFRqklwpjpzoKdzHzVFKJurAaMDkPtt6tt6p3v33xg7QIM1s1KIqRj2LXazr1yRBUJD948NhCRKhv8bkIrUozWl4oF9YLMw4RQsgdOmaVUQ1q9AZ_cUE2yLPcwZli-WCaV49D?purpose=inline)

### Why did Maratha forces move into Punjab?

Maratha leaders were concerned about Abdali's growing influence over Delhi and Punjab.

They also wanted to expand their political and fiscal interests in northern India.

### Who led the campaign?

The important Maratha commanders included:

- Raghunathrao, younger brother of Nanasaheb.
    
- Malhar Rao Holkar.
    
- Other Maratha commanders working alongside local allies.
    

The regional official Adina Beg Khan and Sikh forces played important roles in the anti-Afghan operations.

### What happened?

In 1758, Maratha forces advanced through Punjab.

They expelled Afghan representatives from Lahore and other positions.

Maratha forces or allied contingents also extended operations towards Attock and Peshawar, although the extent and durability of control over the far northwestern frontier are debated.

### Did the Marathas permanently conquer Afghanistan?

No.

Punjab was contested, and the Maratha forward positions near Attock and Peshawar were temporary.

These campaigns did not establish stable Maratha rule over Afghanistan or Kandahar.

### Why was the expansion risky?

Punjab lay far from Pune.

Maintaining control required:

- A large permanent military presence.
    
- Financial resources.
    
- Cooperation from local allies.
    
- Defence of major river crossings.
    
- Reliable communications with Delhi and the Deccan.
    

The Marathas had achieved rapid territorial gains, but maintaining them was much harder.

After Adina Beg's death in September 1758, the northern settlement became increasingly fragile.

## 10. Abdali returns in 1759 — the road to Panipat

Ahmad Shah Abdali was unwilling to accept the loss of his influence over Punjab.

In 1759, he returned with a major army.

### Who supported Abdali?

One of his most important Indian allies was Najib-ud-Daula, a Rohilla Afghan chief.

Other Rohilla leaders supported the Afghan coalition.

Abdali also sought cooperation from Shuja-ud-Daula, the Nawab of Awadh, who eventually joined his side.

### Why did some Indian rulers oppose the Marathas?

Not every regional ruler welcomed Maratha expansion.

Some objected to revenue demands, previous military interventions or the growing influence of Maratha commanders.

Others wanted to preserve their own political autonomy.

Religious appeals played a part in mobilization, but the alliances also reflected political interests, revenue disputes and regional rivalries.

Suraj Mal, ruler of the Jat state of Bharatpur, was a powerful regional figure.

## 13. Marathas capture Delhi (August 1760)

Bhau's army advanced north from the Deccan.

In August 1760, Maratha forces occupied Delhi.

### Was this a major achievement?

Yes.

Delhi was the symbolic centre of the Mughal Empire.

Its occupation strengthened the Marathas' political position.

### Did capturing Delhi defeat Abdali?

No.

Abdali's principal army remained active.

Possession of Delhi did not solve the Marathas' supply problems or guarantee support from neighbouring rulers.

### Why not retreat to the Deccan immediately?

The Maratha leadership was trying to maintain its northern political position and force a favourable settlement.

Abandoning Delhi and Punjab would have undermined years of expansion.

But remaining in the region increased dependence on long and vulnerable supply routes.

This created a difficult strategic dilemma.

In October 1760, Bhau's army attacked Kunjpura, an important Afghan-held fortified position north of Delhi.

The Marathas captured Kunjpura and defeated its defending forces.

### Why was this successful?

The operation provided supplies and demonstrated the fighting capabilities of Bhau's army.

However, the victory did not eliminate Abdali's main force.

### What did Abdali do next?

Abdali managed to cross the Yamuna River downstream, near the Baghpat area, in late October.

Abdali's forces were better positioned to disrupt the movement of food and supplies to the Maratha camp.

## 15. The siege-like confrontation at Panipat (October 1760–January 1761)

The Third Battle of Panipat did not begin suddenly on 14 January.

The armies had already spent weeks confronting one another.

### The Maratha problem

The army needed food, fodder and financial resources.

As supplies became increasingly scarce:

- Horses became difficult to maintain.
    
- Soldiers experienced hunger and exhaustion.
    
- The camp followers suffered severely.
    
- Attempts to obtain supplies were increasingly dangerous.
    
- Continued waiting became unsustainable.
    

### The Afghan advantage

Abdali's coalition was able to limit Maratha access to food and resources.

It also maintained connections with supporting forces in northern India.

### Why did Bhau decide to fight?

By January 1761, the Maratha leadership faced an increasingly desperate situation.

Remaining in camp threatened further starvation and deterioration.

A major engagement offered a chance to break the enemy's position and move towards supplies.

The decision was not simply the result of personal overconfidence. It was made under severe logistical pressure.

### Where did the battle occur?

Near Panipat, in present-day Haryana, north of Delhi.

### Who fought?

|Maratha side|Afghan coalition|
|---|---|
|Sadashivrao Bhau|Ahmad Shah Abdali / Durrani|

|Shamsher Bahadur and other chiefs|Afghan cavalry and allied Indian forces|

### How did the battle unfold?

1. Maratha advance
    
    On the morning of 14 January, the Marathas moved to engage Abdali's coalition.
    
2. Effective infantry and artillery fighting
    
    Ibrahim Khan Gardi's disciplined troops inflicted significant losses on opposing forces, particularly on sections of the Afghan coalition's flank.
    
3. Intense central fighting
    
    Bhau's principal forces engaged the Afghan centre. At points the Marathas achieved important tactical successes.
    
4. Vishwasrao killed
    
    The death of Vishwasrao damaged Maratha morale and command confidence during the battle.
    
5. Afghan reserves and counterattacks
    
    Abdali committed additional forces and exploited weaknesses developing in the Maratha lines.
    
6. Collapse of Maratha resistance
    
    The exhausted Maratha army broke under sustained attack. Sadashivrao Bhau was killed, and many commanders and soldiers died or were captured.
    

### Result

Ahmad Shah Abdali won a decisive battlefield victory.

The Marathas suffered catastrophic military and human losses.

Large numbers of soldiers, camp followers and captives died during and after the battle.

Precise casualty estimates are disputed and should not be memorized as unquestionably exact figures.

### Was religion the sole cause of the conflict?

No.

Religion was used in political mobilization, and some participants framed the conflict in religious terms.

But alliances did not follow simple religious lines.

For example, Ibrahim Khan Gardi, a Muslim commander, fought for the Marathas, while Shuja-ud-Daula, another Muslim ruler, sided with Abdali for political reasons. Hindu-ruled regional states also pursued their own interests rather than automatically joining the Marathas.

## 18. Consequences of the Third Battle of Panipat

### Immediate consequences

1. Loss of senior leaders

Sadashivrao Bhau, Vishwasrao, Jankoji Scindia and many important Maratha officers were killed or lost in the aftermath.

Shamsher Bahadur was severely wounded and died soon afterward.

2. Military weakening

The Maratha field army suffered enormous losses in manpower, equipment and experienced leadership.

3. Loss of northern influence

Maratha authority in northern India experienced a serious setback.

4. Political uncertainty

Regional Maratha chiefs increasingly needed to protect their own territories and resources.

5. Human suffering

The battle and its aftermath caused deaths, displacement, captivity and extensive suffering among combatants and non-combatants.

### Did Abdali become emperor of India?

No.

Abdali defeated the Marathas, but he did not replace the Mughal dynasty with permanent Durrani government over all northern India.

He eventually withdrew towards Afghanistan.

### Did Panipat permanently destroy the Maratha Empire?

No.

This is one of the most important UPSC distinctions.

The Marathas suffered a major defeat but subsequently recovered much of their influence under Madhavrao I and later military leaders.

They re-established a significant position in northern India during the 1770s.

Historical significance: Panipat interrupted Maratha expansion and exposed its structural weaknesses, but it was not the final end of Maratha political power.

## 22. UPSC Mains — model answer

Question: “The Third Battle of Panipat represented the limits of Maratha expansion rather than the permanent destruction of Maratha power.” Examine. (250 words)

The Third Battle of Panipat, fought on 14 January 1761 between the Marathas and Ahmad Shah Abdali's coalition, was a major turning point in eighteenth-century Indian history.

Under Nanasaheb Peshwa, Maratha influence expanded into central, northern and eastern India. The occupation of Punjab in 1758 demonstrated their military reach but also created a direct confrontation with Abdali.

Limits of expansion: The Marathas struggled to maintain secure supply lines and permanent garrisons across distant northern territories. Their relations with major regional powers were also strained by earlier interventions and revenue demands.

Diplomatic failures: The Maratha leadership could not secure dependable support from important northern powers, including Bharatpur and Awadh. Abdali, by contrast, built an effective coalition with Rohilla leaders and Shuja-ud-Daula.

Military difficulties: Sadashivrao Bhau's army included substantial infantry and artillery but suffered from serious logistical problems. The prolonged confrontation near Panipat caused food shortages, weakened horses and exhausted soldiers.

Consequences: Abdali's victory caused catastrophic losses and temporarily weakened Maratha influence over northern India. The death of Nanasaheb in June 1761 deepened the leadership crisis.

However, the Maratha political system survived. Under Madhavrao I and subsequent commanders, Maratha power recovered substantially, including renewed influence over Delhi.

Thus, Panipat revealed the difficulties of translating rapid military expansion into durable territorial administration, but did not mark the permanent disappearance of Maratha power.
