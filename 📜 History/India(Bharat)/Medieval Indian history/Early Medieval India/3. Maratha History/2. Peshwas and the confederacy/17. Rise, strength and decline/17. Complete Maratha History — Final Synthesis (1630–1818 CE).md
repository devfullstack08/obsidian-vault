**Beginning:** Shivaji Maharaj's birth in 1630 and the rise of Swarajya.
**Major transition:** Rise of the Peshwas after 1713.
**Greatest expansion**: During the eighteenth century, especially under Baji Rao I and Nanasaheb.
**Major setback:** Third Battle of Panipat, 1761.
**Political end**: Abolition of the Peshwaship in 1818.

This is the final revision chapter of our 17-chapter Maratha history series.

Rather than repeating every event in full, we will connect the entire story through chronology, family succession, political changes, military strategy, geography, battles, treaties and causes of expansion and decline.

## 1. The entire Maratha story in one view

1. 1630–1680 — Rise of Shivaji and Swarajya
    
    Fort acquisitions, conflict with Bijapur and Mughals, coronation in 1674 and creation of a sovereign kingdom.
    
2. 1680–1707 — War of survival
    
    Sambhaji, Rajaram and Tarabai resisted Aurangzeb despite losing kings and major fortresses.
    
3. 1707–1740 — Rise of Shahu and the Peshwas
    
    Shahu's succession struggle, Balaji Vishwanath's diplomacy and Baji Rao I's expansion.
    
4. 1740–1761 — Height of Maratha expansion
    
    Nanasaheb's administration, influence across northern India, followed by the defeat at Panipat.
    
5. 1761–1782 — Recovery and internal crisis
    
    Madhavrao I restored Maratha strength, but succession conflicts opened the way for the First Anglo-Maratha War.
    
6. 1782–1802 — Confederacy politics
    
    Nana Fadnavis, Mahadji Scindia, regional rivalries and Baji Rao II's Treaty of Bassein.
    
7. 1803–1818 — British supremacy
    
    The Second and Third Anglo-Maratha Wars weakened the major houses and ended the Peshwaship.
    

Master memory chain:
Shivaji → Sambhaji → Rajaram → Tarabai → Shahu → Balaji Vishwanath → Baji Rao I → Nanasaheb → Madhavrao I → Succession crisis → Anglo-Maratha Wars → 1818.

## 2. Historical background — why did the Marathas emerge?

Before Shivaji, much of the Deccan was contested between the Mughal Empire and the Deccan Sultanates, especially Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda.

### Important background personalities

| Personality     | Role                                                                   |
| --------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Malik Ambar     | Ahmadnagar statesman and military leader who resisted Mughal expansion |
| Shahaji Bhonsle | Shivaji's father; served different Deccan powers                       |
| Jijabai         | Shivaji's mother; influential in his upbringing                        |
| Dadoji Konddev  | Associated with administration of the Pune jagir                       |
| Aurangzeb       | Mughal prince, later emperor, whose Deccan wars shaped Maratha history |
### Why was the geography important?

The Western Ghats, Maval hills, Konkan coast and Deccan plateau were central to Maratha military and political development.

They provided:

- Fortified hill positions.
    
- Difficult passes.
    
- Opportunities for mobile warfare.
    
- Coastal routes and maritime trade.
    
- Local military and revenue networks.

The Marathas did not become powerful through terrain alone. They also developed effective military organization, fort administration, local alliances and revenue systems.

### Why was Shivaji historically significant?

He built a durable political system through the combination of forts, mobile warfare, revenue administration, military discipline and royal authority.

His coronation in 1674 formally asserted sovereign kingship.

His southern campaigns also had long-term consequences: Gingee became a refuge for Rajaram in 1689.

### Three events to distinguish

Pratapgad (1659): Victory over Afzal Khan and Bijapur's forces.

Purandar (1665): Treaty with the Mughal commander Jai Singh I.

Raigad (1674): Coronation of Shivaji.

## 4. Sambhaji, Rajaram and Tarabai — why did Aurangzeb fail?

The period 1680–1707 was a struggle for survival.
### Sambhaji (1680–1689)

After Shivaji's death, Sambhaji overcame a succession dispute involving his younger half-brother Rajaram.

Aurangzeb personally moved south in 1681 and eventually conquered Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687).

In February 1689, Sambhaji was captured at Sangameshwar.

He was executed in March 1689.
### Rajaram (1689–1700)

After Sambhaji's death, Rajaram assumed leadership.

The Mughals captured Raigad in November 1689, and Sambhaji's widow Yesubai and son Shahu were taken captive.

But Rajaram escaped to Gingee in Tamil Nadu, where he established an alternative royal centre.

Commanders such as Santaji Ghorpade and Dhanaji Jadhav continued military resistance.

The Mughals captured Gingee in January 1698, but Rajaram had already escaped.

He died at Sinhagad in March 1700.
### Tarabai (1700–1707)

Tarabai governed as regent for her son Shivaji II.

She maintained Maratha military resistance, coordinated commanders and supported raids into Mughal territories.

Aurangzeb died on 3 March 1707 without permanently suppressing the Marathas.

### Why did the Marathas survive?

Royal leadership survived beyond the loss of individual rulers.

They also possessed decentralized military networks, strongholds and mobile forces that made complete conquest difficult.
## 5. Shahu and the rise of the Peshwas (1707–1740)

### The succession problem after Aurangzeb's death

The Mughals released Shahu, Sambhaji's son, in 1707.

He claimed the Maratha throne, but Tarabai defended the rights of her son Shivaji II.

At the Battle of Khed (1707), the shift in allegiance of Dhanaji Jadhav helped Shahu prevail.

Shahu established his principal court at Satara in 1708.

The rivalry with the Kolhapur branch continued.

### What changed during these years?

Under Shivaji, the Chhatrapati had been the principal sovereign authority.

Under Shahu, the Peshwa increasingly coordinated administration, finance, military campaigns and diplomacy.

The change was gradual—not a single transfer of all royal powers in 1713.

## 6. Nanasaheb, Panipat and Madhavrao's recovery (1740–1772)

### Nanasaheb Peshwa (1740–1761)

Nanasaheb inherited a much larger political system.

Maratha military and revenue interests extended into Malwa, Gujarat, Bundelkhand, Odisha and northern India.

But a larger sphere of influence required greater financial resources and cooperation from regional chiefs.

In 1749, Chhatrapati Shahu died.

The succession of Rajaram II (Ramraja) and the political arrangements associated with Sangola (1750) further strengthened the Peshwa's executive position.

### The Third Battle of Panipat (1761)

| Feature                   | Details                                                       |
| ------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Date                      | 14 January 1761                                               |
| Maratha commander         | Sadashivrao Bhau                                              |
| Afghan commander          | Ahmad Shah Abdali (Durrani)                                   |
| Important Maratha leaders | Vishwasrao, Ibrahim Khan Gardi, Jankoji Scindia               |
| Important Afghan allies   | Najib-ud-Daula, Shuja-ud-Daula                                |
| Result                    | Decisive Afghan victory                                       |
| Long-term effect          | Major setback, but not permanent destruction of Maratha power |

### Why did the Marathas lose?

The main factors were distant supply lines, inadequate dependable regional alliances, serious shortages, the expense of a large army and camp, and the effectiveness of Abdali's coalition.

Panipat revealed the limits of rapid expansion without secure logistical and political support.

### Madhavrao I (1761–1772)

Madhavrao inherited a weakened government but rebuilt much of its strength.

|Year|Achievement|
|---|---|
|1762|Initially defeated politically and militarily by his uncle Raghunathrao at Alegaon|
|1763|Defeated the Nizam at Rakshasbhuvan|
|1764–1772|Conducted or directed campaigns against Haidar Ali of Mysore|
|1768|Overcame Raghunathrao's renewed challenge|
|1771|Maratha forces recovered Delhi|
|1772|Shah Alam II returned to Delhi under Maratha protection|

Madhavrao died in November 1772.

The Marathas had substantially recovered, but continued to depend on powerful regional chiefs and individual leadership.

## 7. From succession crisis to the Anglo-Maratha Wars (1772–1818)

### Narayanrao and the Barbhai Council

After Madhavrao I's death:

- Narayanrao became Peshwa in 1772.
    
- He was murdered on 30 August 1773.
    
- Raghunathrao, his uncle, claimed the Peshwaship.
    
- Nana Fadnavis and the Barbhai Council opposed Raghunathrao.
    
- Narayanrao's posthumous son Madhavrao II was born in 1774 and recognized as Peshwa.
    
- Raghunathrao sought British help through the Treaty of Surat (1775).

This brought the Company into the dispute.

### First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782)

The Pune government resisted British intervention.

The British expedition suffered a major defeat at Wadgaon (1779).

Other British operations achieved successes in Gujarat and Malwa, but the conflict ended through the Treaty of Salbai (1782).

The Maratha government retained its independence.

### Nana Fadnavis and Mahadji Scindia (1782–1802)

Nana Fadnavis exercised considerable influence at Pune.

Mahadji Scindia strengthened Maratha power in northern India, developed a professional army and became a leading protector of Mughal emperor Shah Alam II.

The combined Maratha forces defeated Hyderabad at Kharda (1795).

But the death of Madhavrao II in 1795 triggered another succession crisis.

Baji Rao II became Peshwa in 1796.

After Nana Fadnavis died in 1800, rivalry between Scindia and Holkar intensified.

Yashwantrao Holkar defeated Baji Rao II and Scindia's forces near Pune in October 1802.

The Peshwa fled and signed the Treaty of Bassein on 31 December 1802.

### Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805)

Scindia and the Nagpur Bhonsles opposed the Company's expanded influence.

British victories included:

- Assaye (1803) under Arthur Wellesley.
    
- Delhi and Laswari (1803) under General Lake.
    
- Argaon (1803) under Arthur Wellesley.

Treaties with Scindia and Bhonsle led to major territorial concessions.

Holkar continued resistance into 1805, including the British disaster of Monson's Retreat in 1804.

### Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818)

Baji Rao II increasingly resisted British restrictions.

The Pindari campaign and the Treaty of Poona (1817) intensified the political crisis.

Major events:

|Date|Event|
|---|---|
|5 November 1817|Khadki — British defeated the Peshwa's forces|
|26–27 November 1817|Sitabuldi — Nagpur Bhonsle forces defeated|
|21 December 1817|Mahidpur — Holkar forces defeated|
|1 January 1818|Koregaon Bhima — Company detachment defended its position against Peshwa forces|
|February 1818|Ashti — Bapu Gokhale killed|
|3 June 1818|Baji Rao II surrendered|
|1818|Peshwaship abolished|

The Company annexed much of the Peshwa's territory, while several Maratha ruling houses survived as princely states under British paramountcy.

NCERT identifies the three Anglo-Maratha Wars as central to the Company's eventual supremacy over the Maratha political system.

### Important succession corrections

- Shahu I was Shivaji's grandson, not son.
    
- Rajaram was Sambhaji's younger half-brother, not son.
    
- Tarabai was Rajaram's wife, not Sambhaji's wife.
    
- Shivaji II of Kolhapur was Tarabai's son.
    
- Sambhaji II of Kolhapur was Rajasbai's son.
    
- Rajaram II (Ramraja) of Satara, who followed Shahu in 1749, was a different person from Shivaji's son Rajaram, who died in 1700. His claimed ancestry was disputed.
    
### Important note about royal numbering

Historical books do not always use exactly the same numbering for rulers named Shivaji, Shahu and Rajaram, especially across the Satara and Kolhapur branches.

For UPSC, identify rulers using their parents, branch, and reign—not merely their Roman numeral.

## 10. How Maratha administration evolved

### A. Shivaji's Ashta Pradhan system

| Office                  | Principal responsibility                  |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Peshwa / Mukhya Pradhan | Chief minister and general administration |
| Amatya / Mazumdar       | Finance and accounts                      |
| Sachiv / Surnavis       | Official correspondence and records       |
| Mantri / Waqia-navis    | Court intelligence and records of events  |
| Senapati / Sari-naubat  | Military command                          |
| Sumant / Dabir          | Diplomacy and external affairs            |
| Nyayadhish              | Justice                                   |
| Panditrao               | Religious and charitable affairs          |

The offices did not operate like eight modern ministries with rigidly separated responsibilities. Duties and influence could overlap and changed with circumstances.

### B. Local administration

| Term           | Meaning                                                                      |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Prant          | Larger territorial administrative division                                   |
| Pargana / Tarf | Intermediate local divisions                                                 |
| Patil          | Important village headman                                                    |
| Kulkarni       | Village record keeper and accountant                                         |
| Deshmukh       | Hereditary local authority associated with revenue and landholding interests |
| Deshpande      | Hereditary district-level record or accounting function                      |

### C. Revenue

|Term|Meaning|
|---|---|
|Swarajya|Own territory under Maratha political authority|
|Chauth|Nominal one-fourth revenue claim|
|Sardeshmukhi|Additional one-tenth revenue claim|
|Saranjam|Assignment of revenue or territory to support service, often military|
|Watan|Hereditary local right or office|
|Jagir|Revenue assignment associated with service or status|

Critical distinction: Chauth and sardeshmukhi claims over a Mughal province did not necessarily make the province part of directly administered Maratha territory.

The formal imperial grants of 1719 recognized important claims over the six Mughal Deccan provinces. The actual amount collected often differed from the nominal entitlement.

### E. Three stages of political administration

Stage 1 — Shivaji's monarchy (1674–1680)

Strong personal kingship supported by a council, forts, revenue officers and the army.

Stage 2 — Wartime decentralization and Peshwa rise (1689–1750)

Regional officers and commanders gained influence; the Peshwa became central to coordinating expansion.

Stage 3 — Confederacy (mid/late 18th century)

Peshwa's government at Pune and powerful regional houses maintained overlapping obligations and substantial autonomy.

### Why were the Narmada and Western Ghats important?

The Western Ghats shaped access between the Konkan and the Deccan and made fort defence and mobile operations important.

The Narmada region connected the Deccan with central India. Malwa and its routes provided a strategic avenue for Maratha northern campaigns.

### Three treaties that changed everything

Purandar, 1665: Shivaji had to compromise with Mughal power.

Salbai, 1782: The Company still had to negotiate with the Marathas as a powerful opponent.

Bassein, 1802: The Peshwa accepted major dependence on Company military protection.

The contrast shows the transformation of Maratha relations with larger powers across nearly 140 years.

### The three greatest transformations

First transformation — Shivaji: From a regional military power to a sovereign kingdom.

Second transformation — Balaji Vishwanath and Baji Rao I: From a kingdom concentrated in the Deccan to an expanding, Peshwa-coordinated political power.

Third transformation — later eighteenth century: From a dominant Peshwa government to a confederacy of powerful regional establishments.

## 15. Why did Maratha political power decline?

It is too simplistic to say that the Marathas declined because of one battle or one ruler.

The decline occurred through several interacting problems.

### A. Internal rivalries

Conflicts occurred between:

- Shahu and Tarabai.
    
- Madhavrao I and Raghunathrao.
    
- Raghunathrao and the Barbhai Council.
    
- Nana Fadnavis and competing court factions.
    
- Scindia and Holkar.
    
- Baji Rao II and Yashwantrao Holkar.
    

These divisions diverted resources and opened opportunities for outsiders.

### B. Political decentralization

The Maratha system relied heavily on regional houses with their own forces and revenue interests.

This enabled expansion but sometimes obstructed common military and diplomatic policy.

### C. Inadequate coordination across large territories

A campaign in Punjab required different logistical arrangements from one in the Deccan.

Rapid territorial acquisition did not guarantee secure garrisons, loyal regional allies or dependable revenue collection.

### D. Financial and military pressures

Maintaining professional infantry, artillery, cavalry and numerous forts required large revenues.

Conflict over revenue assignments could itself generate internal rivalry.

### E. British military and political expansion

The Company possessed increasingly large fiscal resources and professional forces.

It also employed subsidiary alliances, Residents, diplomacy and separate treaties to restrict the political independence of regional rulers.

### F. The Treaty of Bassein

The Peshwa's acceptance of British protection in 1802 was a particularly important political turning point.

It weakened the ability of the principal Maratha office to coordinate the Confederacy independently.

### The causal sequence

Rapid expansion across India

Powerful regional military houses

Competition over revenues, territories and appointments

Reduced coordination in foreign relations and war

British exploitation of political divisions and military campaigns

1818 — End of independent Peshwa rule

This is an explanatory framework, not an inevitable sequence. The outcome depended on particular decisions, military events, alliances and wider political conditions.

UPSC Mains conclusion: Internal fragmentation weakened Maratha coordination, but British imperial policy and the Company's growing military and financial strength were also central to the outcome.

## 19. Final UPSC Mains model answer

Question: Trace the rise, expansion and decline of Maratha power from Shivaji to the abolition of the Peshwaship in 1818. (250 words)

The emergence of Maratha power under Shivaji in the seventeenth century marked an important transformation in the political history of the Deccan.

Rise under Shivaji: Shivaji developed a sovereign kingdom through fort networks, mobile warfare, revenue administration and local alliances. His coronation at Raigad in 1674 established formal royal authority.

Survival against the Mughals: Following Shivaji's death, Sambhaji, Rajaram and Tarabai maintained resistance against Aurangzeb. Decentralized command and military mobility helped the Marathas survive despite major territorial losses.

Expansion under the Peshwas: After Shahu's accession, Balaji Vishwanath strengthened the Peshwa's position and secured important imperial revenue grants in 1719. Baji Rao I expanded Maratha influence into Malwa, Bundelkhand and northern India. Under Nanasaheb, this influence reached Punjab before the defeat at Panipat in 1761.

Recovery: Madhavrao I rebuilt Maratha military strength, defeated regional opponents and restored influence over Delhi.

Decline: Subsequent succession disputes and rivalries among powerful Maratha houses weakened political coordination. The British East India Company exploited these divisions through subsidiary alliances, diplomacy and military campaigns.

The Treaty of Bassein (1802) reduced the Peshwa's autonomy. The Second Anglo-Maratha War weakened Scindia and Bhonsle, while the Third War ended with Baji Rao II's surrender and the abolition of the Peshwaship in 1818.

Thus, Maratha history illustrates the achievements of regional state formation and expansion, alongside the challenges of governing a decentralized political system in the face of growing colonial power.

## Final conclusion

The complete story can be understood through four decisive turning points:

1674 — Shivaji's coronation: Formation of a sovereign Maratha kingdom.

1713–1720 — Rise of the Peshwas: Administrative and political leadership increasingly shifted towards the Peshwa's office.

1761 — Third Battle of Panipat: A devastating military setback that exposed the limits of expansion, but did not end Maratha power.

1818 — Abolition of the Peshwaship: British victory fundamentally ended the independent Peshwa-led political system.

The most important lesson from the entire 1630–1818 period is that the Marathas developed from a regional kingdom into one of the major powers of eighteenth-century India, but their long-term fortunes depended on the ability to coordinate military strength, finance, political legitimacy and regional alliances.
