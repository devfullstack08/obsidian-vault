**Period:** 1817–1818
**Last Peshwa:** Baji Rao II
**British Governor-General:** Lord Hastings (Francis Rawdon-Hastings)
**British Resident at Pune:** Mountstuart Elphinstone
**Important battles:** Khadki, Sitabuldi, Mahidpur, Koregaon, Ashti
**Outcome:** Peshwaship abolished in 1818

Central question: How did the Maratha political system, which had survived Aurangzeb and recovered after Panipat, finally lose its principal political centre to the British East India Company in 1818?

This chapter explains the final war of the Peshwa government, the resistance offered by other Maratha houses, and why the abolition of the Peshwaship did not mean that every Maratha princely house disappeared.

## 1. Where did Chapter 15 end?

In Chapter 15, we studied the Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805).

The war developed after Baji Rao II signed the Treaty of Bassein on 31 December 1802.

Major events included:

- 1803: Arthur Wellesley defeated Maratha forces at Assaye and Argaon.
    
- 1803: General Lake captured Delhi and defeated Scindia at Laswari.
    
- 1803: Scindia and Nagpur Bhonsle signed separate peace treaties.
    
- 1804: Yashwantrao Holkar continued fighting and inflicted serious losses during Monson's Retreat.
    
- 1805: British attacks on Bharatpur failed; Holkar subsequently made peace.

### What was the situation after 1805?

The British had acquired a dominant political position across extensive parts of India.

But major Maratha ruling houses survived.

|Maratha power|Position after the Second War|
|---|---|
|Peshwa at Pune|Baji Rao II remained in office, dependent on British protection|
|Scindia of Gwalior|Retained substantial territories but lost important northern possessions|
|Holkar of Indore|Retained a significant political and military establishment|
|Bhonsle of Nagpur|Continued to govern Nagpur and surrounding territories|
|Gaekwad of Baroda|Maintained a separate state under increasing British influence|
|Chhatrapati at Satara|Retained dynastic legitimacy but little independent executive power|

The Company had weakened the Maratha system, but had not yet abolished the Peshwa's office.

## 2. India around 1815 — why did another war become possible?

### The British position

By 1815, the Company possessed large armies and extensive revenue resources.

Its influence extended across Bengal, the Deccan, Delhi and several princely states.

### The Peshwa's position

Baji Rao II was dissatisfied with the restrictions imposed by the Treaty of Bassein.

British troops were stationed near Pune, and the British Resident exercised considerable diplomatic influence.

The Peshwa could not independently conduct major foreign relations or rebuild his regional supremacy without risking conflict with the Company.

### Why was Baji Rao dissatisfied?

He wanted to recover the political authority previously associated with the Peshwa.

The treaty had helped restore him to power in 1803, but it also reduced his autonomy.

The central contradiction: Baji Rao II owed his restoration to British military assistance, yet increasingly resented the dependence that followed.

## 3. Trimbakji Dengle — the Peshwa's influential adviser

### Who was Trimbakji Dengle?

Trimbakji Dengle (Trimbakji Dengale) was a close adviser and political associate of Baji Rao II.

He became influential in Pune's government and opposed British interference in the Peshwa's affairs.

### Why did the British distrust him?

He encouraged the Peshwa to assert greater independence.

He also became associated with attempts to rebuild political connections among Maratha chiefs and mobilize resources outside British control.

### What was his political importance?

Trimbakji's position made him more than an ordinary court official.

He became a symbol of Baji Rao's efforts to resist the restrictions of the subsidiary alliance.

An incident involving the Gaekwad's representative soon brought him into direct conflict with the British.

## 4. Murder of Gangadhar Shastri at Pandharpur (July 1815)

This is the most important incident leading to the Treaty of Poona in 1817.

### Who was Gangadhar Shastri?

Gangadhar Shastri Patwardhan was an important official associated with the Gaekwad government of Baroda.

He was sent to Pune to negotiate financial and political disputes between the Peshwa and the Gaekwad.

### What was the dispute?

The Peshwa historically claimed certain revenue and political rights in Gujarat.

The Gaekwad had acquired substantial autonomy and enjoyed British support.

Both sides disagreed over outstanding payments and the nature of their relationship.

### Why did the British become involved?

Gangadhar Shastri conducted his negotiations under British diplomatic protection.

The British therefore regarded his personal security as an important responsibility.

### What happened?

Gangadhar Shastri was murdered at Pandharpur in July 1815, during a religious visit.

The Maharashtra State Gazetteer gives 20 July 1815 for the murder. Other contemporary accounts refer to 19 July, reflecting differences in recording the event.
### Who was accused?

Trimbakji Dengle was strongly implicated.

British officials believed the murder had been organized with his involvement.

They also suspected that Baji Rao II knew more about the conspiracy than he admitted.

However, the exact extent of the Peshwa's personal responsibility remains uncertain.

### Why was the incident serious?

The victim was an envoy involved in negotiations under British protection.

The Company treated his assassination as both a criminal offence and a political challenge to its authority.

### What did the British demand?

They demanded that Baji Rao surrender Trimbakji for punishment.

The Peshwa eventually complied.

Trimbakji was imprisoned at Thane Fort.

## 5. Trimbakji escapes from Thane (1816)

Despite his imprisonment, Trimbakji escaped from Thane in September 1816.

### Why was this politically dangerous?

British officials suspected that the Peshwa was secretly protecting him or cooperating with his supporters.

Trimbakji's followers remained active in parts of Maharashtra.

### What did the British believe?

They believed Baji Rao II was using the appearance of cooperation while secretly preparing to challenge British authority.

### What did Baji Rao hope to achieve?

He appeared to be exploring possibilities for renewed Maratha cooperation and military resistance.

But the Maratha houses had different interests, and the British were monitoring their diplomatic contacts.

### Result

The British increased pressure on Pune.

The confrontation led to the Treaty of Poona (1817).

The British Library's India Office collection preserves correspondence on Gangadhar Shastri, Trimbakji and the negotiations leading to the treaty.
## 6. Treaty of Poona — 13 June 1817

This treaty is extremely important for UPSC.
### Who signed it?

Baji Rao II and the British East India Company, represented by its authorities at Pune.

### Why was Baji Rao compelled to sign?

The British held him responsible for failing to cooperate adequately over Trimbakji and for pursuing activities contrary to the subsidiary alliance.

Company forces increased military pressure on Pune.

Baji Rao had limited options.

### Principal terms

| Provision            | Meaning                                                                               |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Trimbakji Dengle     | Peshwa required to assist in his capture and surrender                                |
| Territorial cessions | Further territories and revenues transferred to the Company                           |
| Gaekwad claims       | Peshwa **relinquished** important claims against the Gaekwad                          |
| Maratha leadership   | Peshwa formally **abandoned** claims to paramount authority over other Maratha chiefs |
| Foreign relations    | Independent political negotiations were further **restricted**                        |
| Earlier obligations  | Existing subsidiary arrangements strengthened                                         |
### Why was this treaty more humiliating than Bassein?

Bassein, 1802

Baji Rao accepted British protection to regain the Peshwaship.

Poona, 1817

The British imposed further restrictions and undermined his claims over other Maratha chiefs.

### Did Poona abolish the Peshwaship?

No.

The office continued in 1817, although with severely restricted political authority.

Its abolition came in 1818 after the war.

### Date clarification

The Treaty of Poona is dated 13 June 1817 in British Library records and contemporary gazetteer material. Some examination summaries describe it simply as June 1817 or give a different day.

## 7. The Pindaris — why did they matter?

The Third Anglo-Maratha War was closely connected with the Company's campaigns against the Pindaris.

### Who were the Pindaris?

The Pindaris were irregular mounted groups that conducted raids and sought plunder.

They had developed relationships with different armies and chiefs, including Maratha military establishments.

Over time, many operated as semi-independent raiding formations.

### Were the Pindaris a single Maratha army?

No.

They were not a centrally organized branch of the Peshwa's government.

Their leaders had varying political relationships with powerful regional chiefs.

### Important Pindari leaders

- Chitu
    
- Karim Khan
    
- Wasil Muhammad

### Why did the British want to suppress them?

Pindari raids threatened territories under Company control and allied states.

The Company wanted to stop raids and establish a stronger political and military position in central India.

### Who was the Governor-General?

Lord Hastings (Francis Rawdon-Hastings)

Governor-General: 1813–1823

Organized major military operations against the Pindaris and the Maratha powers in 1817–1818.

UPSC trap: Lord Hastings of the Third Anglo-Maratha War was not Warren Hastings, who had been Governor-General during the First Anglo-Maratha War.

## 8. British preparations for the Pindari campaign (1817)

Lord Hastings assembled large forces for operations across central India.

### Why was this strategically important?

The campaign was designed to surround and suppress the Pindari groups.

But the military preparations also enabled the Company to apply pressure on major Maratha powers.

### British objectives

1. Eliminate organized Pindari raiding.
    
2. Prevent Maratha chiefs from giving them refuge.
    
3. Secure roads and territories in central India.
    
4. Strengthen British political supremacy.

### Why were Scindia and Holkar important?

Their territories lay near important Pindari operating areas.

The Company wanted to prevent them from supporting raiding groups or joining a larger anti-British alliance.

### Treaty of Gwalior — 5 November 1817

Daulat Rao Scindia accepted an agreement that restricted his political freedom and required cooperation with British operations against the Pindaris.

The British thereby reduced the likelihood that Scindia would join Baji Rao II in open war.

### Why does this matter?

The British used diplomacy to isolate potential opponents before and during military operations.

The result was that the Peshwa could not rely on a united confederacy when he finally attacked British forces at Pune.
## 9. Battle of Khadki (Kirkee) — 5 November 1817
This battle marked the beginning of open warfare between Baji Rao II and the Company in 1817.
### Where was Khadki?

Khadki (Kirkee) is in present-day Pune.

British forces were positioned near the Company's Residency and military establishments.

### Who fought?

| Maratha side                            | British-led side                                  |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Peshwa Baji Rao II                      | Company troops at Pune                            |
| Bapu Gokhale, leading commander         | Colonel Charles Barton Burr                       |
| Maratha cavalry, infantry and artillery | British and Indian Company infantry and artillery |

### Why did the Peshwa attack?

Baji Rao was increasingly unwilling to accept British restrictions.

He had gathered troops and hoped to challenge the British military position at Pune.

The British had also mobilized forces in the region as part of their broader military preparations.

### What happened?

On 5 November 1817, Maratha forces attacked the British position near Khadki.

The British Residency was attacked and burned during the outbreak of hostilities.

Company troops held their positions and repelled the attack.

### Why did the Marathas fail?

The British-led force maintained disciplined defensive formations.

Maratha forces could not translate their numerical superiority into a decisive victory.

The military and political coordination of Baji Rao's government also proved inadequate.

### What happened afterward?

Baji Rao II abandoned Pune.

Company troops subsequently occupied the city.

This was a serious political defeat because the Peshwa had lost his principal capital.

The Maharashtra State Gazetteer gives 5 November 1817 as the date of the battle and records the attack on the Residency.
## 10. Bapu Gokhale — the Peshwa's principal military commander
### Who was Bapu Gokhale?

Bapu Gokhale was a leading military commander who remained loyal to Baji Rao II during the final struggle against the Company.

### Why was he important?

Even after the Peshwa lost Pune, Gokhale helped preserve the mobility and fighting capability of his remaining forces.

He organized cavalry operations and sought to protect Baji Rao from British pursuit.

### Was the war over after Khadki?

No.

Baji Rao still possessed troops and supporters.

The British needed to pursue him and secure important forts and territories.

Gokhale became central to the effort to prolong Maratha resistance.

A biographical historical study held by IGNCA records his role in the retreat and later fighting at Ashti.
## 11. Battle of Sitabuldi — 26–27 November 1817
The war was not confined to Pune.

Fighting also began in the Nagpur Bhonsle territory.

### Who was Appa Sahib Bhonsle?

Appa Sahib (Mudhoji II Bhonsle) had become the leading ruler at Nagpur.

He was dissatisfied with the restrictions created by British influence and his subsidiary alliance.

### Why did fighting begin?

Tensions increased between Appa Sahib's government and the British Resident at Nagpur.

Troops associated with Appa Sahib moved against the British position around the Sitabuldi hills.

### What happened?

On 26–27 November 1817, intense fighting took place around the two hills of Sitabuldi.

The defending British-led force initially faced a difficult situation.

However, the Company troops held important positions and eventually defeated the attacking forces.

### Why was the battle significant?

It prevented Appa Sahib from driving the British out of Nagpur.

It also weakened the possibility of effective cooperation between the Nagpur Bhonsles and the Peshwa.

### Did the Bhonsle dynasty end immediately in November 1817?

No.

Appa Sahib's position was subsequently undermined, but the British later installed **Raghuji III** as ruler of the reduced Nagpur state in 1818.

The Nagpur kingdom continued under subsidiary arrangements until its annexation in 1853.

## 12. Battle of Mahidpur — 21 December 1817
### Why did the Holkar state become involved?

The Holkar establishment had retained substantial power after the Second Anglo-Maratha War.

But it experienced political instability following the death of Yashwantrao Holkar in 1811.

The ruling house was headed by the young Malhar Rao Holkar III.

Various chiefs and officers competed over influence within the state.

### Who fought?

|Holkar forces|British forces|
|---|---|
|Army of the Holkar state|Sir Thomas Hislop|
|Commanders including Roshan Beg|Company infantry, cavalry and artillery|
|Regular infantry and substantial artillery|Experienced British-led formations|

### What happened?

On 21 December 1817, British forces confronted the Holkar army near Mahidpur, on the Shipra River.

The Holkar artillery offered determined resistance.

But the British eventually won a decisive victory.

### Why was this important?

The defeat weakened the Holkar state's ability to resist British demands.

It also removed another possible source of support for the Peshwa.

### Treaty of Mandsaur — 6 January 1818

The Holkar government subsequently signed the Treaty of Mandsaur (Mandeswar).

Principal consequences included:

- Territorial concessions.
    
- Acceptance of British political influence.
    
- Restrictions on independent diplomacy.
    
- A closer subsidiary relationship with the Company.

The original treaty is dated 6 January 1818.

## 13. Battle of Koregaon Bhima — 1 January 1818

This is one of the most historically significant and socially remembered battles of the final Anglo-Maratha War.

### Where was Koregaon?

Koregaon Bhima lies on the Bhima River, northeast of Pune.

It should not be confused with other places named Koregaon in Maharashtra.

### Who fought?

A British East India Company detachment under Captain Francis Staunton encountered forces of Peshwa Baji Rao II.

The Company detachment consisted of approximately 800 troops, including infantry, irregular cavalry and artillery personnel.

The opposing Peshwa army was much larger, although only part of it was committed to the direct assault.

### Why were the Company troops there?

They had marched from Seroor (Shirur) towards Pune in response to fears of renewed Maratha military activity.

On 1 January 1818, they encountered Baji Rao's forces at Koregaon.

### How did the battle unfold?

1. Company detachment arrived near Koregaon.
    
    It encountered a much larger Maratha army and took defensive positions in the village.
    
2. Peshwa troops attacked.
    
    Maratha infantry and supporting forces advanced against the village.
    
3. Intense fighting occurred.
    
    The combat involved assaults on houses, defensive positions and artillery.
    
4. Heavy casualties were suffered.
    
    Both sides experienced significant losses.
    
5. Maratha forces disengaged.
    
    After a day of fierce fighting, the Peshwa's forces withdrew from the immediate engagement.
    

### Who won?

The Company force held its position and the British later commemorated the engagement as a victory.

However, the precise tactical interpretation is debated because the Peshwa's much larger army was not decisively destroyed, and the engagement formed part of a wider campaign rather than a final battlefield surrender.

It is safest to distinguish the successful defence by Staunton's detachment from the broader military defeat of the Peshwa that followed later in 1818.

### The Mahar soldiers and the battle's later significance

A significant number of soldiers serving in the Company's infantry were from the Mahar community.

The battlefield memorial subsequently became an important symbol in **Dalit history**.

**Dr. B. R. Ambedkar** visited the memorial and emphasized the role of Mahar soldiers.

The event has therefore acquired two connected but distinct historical meanings:

1. An engagement during the Third Anglo-Maratha War.
    
2. A site of later remembrance connected with caste discrimination, dignity and social equality.

### Was there a separate Mahar Regiment in 1818?

Not in the sense of the later formal Mahar Regiment, which was raised in the twentieth century.

Mahar soldiers served within the Bombay Army's infantry units during the 1818 battle.

The Indian Express's examination of the historical gazetteer and Ambedkar's writings document both the military events and later interpretations.
## 14. Battle of Ashti (Ashta) — February 1818

After Khadki, Baji Rao II moved across Maharashtra to avoid capture.

British forces under commanders including **General Lionel Smith** pursued him.

Bapu Gokhale attempted to protect the Peshwa and his remaining forces.

### What happened?

In February 1818, British cavalry encountered Maratha forces near Ashti (Ashta) in the Solapur region.

Bapu Gokhale was killed during the fighting.

### Exact date

The battle is most commonly dated 20 February 1818, although some historical accounts place Gokhale's death on 19 February.

For examination preparation, February 1818 — Ashti — Bapu Gokhale killed is the essential fact.
### Why was this a major setback?

Gokhale had been one of Baji Rao's most loyal and capable commanders.

His death weakened the Peshwa's remaining military resistance.

### What happened to the Satara Chhatrapati?

During the campaign, British forces also gained custody of Pratap Singh Bhonsle, the Chhatrapati of Satara, and his family.

The British subsequently used the distinction between the Chhatrapati's dynasty and the Peshwa's government to justify a separate political settlement.

This was an important part of their strategy for dismantling the Peshwa's authority.

## 15. British capture of Maratha forts (1818)

While British forces pursued Baji Rao, other Company detachments attacked important Maratha forts.
### Why capture the forts?

Forts contained military stores, defensive positions, officials and valuable resources.

As long as they remained under Peshwa control, resistance might continue even if the field army was defeated.

### Important positions

During 1818, British forces captured numerous forts, including:

- Satara / Ajinkyatara
    
- Sinhagad
    
- Purandar
    
- Raigad
    
- Vasota

### Why was the fall of Raigad symbolically important?

Raigad had been Shivaji Maharaj's capital and the site of his coronation in 1674.

Its capture during the final Peshwa campaign symbolized the loss of a historic centre of Maratha power.

However, Raigad had changed hands at different times over the preceding centuries, so its capture in 1818 should not be described as the first time it had ever fallen to an enemy.

### What did the British achieve?

By capturing forts and occupying the Peshwa's territory, they progressively eliminated his ability to maintain a functioning territorial government.

The military conflict was now approaching its final stage.
## 16. Baji Rao II's surrender — 3 June 1818

Baji Rao II continued moving through central India in the hope of securing support.

But Scindia, Holkar and other powers did not provide the unified assistance he needed.

His forces were increasingly exhausted.

Many forts had fallen, and important commanders had been killed.
### To whom did he surrender?

Sir John Malcolm, a senior British political and military officer.

### When?

The standard date for the surrender is 3 June 1818.

Negotiations had taken place in the Dhulkot–Mhow region of central India.

### What were the terms?

Baji Rao agreed to relinquish his political authority as Peshwa.

In return, the British provided for his personal maintenance.

He was granted an annual pension conventionally recorded as ₹8 lakh.

### Where did he live afterward?

He settled at Bithur, near Kanpur, on the Ganges.

He remained there until his death in 1851.

### Was Baji Rao executed?

No.

He was pensioned and removed from active political power.

### Why did he surrender?

He could no longer maintain an effective military and territorial resistance.

His allies had been neutralized or defeated, and the Company occupied much of his territory.

The Maharashtra State Gazetteer records his surrender on 3 June 1818 and subsequent retirement at Bithur.

## 17. Abolition of the Peshwaship (1818)

This was the principal political consequence of the Third Anglo-Maratha War.

### What did the British decide?

They abolished the office of Peshwa as the executive head of the Maratha state.

Baji Rao II's territories were largely absorbed into Company rule.

### Was this the end of the entire Bhonsle royal family?

No.

The British separated the office of Peshwa from the older Chhatrapati dynasty.

### What happened to Pratap Singh of Satara?

Pratap Singh Bhonsle, a descendant of Shivaji's royal line, was recognized as the ruler of a reduced Satara state.

He governed under British paramountcy.
### Why did the British do this?

It served several political purposes:

1. Distinguished the Chhatrapati's royal lineage from the Peshwa.
    
2. Helped justify the removal of Baji Rao II.
    
3. Provided a local ruler dependent on British support.
    
4. Reduced the likelihood of a united Maratha political revival.

### Did Satara regain the power of Shivaji's kingdom?

No.

The restored Satara principality was much smaller and politically dependent on the Company.

It was not a revival of the independent seventeenth-century Maratha kingdom.

### What happened later?

The British deposed(પદભ્રષ્ટ) Pratap Singh in 1839.

Satara was eventually annexed in 1848 under Lord Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse, following the death of his successor Shahaji (Appa Saheb).

Those developments belong to the history of British paramountcy rather than the 1817–1818 war itself.

## 18. Did every Maratha ruling house disappear in 1818?

No. This is an important UPSC distinction.

| House                 | Position after 1818                                                        |
| --------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Peshwa of Pune        | Office abolished                                                           |
| Chhatrapati of Satara | Reduced princely state under British paramountcy                           |
| Scindia of Gwalior    | Continued as a princely ruler                                              |
| Holkar of Indore      | Continued under subsidiary arrangements                                    |
| Gaekwad of Baroda     | Continued as a princely ruler                                              |
| Bhonsle of Nagpur     | Continued through a British-supported succession, until annexation in 1853 |
| Other Maratha chiefs  | Several retained estates, titles or local political positions              |

### So what actually ended?

The Peshwa-led Maratha political system ceased to function as an independent great power.

The Company became the paramount power across much of the former Maratha sphere.

But a number of Maratha dynasties continued ruling princely states under British supremacy.

Historical conclusion: The end of the Peshwaship was not identical to the extinction of every Maratha royal or noble family.

### Was the loss inevitable from 1802?

Not necessarily.

Maratha leaders retained considerable military capabilities.

But the **Treaty of Bassein** and the subsequent British victories had fundamentally changed the balance of power.

By 1817, the Company possessed stronger territorial resources and had developed effective ways to isolate individual Maratha states.

The failure to build a unified political and military response was critical.

## 23. UPSC Mains — model answer

Question: “The Third Anglo-Maratha War completed the establishment of British paramountcy over the Maratha political system.” Examine. (250 words)

The Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1818) marked the final collapse of the Peshwa-led Maratha state and established British paramountcy over much of western and central India.

Political background: The Treaty of Bassein (1802) had made Peshwa Baji Rao II dependent on British military protection. Further restrictions imposed through the Treaty of Poona (1817) increased his resentment.

Immediate causes: The murder of Gangadhar Shastri in 1815, Trimbakji Dengle's escape and British operations against the Pindaris intensified tensions between the Peshwa and the East India Company.

Military campaign: In November 1817, the Peshwa's forces attacked British positions near Pune but were defeated at Khadki. British victories over the Nagpur Bhonsles at Sitabuldi and Holkar's forces at Mahidpur prevented the development of coordinated Maratha resistance.

Further resistance: The engagement at Koregaon Bhima in January 1818 demonstrated continued Maratha military activity. However, the death of Bapu Gokhale at Ashti and the capture of important forts weakened the Peshwa's position.

Final settlement: Baji Rao II surrendered in June 1818. The British abolished the Peshwaship, pensioned the former Peshwa at Bithur and annexed much of his territory.

A reduced Satara state was recognized under Pratap Singh, while Scindia, Holkar, Gaekwad and other houses survived under British paramountcy.

Thus, the war ended the Peshwa's independent political authority and consolidated the Company's position as the dominant power over the former Maratha sphere.

## 25. Important connection with the Revolt of 1857

Baji Rao II's exile at Bithur created an important link between the fall of the Peshwas and the later uprising of 1857.

### Who was Nana Sahib of 1857?

Nana Sahib (Dhondu Pant) was an adopted son of Baji Rao II.

He was not Nanasaheb Peshwa (Balaji Baji Rao), who had died in 1761.

### What happened?

After Baji Rao II died in 1851, disputes arose over the continuation of his pension.

Nana Sahib later became an important leader of the Revolt of 1857 at Kanpur.

This does not mean the Peshwaship was restored in 1857.

It means the consequences of the 1818 settlement continued to influence later political grievances.
