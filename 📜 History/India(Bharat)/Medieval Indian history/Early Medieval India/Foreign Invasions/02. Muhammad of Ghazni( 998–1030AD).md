## Where he comes from

By the end of the **ninth century** the Abbasid caliphate had declined. Commanders in the provinces kept the armies and the revenue and set up kingdoms of their own. Many of those commanders were **Turkish** military slaves. The caliph in **Baghdad** remained. Rulers still wanted a title from him, but his authority had become **symbolic**. He did not command their armies.

The clearest moment in Baghdad itself is **945**, when the Buyids occupied the city and left the caliph on the throne as a symbol. The caliphate had not ended. Real power had moved.

One of those commanders was **Alptigin**. He was a Turkish slave-soldier of the **Samanids**, an Iranian dynasty whose centre was **Bukhara**. He was not a son of the caliph, and the Samanids were not a Turkish dynasty. In **962** he broke with them and made **Ghazni** his capital.

**Ghazni** is a city in present-day Afghanistan. It is west of India. **Ghor** is a different, mountainous region. Do not mix the city and the region.

![[ghazni-alptigin-capital.png]]

Read the map from west to east. Baghdad still has the caliph. Bukhara is where Alptigin had served. The marked capital is **Ghazni**. Ghor is the other name, and it is not this city. | Person         | Date         | What he did                                                                                                      |
| -------------- | ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Alptigin       | 962          | Turkish commander of the Samanids. Capital: **Ghazni**. He died soon after. He did not invade India.            |
| Sebuktigin     | 977–997      | Alptigin’s **slave**, not his son. He built the Ghaznavid state and defeated the Hindu Shahi king **Jayapala**. |
| **Mahmud**     | **998–1030** | Sebuktigin’s **son**. He took the throne after defeating his brother **Ismail**. The Indian campaigns are his.  |

So 962, 977 and 998 are three different steps. Mahmud did not invent the war on the Indian frontier. He inherited it and carried it much further.

The ruling soldiers were **Turkic**. The court language was **Persian**. He was a **Sunni** Muslim. The Abbasid caliph, **al-Qadir**, gave him the titles **Yamin al-Dawla** (“right hand of the state”; textbooks also write Yamin-ud-Daulah) and **Amin al-Milla** (Amin-ul-Millah, “trustee of the community”). The titles gave prestige. They did not put the caliph in command of the army.

Some exam books call Mahmud the first man to take the title **sultan**. Use the two titles above. Do not build the answer on “first sultan” alone.

His real base stayed at **Ghazni**. He needed Indian wealth to pay that army and to hold Khurasan and fight in Central Asia. He was not trying to sit in Delhi.

## The spirit of the ghazi

A **ghazi** is a fighter on the frontier who wages war in the name of Islam.

Mahmud ruled in that spirit. It gave his raids a religious meaning: temples could be destroyed, and he could claim to be the champion of Sunni Islam. That is the “spirit of the ghazi.”

It does not mean religion was the only motive, or that he fought only Hindus.

- **Wealth.** Temples held treasure. The gold went back to Ghazni.
- **Politics.** Victory raised his prestige and paid for wars outside India.
- **Territory.** He did annex the northwest, especially the Punjab frontier. Calling him a raider who took no land is wrong.
- **Other Muslims.** In 1006 he overthrew the Ismaili ruler of **Multan**. A ghazi campaign could be aimed at Muslims he called heretics.

He went home after the deep raids. He did not leave a sultan at Delhi.

## The two roads

![[ghazni-to-india-routes.png]]

Read the map before the table. Every line starts at **Ghazni** and is meant to come back there.

1. **Blue — north India, 1001–1022.** Ghazni, through Peshawar, into Punjab. One branch goes up to **Nagarkot** (Kangra). The main line goes on to **Thanesar**, **Mathura** and **Kannauj**. From Mathura a branch turns south to **Gwalior** and **Kalinjar**.
2. **Orange — Gujarat, 1025–26.** Ghazni to **Multan**, then across the **Thar** desert to **Somnath**. He did not take the straight road through the strongest Rajput kingdoms. On the way back, in **1027**, Jats in Sindh attacked the column.
3. **Red — a stop, not a conquest.** The short line toward **Lohkot** is the failed push at Kashmir in **1015**.
4. **Delhi** is marked so you can see it. The **Tomara** Rajputs were ruling there. He did not defeat them, and he did not make Delhi his capital. The Chauhans took Delhi in the 12th century.

On each label, the second line is the ruler and the dynasty. A **brown** word means Mahmud won that fight or took the spoil. A **red** word means he was stopped, or he did not keep the place. The lines are classroom guides, not the measured track of each year’s army.

**Al-Biruni**, a scholar from Khwarazm, worked at Mahmud’s court and wrote *Kitab al-Hind*, a study of India. He is part of this reign. He is not a general.

The Persian poet **Firdausi** composed the *Shahnameh* in this age, and it was presented at Mahmud’s court. Learn the book and the court. A later story says poet and sultan quarrelled over the poet’s pay. Do not treat that quarrel as a dated fact.

## Iqta

**Iqta** is an assignment of the **revenue of a piece of land** to an officer, instead of a cash salary. The holder is later called a **muqti** or **iqtadar**. He collects the revenue, maintains troops, and is supposed to send the surplus on.

That system, as the Indian exam uses the word, belongs to the **Delhi Sultanate**, above all to **Iltutmish**. It is not Mahmud’s way of ruling India.

What Mahmud actually did:

- He paid his army from **plunder** and from the revenue of lands he really held: Ghazni, Khurasan, and the Punjab frontier.
- He did **not** plant iqtadars across the Ganga plain.
- He did **not** found the Delhi Sultanate.

If a question says “iqta,” do not answer “Mahmud’s seventeen raids.” If it says “ghazi,” the answer is Mahmud’s frontier warfare in the name of Islam, tied to plunder and prestige.

## Impact of Mahmud

He died in **1030**. The expeditions were his. The impact is what remained after the armies went home to Ghazni.

### What changed

| Result         | What to say                                                                                                                                                                          |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| The northwest  | The **Hindu Shahis** were finished. **Punjab** became a Ghaznavid frontier. Later Ghaznavid rulers also used **Lahore**. This is annexation of the frontier, not of the Ganga plain. |
| Wealth         | Temple treasuries went back to **Ghazni**. **Somnath, 1025**, is the raid to name. Ghazni became a rich court, not a new Indian capital.                                             |
| The road ahead | Divided north Indian kingdoms had been shown to be open to a fast cavalry attack. That made **Muhammad Ghori’s** later conquest easier. It did not itself found a sultanate.         |
| Learning       | **Al-Biruni’s** *Kitab al-Hind* is the book. **Firdausi’s** *Shahnameh* belongs to the same court.                                                                                   |

### What did not follow

| Claim | The fact |
| --- | --- |
| He conquered India | He kept the northwest and raided beyond it. |
| He took Delhi | The Tomaras were still there. Delhi was not his capital. |
| He founded the Delhi Sultanate | That begins in **1206**, with Qutb ud-Din Aibak. |
| He set up iqta in India | Iqta, in the exam sense, belongs to the Delhi Sultanate, above all to Iltutmish. |
| His death handed India to his son | **Muhammad of Ghazni** was a different, short-lived ruler. The table above is Mahmud’s. |

Set him beside the other two invasions. **Qasim, 712**, was an Arab commander, and the land that stayed taken was Sindh to Multan. **Mahmud, 998–1030**, was a Turkish king at Ghazni, and the land that stayed taken was the Punjab frontier. **Ghori**, at the end of the twelfth century, won Tarain and left the foundations of rule at Delhi.

## His son Muhammad — a different man

**Muhammad of Ghazni** (about 998–1041) was Mahmud’s son, and the twin of **Masud**.

- In **1030**, on Mahmud’s death, Muhammad was put on the throne.
- Masud removed him within months. One later writer, Ferishta, says the first reign lasted only about fifty days. He was blinded and imprisoned.
- Around **1040–1041** he was brought out again and then killed after a defeat.

He did not lead the expeditions in the table above.

## One answer

By the end of the ninth century the caliphate had declined. Turkish commanders founded their own kingdoms, and the caliph became a symbolic authority. **Alptigin** made **Ghazni** his capital in **962**. His slave **Sebuktigin** built the state from **977**. **Mahmud**, Sebuktigin’s son, reigned **998–1030**. Ghazi raids plus annexation of the northwest. Somnath **1025**. Not the iqta system. Not the Delhi Sultanate. His son Muhammad is a later, short-lived sultan.
