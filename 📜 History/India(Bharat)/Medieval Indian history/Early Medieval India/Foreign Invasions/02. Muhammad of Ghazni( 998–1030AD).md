# Mahmud of Ghazni, 998–1030

The file is named Muhammad. The invader of India is **Mahmud**. His son **Muhammad** ruled only after him, and did not lead these campaigns. That pair is at the end of the page.

## Where he comes from

**Ghazni** is a city in present-day Afghanistan. **Ghor** is a different, mountainous region. Do not mix them.

| Person     | Date         | What he did                                                                                                      |
| ---------- | ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Alptigin   | 962          | A Turkic commander of the Samanids. He made Ghazni his base.                                                     |
| Sebuktigin | from 977     | Also of slave-soldier origin. He built the Ghaznavid state and had already fought the Hindu Shahi king Jayapala. |
| **Mahmud** | **998–1030** | Sebuktigin’s son. He took the throne after a struggle with his brother Ismail.                                   |

So 962, 977 and 998 are three different steps. Mahmud did not invent the war on the Indian frontier. He inherited it and carried it much further.

The ruling soldiers were **Turkic**. The court culture and language of administration were **Persian**. He was a **Sunni** Muslim. The Abbasid caliph in Baghdad was still the source of prestige: Mahmud took titles from him, including **Yamin al-Dawla** (“right hand of the state”). The caliph did not command Mahmud’s army.

His real base stayed at **Ghazni**. He needed Indian wealth to pay that army and to hold his lands in Khurasan and fight in Central Asia. He was not trying to sit in Delhi.

## The spirit of the ghazi

A **ghazi** is a fighter on the frontier who wages war in the name of Islam.

Mahmud ruled in that spirit. It gave his raids a religious meaning: temples could be destroyed, and he could claim to be the champion of Sunni Islam. That is the “spirit of the ghazi.”

It does not mean religion was the only motive, or that he fought only Hindus.

- **Wealth.** Temples held treasure. The gold went back to Ghazni.
- **Politics.** Victory raised his prestige and paid for wars outside India.
- **Territory.** He did annex the northwest, especially the Punjab frontier. Calling him a raider who took no land is wrong.
- **Other Muslims.** In 1006 he overthrew the Ismaili ruler of **Multan**. A ghazi campaign could be aimed at Muslims he called heretics.

He went home after the deep raids. He did not leave a sultan at Delhi.

## The two roads

![[ghazni-to-india-routes.png]]

Read the map before the table. Every line starts at **Ghazni** and is meant to come back there.

1. **Blue — north India, 1001–1022.** Ghazni, through Peshawar, into Punjab. One branch goes up to **Nagarkot** (Kangra). The main line goes on to **Thanesar**, **Mathura** and **Kannauj**. From Mathura a branch turns south to **Gwalior** and **Kalinjar**.
2. **Orange — Gujarat, 1025–26.** Ghazni to **Multan**, then across the **Thar** desert to **Somnath**. He did not take the straight road through the strongest Rajput kingdoms. On the way back, in **1027**, Jats in Sindh attacked the column.
3. **Red — a stop, not a conquest.** The short line toward **Lohkot** is the failed push at Kashmir in **1015**.
4. **Delhi** is marked so you can see it. He raided past that country. He did not make Delhi his capital.

The lines are classroom guides. They are not the measured track of each year’s army.

## The attacks to remember

Traditional accounts count about **17** expeditions between **1000 and 1027**. Lists do not agree on the number. Learn these. They are the ones a question can name.

| Year | Who or where | Result |
| ---- | ------------ | ------ |
| 1001 | **Jayapala**, Hindu Shahi king, near Peshawar | Defeated. The chronicle story is that he later burned himself. |
| 1006 | **Multan** | Ismaili ruler removed. |
| 1008 | **Anandapala**, Jayapala’s son, at Waihind | Hindu Shahi power in the northwest broken. Often dated 1008–1009. |
| 1009 | **Nagarkot** (Kangra) | The fort and its temple treasury taken. |
| 1014 | **Thanesar** | Raided. |
| 1015 | **Lohkot**, toward Kashmir | Failed. He did not win every campaign. |
| 1018 | **Mathura** and **Kannauj** | The deepest raid into the Ganga valley before Somnath. |
| 1021–22 | **Gwalior** and **Kalinjar** | Pressure on the Chandelas. No lasting occupation of central India. |
| **1025–26** | **Somnath**, in Kathiawar | The famous raid. Exam shorthand is **1025**. He went by Multan and the desert rather than straight through the strongest Rajput lands. |
| 1027 | Jats of Sindh | They had attacked his army on the way back from Somnath. This was punishment, and it is usually counted as the last Indian expedition. |

**Al-Biruni**, a scholar from Khwarazm, worked at Mahmud’s court and wrote *Kitab al-Hind*, a study of India. He is part of this reign. He is not a general.

## Iqta

**Iqta** is an assignment of the **revenue of a piece of land** to an officer, instead of a cash salary. The holder is later called a **muqti** or **iqtadar**. He collects the revenue, maintains troops, and is supposed to send the surplus on.

That system, as the Indian exam uses the word, belongs to the **Delhi Sultanate**, above all to **Iltutmish**. It is not Mahmud’s way of ruling India.

What Mahmud actually did:

- He paid his army from **plunder** and from the revenue of lands he really held: Ghazni, Khurasan, and the Punjab frontier.
- He did **not** plant iqtadars across the Ganga plain.
- He did **not** found the Delhi Sultanate.

If a question says “iqta,” do not answer “Mahmud’s seventeen raids.” If it says “ghazi,” the answer is Mahmud’s frontier warfare in the name of Islam, tied to plunder and prestige.

## What he left behind

He died in **1030**. The Hindu Shahis of the northwest were finished. Punjab was a Ghaznavid frontier. The rest of northern India had been raided, not occupied. His successors later ruled from **Lahore** as well as Ghazni, especially after they lost their western lands. That is still not a Delhi sultanate. The conventional start of that is **Aibak in 1206**.

## His son Muhammad — a different man

**Muhammad of Ghazni** (about 998–1041) was Mahmud’s son, and the twin of **Masud**.

- In **1030**, on Mahmud’s death, Muhammad was put on the throne.
- Masud removed him within months. One later writer, Ferishta, says the first reign lasted only about fifty days. He was blinded and imprisoned.
- Around **1040–1041** he was brought out again and then killed after a defeat.

He did not lead the expeditions in the table above.

## One answer

Mahmud, **998–1030**, Ghazni. Ghazi raids plus annexation of the northwest. Somnath **1025**. Not the iqta system. Not the Delhi Sultanate. His son Muhammad is a later, short-lived sultan.
