## 1. India around 1600 CE — the political situation

Imagine India around 1600 CE.

In northern India, the Mughal emperor Akbar (1556–1605) had built a powerful empire. He controlled extensive territories, possessed a large military establishment and was expanding towards the Deccan.

However, southern and central India were not completely under Mughal authority.

The Deccan had several competing kingdoms, powerful military nobles, fortified settlements and regional landholding families. Control over territory was frequently contested.

## 2. How did the Deccan Sultanates emerge?

To understand 1600, we must briefly return to the Bahmani Sultanate (1347–1527).

The Bahmani kingdom had once controlled much of the Deccan. As its central authority weakened, powerful provincial elites established separate states.

Five major successor Sultanates emerged:

|Sultanate|Dynasty|Approximate emergence|
|---|---|---|
|Ahmadnagar|Nizam Shahi|1490|
|Bijapur|Adil Shahi|1490|
|Berar|Imad Shahi|1490|
|Bidar|Barid Shahi|Late 15th century|
|Golconda|Qutb Shahi|Early 16th century|

By about 1600, Ahmadnagar, Bijapur and Golconda were particularly important, while Berar had been absorbed and Bidar subsequently came under Bijapur.

These were not simply foreign ruling establishments separated from local society. They employed Maratha chiefs, Brahmin administrators, Deccani military groups and other local elites. Persianate and regional political cultures coexisted.

### Why did fragmentation matter?

The breakup of the Bahmani Sultanate created competition.

Each ruler needed:

- Soldiers and cavalry commanders.
- Forts and strongholds.
- Revenue from agriculture and trade.
- Support from local chiefs.
- Alliances against neighbouring kingdoms.
This opened opportunities for ambitious Maratha families to obtain military commands, land-revenue assignments and political influence.

Cause → effect:
> Decline of Bahmani central authority
> Rise of competing Deccan Sultanates
> Greater demand for local military commanders
> Growing influence of Maratha families


## 3. Who were the Marathas before Shivaji?

This is one of the most important questions in the chapter.

The term Maratha did not originally refer to a single unified kingdom. It had overlapping regional, social and political meanings, and Maratha families differed considerably in status and resources.

In Maharashtra, there were cultivators(ખેડૂતો), hereditary village officials, cavalrymen(ઘોડેસવાર સૈનિકો), military commanders and landed chiefs who became associated with Maratha political power.

Important families included:

- Bhonsle (Bhosale) — the family of Shivaji.
- Jadhav (Jadhavrao) — connected to Shivaji's maternal family.
- Nimbalkar — influential landed and military family.
- Ghorpade — military and aristocratic(noble) family.
- More — influential chiefs, including in Javli.
- Jedhe — important among regional landed elites.


These families were not united under one Maratha ruler in 1600.
Some served Ahmadnagar, some served Bijapur, and others could change allegiance according to political circumstances.

### Why would a Maratha chief serve a Sultanate?

Imagine a chief controlling several villages and commanding 500 cavalrymen.

The Sultan of Ahmadnagar might offer him a military command and revenue assignment in return for service.

If Ahmadnagar became unstable, Bijapur or the Mughals might offer better terms.

The chief could then change allegiance, hoping to protect his hereditary rights and expand his family's influence.

This is essential historical context: Service under a Sultanate and the development of later Maratha independence were not inherently contradictory.

## 4. The political and social system of Maharashtra

Before the rise of the independent Maratha state, much of Maharashtra's local government functioned through hereditary officeholders.

### Important administrative terms

| Term                                                 | Meaning                                                                     | Why important                                            |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| Deshmukh<br>(પરગણાનો વડો / દેશાઈ)                    | Influential hereditary regional chief or revenue intermediary               | Could mobilize soldiers and influence groups of villages |
| Deshpande<br>(પરગણાનો મુખ્ય હિસાબનીસ)                | Hereditary record-keeping/revenue official at the district or pargana level | Helped maintain revenue accounts                         |
| Patil<br>(ગામનો પટેલ / મુખી)                         | Village headman                                                             | Assisted in local administration and revenue collection  |
| Kulkarni<br>(ગામનો હિસાબનીસ / તલાટી જેવું કામ)       | Village accountant                                                          | Maintained village records                               |
| Jagir<br>(મહેસૂલ આવક મેળવવાનો અધિકાર ધરાવતો પ્રદેશ)  | Assignment of revenue from specified territory, usually linked to service   | Source of income and political influence                 |
| Watan<br>(વારસાગત પદ, હક અને તેની સાથે જોડાયેલા લાભ) | Hereditary office or service right, often carrying emoluments               | Connected local authority to hereditary privilege        |
| Mansab<br>(હોદ્દો / પદ / દરજ્જો)                     | Official rank in the Mughal service system                                  | Determined rank and service obligations                  |

### Understand the difference between jagir and watan

Jagir: A ruler assigns a commander the right to collect or receive specified revenue, commonly in return for service. It did not necessarily give the commander ownership of the land.

Watan: A hereditary office or customary right associated with a local family.

A family could possess hereditary local influence and also receive jagir assignments from a more powerful state.

### Why did these institutions help the Marathas rise?

They created networks of local authority.

A regional chief might already know:

- Which villages could supply food.
    
- Which passes could be used by cavalry.
    
- Which forts were vulnerable.
    
- Which families controlled local manpower.
    
- How revenue could be collected.
    

When central Sultanate authority weakened, these local networks could become the foundation of more independent political power.

UPSC Mains value addition: State formation in the western Deccan involved the transformation of existing local military and revenue networks, not their sudden creation from nothing

## 5. Geography — why western Maharashtra was strategically important

![Torna Fort - Qualla](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/wmsHN88IU_FmMF3Kn-oNuk7frn7mEbYsgdRPXLvBq0F2n8atQxKDyozYuHRILMO8r5IjPfQTxsZtcJB2hjbnZUmgZhMNYQJ522vAnmeXFVcvQ3NgQPpaWfFPtBG7DxgSYpwJPl3TZzMrkUvnaHAqERycsPJTKXeGazYShmQAkHk?purpose=inline)

Western Maharashtra contained the **Sahyadri** (Western Ghats), rugged valleys, hill forts and routes connecting the inland Deccan plateau with the Konkan coast.

### Three geographical zones

| Zone                  | Characteristics                                  | Political importance                                            |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Desh / Deccan plateau | Inland agricultural settlements and open terrain | Revenue, cavalry movement and political centres                 |
| Maval region          | Valleys and hill country west of Pune            | Local fighters, village networks and difficult approaches       |
| Konkan                | Narrow western coastal belt                      | Ports, trade, coastal routes and access through mountain passes |

### Why did the Maval region matter?

The Mavals were not an entirely separate kingdom. They were a set of hill valleys and settlements whose populations possessed considerable familiarity with the local terrain.

This geography made it possible for relatively small forces to move effectively through passes, hills and forests.

By contrast, large imperial armies often depended on more elaborate supply arrangements, baggage trains, heavy artillery and animals.

This contrast later helped explain some of Shivaji's military methods, although hill terrain never made the Marathas invincible.

### Geography and power

A fort controlled more than a stone building.

It could help regulate:

- Access through a mountain pass.
- Movement of troops.
- Communication between valleys.
- Collection and protection of revenue.
- Stores and military supplies.

In a fragmented political environment, a network of forts could become a foundation for territorial authority.

## 6. The Mughal expansion into the Deccan (1595–1600)

Now the story becomes more complicated.

In the late sixteenth century, Mughal emperor Akbar sought to expand his authority southwards.

One major target was the Ahmadnagar Sultanate, which controlled important territory between the expanding Mughal Empire and other Deccan powers.

### Why did the Mughals want Ahmadnagar?

Ahmadnagar was strategically valuable because it offered:

1. Access to the Deccan plateau.
2. Agricultural and commercial revenues.
3. Military routes towards Bijapur and Golconda.
4. The opportunity to prevent regional rulers from challenging imperial expansion.

### Chand Bibi and the defence of Ahmadnagar

![Chand bibi | IndiaFactsIndiaFacts](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/rvu9e9sWj-mL8YJ__VgJ5By9YVq642ITBrYXQmPKpcZEreJc4vRF4zGxRHXp_1vYQE7x2Zq0KLy_upxuH_LiiajNsjjqKLeYuAg_iP9FqEh-B93rTqfAM-RKxD4RDC_CECfhRMPx-u5IISJFmD8plDebJ36Rz2TAG0lUIlI514A?purpose=inline)

During the Mughal intervention in Ahmadnagar, Chand Bibi emerged as an important political and military leader.

She organized the defence of Ahmadnagar against Mughal forces and attempted to preserve the Sultanate during a succession crisis.

In 1595–1596, she led resistance against a Mughal siege and reached an agreement involving the surrender of Berar.

But hostilities resumed.

1600: Mughal forces captured the fort of Ahmadnagar. Chand Bibi was killed during the crisis.

### Did Ahmadnagar disappear in 1600?

No. This is a very important UPSC distinction.

The Mughals captured the principal fort and capital, but they did not immediately control all of Ahmadnagar's territory.

Several Nizam Shahi nobles and commanders continued resistance from other parts of the kingdom.

This created an opportunity for **Malik Ambar**, one of the most important personalities in the history of the early seventeenth-century Deccan.

## 7. Malik Ambar (c. 1600–1626): The man who resisted the Mughals

![Malik Ambar – The African King who ruled in India – The Muslim Times](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/vE1rTeUCZ24JqGUPzSPZIfG4JukJVwkXXrbCrhJRrZNWgSc_vflcqkQo4uPGwOg8d2WnGWy2EKvklN5QGr-hUi2tAdvj9tCxan1nWGgSDLRtmW7ZEcOQ0-OU-IQsr98jyaMfIJmt-ThtpBLmIhNyyiRnxUcep-kWe3O55WaDxXI?purpose=inline)

**Origin:** Born in the Horn of Africa, traditionally identified with Ethiopia.
**Political role:** Powerful military commander and regent of the Ahmadnagar Sultanate.
**Principal enemy:** Mughal expansion.
**Death:** 1626.
### 7.1 How did Malik Ambar become powerful?

Malik Ambar's early life was extraordinary.

He had been enslaved, taken through western Asian trading networks and eventually brought to the Deccan.

After gaining his freedom, he built a career as a soldier and military leader.

When Ahmadnagar's political establishment was shattered by Mughal attacks around 1600, many commanders searched for new patrons and opportunities.

Malik Ambar gathered military followers, supported a **Nizam Shahi claimant** and emerged as the effective leader of resistance against the Mughals.

### 7.2 How did he fight a stronger Mughal army?

The Mughals could deploy large armies with cavalry, elephants, artillery and extensive camp establishments.

Malik Ambar recognized that direct confrontation on unfavourable ground could be dangerous.

His forces therefore relied heavily on:

- Highly mobile cavalry.
- Attacks on vulnerable detachments.
- Disruption of Mughal supply lines.
- Avoiding pitched battles when conditions favoured the Mughals.
- Exploitation of local geography.
- Strategic retreat and regrouping.

These methods are often associated with **bargir-giri**, a form of mobile Deccan warfare.

### 7.3 His administrative contributions

Malik Ambar also strengthened the administration of the territories under his authority.

He is especially associated with efforts to improve land-revenue assessment and the organization of the countryside.

He developed **Khadki**, which later became associated with the city of Aurangabad.

### 7.4 Why is Malik Ambar important for Maratha history?

Malik Ambar did not establish the Maratha kingdom.

However, his political system employed and influenced regional military elites, including Maratha commanders.

His campaigns demonstrated how mobile regional forces could resist a much larger Mughal army.

UPSC interpretation: Malik Ambar helped shape the military and political environment from which Maratha power later emerged. But it would be an oversimplification to say Shivaji simply copied all his methods.

Malik Ambar died in 1626. His death deprived the weakened Ahmadnagar Sultanate of an exceptionally capable leader.
