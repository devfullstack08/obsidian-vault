## 1. Historical background and rise to power

Ghiyas ud-Din Tughlaq, originally known as Ghazi Malik, was the founder of the Tughlaq dynasty, which ruled the Delhi Sultanate from 1320 to 1414 CE. Before becoming Sultan, he served as a military commander and frontier governor associated with Dipalpur, an important defensive centre in the north-western region of the Sultanate.

During the reign of Alauddin Khalji (1296–1316 CE), Ghazi Malik gained a reputation for defending the north-western frontier against Mongol invasions. His military experience, knowledge of frontier warfare and support among influential commanders contributed to his later rise.

After Alauddin Khalji's death, the Delhi Sultanate experienced considerable political instability. His successor, Qutb ud-Din Mubarak Shah Khalji, was assassinated in 1320 by Khusrau Khan, who subsequently occupied the throne of Delhi. Khusrau Khan's accession caused resentment among several members of the military and political elite.

Taking advantage of this situation, Ghazi Malik marched from Dipalpur towards Delhi, defeated Khusrau Khan's forces and overthrew him in 1320 CE. He subsequently assumed the royal title Ghiyas ud-Din Tughlaq Shah, establishing the Tughlaq dynasty and ending the Khalji period.

The establishment of the new dynasty represented an attempt to restore central authority after the political disorder following Alauddin Khalji's death.

## 2. Administrative and economic policies

Ghiyas ud-Din Tughlaq was regarded as a practical and relatively **moderate** administrator. Unlike Alauddin Khalji, whose administration relied heavily on strict economic regulations and direct state intervention, Ghiyas sought to balance the financial interests of the Sultanate with the welfare of its subjects.

### Land revenue and agriculture

Agriculture constituted the principal economic foundation of the Delhi Sultanate. Ghiyas ud-Din recognized that excessive taxation and harsh revenue collection could weaken agricultural production and create rural discontent.

He introduced measures intended to provide relief to cultivators and moderate the burden of taxation. Accounts of his reign describe a shift away from some of Alauddin Khalji's stringent land-revenue practices, including the abandonment of systematic measurement as the principal method of assessment in certain areas.

He also sought to prevent oppressive revenue collection and encourage agricultural expansion. Some educational accounts describe a reduction in the land-revenue demand to approximately one-third of the produce, although such numerical generalizations should be treated cautiously because assessment practices varied.

These policies reflected his preference for moderation, agricultural prosperity and stable state revenue, rather than maximizing immediate extraction from the peasantry.
### Military and public administration

As an experienced frontier commander, Ghiyas ud-Din appreciated the importance of maintaining military discipline and an effective army. He attempted to restore order within the Sultanate, improve the functioning of officials and strengthen the authority of the central government.

He also promoted public works and improvements in communication, including roads, bridges and other infrastructure, though specific claims about individual works should be assessed against the historical evidence.

His government is commonly characterized as more cautious and conventional than that of his successor, Muhammad bin Tughlaq.

## 3. Military campaigns and territorial expansion

Although Ghiyas ud-Din ruled for only five years, his reign witnessed important military campaigns intended to restore Delhi's authority over territories that had become increasingly independent during the later Khalji period.

### Warangal campaign (1321–1323 CE)

One of the most significant campaigns was directed against the Kakatiya kingdom of Warangal, ruled by **Prataparudra II**.

Ghiyas ud-Din appointed his son **Jauna Khan**, also known by the title **Ulugh Khan**, to lead the expedition. The first campaign, launched in 1321, failed, and the prince was forced to withdraw.

A second expedition in 1323 achieved success. Warangal was captured, Prataparudra II was taken prisoner, and the Kakatiya kingdom came to an end as an independent political power. Warangal was renamed Sultanpur and incorporated into the administrative structure of the Delhi Sultanate.

This conquest substantially expanded Delhi's authority in the Deccan, although maintaining effective control over such distant territories remained difficult.

### Bengal campaign (1324–1325 CE)

During the later part of his reign, Ghiyas ud-Din personally led an expedition into Bengal, where political disputes had weakened the position of local rulers.

He intervened in the region's succession conflicts and attempted to re-establish the authority of the Delhi Sultanate. The campaign brought important parts of Bengal under his influence, but Delhi's control remained unstable because of the considerable geographical distance and Bengal's challenging riverine environment.

It was while returning from this campaign that Ghiyas ud-Din died in 1325 CE.

One of Ghiyas ud-Din Tughlaq's most significant contributions was the construction of Tughlaqabad, a fortified city near Delhi. Construction began soon after his accession(પદગ્રહણ), around 1320 CE, and the city was designed to function as a secure political and military centre.

The fort was built on elevated, rocky terrain and surrounded by massive stone walls, strengthened with projecting bastions and gateways. Its most distinctive architectural feature was the use of battered walls(જર્જરિત દીવાલો), which slope inward as they rise. These provided structural stability and gave the fort a formidable defensive appearance.

Tughlaq architecture was characterized by strength, simplicity, limited ornamentation and an emphasis on military security. These features contrasted with some of the more decorative buildings associated with the earlier Sultanate.

Ghiyas ud-Din's tomb, located near the fort, is another important example. It has a square chamber surmounted by a dome and is enclosed within a fortified pentagonal complex. Red sandstone, white marble and sloping walls are prominent features.

Tughlaqabad consequently represents an important stage in the development of medieval Indo-Islamic architecture and urban planning.

Ghiyas ud-Din Tughlaq's reign is also associated with tensions between the Sultan and the celebrated **Chishti Sufi saint Nizamuddin Auliya**.

Traditional accounts describe disagreements concerning royal authority, religious independence and construction activities. The Sultan's building of Tughlaqabad became associated with stories involving the saint.

A famous Persian expression connected with this tradition is:
“Hunuz Dilli dur ast” — Delhi is still far away.

According to later Sufi traditions, Nizamuddin Auliya made this remark when informed that Ghiyas ud-Din was returning to Delhi. The Sultan died before reaching his capital.

However, this anecdote belongs primarily to religious and literary tradition. It should not be treated as conclusive historical evidence that the saint predicted or caused the Sultan's death.

## 6. Death at Afghanpur and the succession dispute

In 1325 CE, while returning from his Bengal expedition, Ghiyas ud-Din Tughlaq stopped at Afghanpur, near Delhi. A temporary wooden reception pavilion had been constructed to welcome him.

During the reception, the pavilion collapsed, killing the Sultan and several others. Following his death, his son Jauna Khan ascended the throne with the title Muhammad bin Tughlaq.

The circumstances surrounding the collapse remain a subject of historical debate.

The historian **Ziauddin Barani** described the event as a calamity associated with a thunderbolt, without clearly accusing the prince of murder. In contrast, Ibn Battuta and Isami preserved accounts suggesting that **Jauna Khan might have arranged the collapse to eliminate his father**.

However, Ibn Battuta arrived in India years after the event, and his description relied on information communicated to him rather than direct observation. Historians therefore differ over whether the death resulted from an accident or a deliberate political conspiracy.

The most defensible historical conclusion is that Ghiyas ud-Din died when the pavilion collapsed at Afghanpur in 1325, while the allegation of murder remains disputed.

## 7. Historical significance and critical assessment

Despite his relatively short reign, Ghiyas ud-Din Tughlaq played an important role in the political recovery of the Delhi Sultanate.

His accession ended the instability that followed the decline of the Khalji dynasty. His experience as a military commander helped him restore discipline and strengthen central authority. His military expeditions extended the Sultanate's influence into Warangal and Bengal, although the effective administration of distant territories continued to present serious difficulties.

His administrative policies emphasized agricultural production, moderate revenue demands and greater stability. Through the construction of Tughlaqabad, he also introduced an architectural style that reflected the defensive priorities of a frontier soldier.

Nevertheless, his achievements had limitations. His reign was too brief for his policies to produce enduring institutional changes, and several territorial gains were difficult to consolidate. The disputed circumstances of his death also reveal the political uncertainty associated with succession in the Delhi Sultanate.

Thus, Ghiyas ud-Din Tughlaq may be regarded as a ruler who attempted to restore political stability, military strength and administrative moderation after the decline of the Khaljis. His reign laid the foundations for the more ambitious, but controversial, policies of Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
