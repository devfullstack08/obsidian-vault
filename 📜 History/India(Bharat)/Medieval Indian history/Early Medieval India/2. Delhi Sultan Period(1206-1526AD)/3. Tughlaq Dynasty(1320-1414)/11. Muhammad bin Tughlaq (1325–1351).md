Muhammad bin Tughlaq was one of the most intellectually accomplished, ambitious and controversial rulers of the Delhi Sultanate. He is particularly remembered for his administrative experiments, including transferring the capital to Daulatabad, introducing token currency, increasing land revenue in the Ganga–Yamuna Doab, planning a Khurasan expedition and launching the Qarachil campaign.

Although his policies were often based on ambitious political and economic objectives, several failed because of inadequate implementation, logistical difficulties, environmental crises and opposition from different sections of society. His reign consequently witnessed both the geographical expansion of the Sultanate's ambitions and the gradual fragmentation of its territories.

## 1. Early life and accession to the throne (1325 CE)

Muhammad bin Tughlaq, originally known as Jauna Khan, was the son of Ghiyas ud-Din Tughlaq, the founder of the Tughlaq dynasty. Before becoming Sultan, he gained considerable military and administrative experience under his father and held the title Ulugh Khan.

In 1323 CE, he successfully led the second expedition against the **Kakatiya kingdom of Warangal**, defeating **Prataparudra II** and bringing the region under Delhi's authority.

In 1325 CE, Ghiyas ud-Din died when a wooden pavilion collapsed at Afghanpur, near Tughlaqabad, while returning from his Bengal campaign. Some later chroniclers alleged that Jauna Khan had deliberately arranged the collapse, but this accusation remains historically disputed and cannot be regarded as established fact.

Following his father's death, Jauna Khan ascended the throne as Muhammad bin Tughlaq.

At the beginning of his reign, the Delhi Sultanate controlled or claimed authority over extensive territories **stretching from northern India into the Deccan**. However, governing such a geographically diverse empire required reliable communication, sufficient revenue, military strength and the cooperation of regional elites.

These challenges shaped the Sultan's major administrative experiments.

## 2. The five major experiments of Muhammad bin Tughlaq

### 2.1 Transfer of capital from Delhi to Daulatabad (c. 1327 CE)

Fortifications of Daulatabad

One of Muhammad bin Tughlaq's **most significant administrative decisions** was to shift a major centre of administration from Delhi to Devagiri, which he renamed Daulatabad, in present-day Maharashtra.

The decision was associated with the growing importance of the Deccan and the need to strengthen the Sultanate's control over its southern possessions. Daulatabad's geographical position offered a possible advantage in administering territories distant from Delhi. Its location also made it less vulnerable to Mongol attacks from the north-west.

The Sultan ordered the relocation of important nobles, administrative personnel, religious scholars and sections of Delhi's population. The journey was long and difficult, and contemporary narratives describe considerable suffering among migrants.

Despite arrangements for travel and settlement, the policy caused resentment and disruption. Maintaining communications across such a large empire remained challenging, and the Sultan eventually permitted many migrants to return to Delhi.

The transfer was therefore substantially reversed, although Daulatabad continued to be an important administrative and cultural centre.

Historical significance: The experiment revealed Muhammad bin Tughlaq's desire to integrate northern and southern India administratively. It also contributed to the movement of scholars, religious figures and cultural traditions into the Deccan.

UPSC analytical point: The idea of a more centrally located administrative centre was not necessarily irrational. The principal weaknesses lay in the coercive relocation, human cost and difficulties of execution. The popular statement that Delhi was permanently emptied is an exaggeration.


### 2.2 Introduction of token currency (c. 1329–1330 CE)

![Delhi Sultan Tughlaqs Muhammad bin Tughlaq Takhtgah Delhi Mint Copper Tanka | Virasat Auctions](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/1ovCj_aMgd0J3Rjtanjb7fbinSfsgE7BPmH0C_drJf2vbkv-aBtu9CKDjCvHliuorCkX-k-jGrD0l87ZI-3zUkYij2PW6LCP4P5Syv33USKfgW8thEA5d3EAz3OMY1gZuKhO-f8U0IiYTfo10dTJZ8RDQw0BvV7j55AzsLyBAJ4?purpose=inline)

Token currency was an attempt to circulate coins made from relatively inexpensive metals, particularly **copper and brass**, at a legally assigned value comparable to silver currency.

Muhammad bin Tughlaq sought to expand the currency supply without relying entirely on costly precious metals.

At the time, military expenditure, administration and ambitious territorial policies placed considerable financial demands on the Sultanate. The Sultan introduced copper and brass token coins, which were assigned an official monetary value higher than their intrinsic metal worth.

The experiment was conceptually similar to fiduciary currency systems, in which money derives its accepted value primarily from government authority and public confidence rather than the material from which it is made.

However, the Sultanate lacked sufficient mechanisms to prevent unauthorized coin production. Counterfeiting became widespread because private individuals could imitate coins made from inexpensive metals.

As counterfeit coins entered circulation, public confidence deteriorated. Genuine silver coins were increasingly hoarded, while token coins were used in payments, including revenue payments to the state.

The Sultan eventually withdrew the token currency and arranged for the redemption of tokens in **precious-metal currency**. This imposed substantial financial costs on the treasury.

Historical significance: Muhammad bin Tughlaq demonstrated an understanding of the possibility of state-backed token money. Nevertheless, the experiment failed because the government could not maintain confidence in the currency or effectively control its production.

UPSC analytical point: The failure should be understood as a problem of monetary administration, authentication and institutional capacity, rather than proof that token currency itself was an impossible idea.

### 2.3 Increase in land revenue in the Ganga–Yamuna Doab (late 1320s–early 1330s)

**The Doab** refers to the fertile land located between the Ganga and Yamuna rivers. It was one of the most agriculturally productive regions of northern India and provided an important source of revenue to the Delhi Sultanate.

Muhammad bin Tughlaq increased revenue demands in this region as part of his efforts to strengthen state finances and support military and administrative expenditure.

However, the policy coincided with severe agricultural distress, drought and famine. Many cultivators were unable to meet the increased revenue demands. Harsh collection methods intensified the crisis and contributed to rural unrest.

According to **Ziauddin Barani**, some peasants(ખેડૂતો) abandoned cultivation, destroyed crops or fled their villages to escape revenue officials. Rebellions occurred, and the Sultan attempted to suppress resistance through coercive measures.

The combined effects of environmental hardship and fiscal pressure weakened agricultural production and damaged the relationship between the state and rural society.

Modern historical interpretations also emphasize that famine and ecological conditions played an important role. The crisis cannot be explained solely as the result of one taxation decision.

#### Agricultural recovery measures

After recognizing the seriousness of agricultural distress, Muhammad bin Tughlaq introduced measures intended to revive cultivation and increase food production.

He provided **takkavi (taqavi) loans** to peasants for agricultural purposes, including the purchase of seeds, cattle and necessary equipment.

He also established the **Diwan-i-Amir-i-Kohi**, an agricultural department intended to bring additional land under cultivation and promote agricultural improvement through government assistance.

The scheme involved experimental cultivation and expenditure on agricultural development. However, its results were limited by poor implementation, corruption, inadequate supervision and environmental difficulties.

Historical significance: These initiatives show that Muhammad bin Tughlaq recognized the importance of agricultural investment and state assistance to cultivators. His agrarian policy therefore combined fiscal coercion with attempts at agricultural reconstruction.

UPSC analytical point: The Doab crisis illustrates the danger of increasing revenue demands without adequately considering agricultural conditions and the capacity of peasants to pay.

### 2.4 Khurasan expedition — proposed foreign conquest

Muhammad bin Tughlaq also contemplated military expansion towards Khurasan, a historical region covering parts of present-day Iran, Afghanistan and neighbouring Central Asian territories.

Contemporary accounts suggest that political instability in the region encouraged the Sultan to consider an expedition beyond India's north-western frontiers.

He reportedly recruited and maintained a large army for the proposed campaign. Barani gives the often-cited figure of approximately 370,000 soldiers, although medieval military figures should not automatically be accepted as exact.

The troops received salaries and preparations were made for the expedition. However, changing political circumstances and strategic considerations resulted in the abandonment of the plan before the intended foreign conquest took place.

The disbandment of the recruited army caused financial losses and placed additional pressure on the treasury.

Historical significance: The Khurasan project demonstrates Muhammad bin Tughlaq's ambition to extend his political influence beyond the Indian subcontinent.

UPSC analytical point: The proposal reveals the mismatch between imperial ambition and the resources required for sustained overseas or transregional military operations.

### 2.5 Qarachil expedition — Himalayan campaign (c. 1337–1338 CE)

Muhammad bin Tughlaq organized a military expedition into the Himalayan region, often associated with Qarachil (Karachil) and commonly identified with parts of the Kumaon region.

The exact geographical scope and objective of the campaign remain debated. It is generally connected with efforts to assert political influence over strategically important hill territories and routes.

The Sultan dispatched a substantial army under the command of Khusrau Malik, according to commonly cited historical accounts.

Although the invading forces initially achieved some success, the expedition encountered severe geographical and logistical problems.

Steep mountains, difficult routes, inadequate supplies, heavy rainfall and local resistance weakened the army. Large numbers of soldiers reportedly died, and the campaign ended in disaster.

The force suffered a major defeat, and only a small proportion reportedly returned.

Historical significance: The campaign revealed the limitations of conventional military operations in difficult mountain environments.

UPSC analytical point: The disaster demonstrates the importance of geographical knowledge, logistical preparation and the adaptation of military strategy to local terrain.

## 3. Revolts and disintegration of the Delhi Sultanate

One of the most important developments during Muhammad bin Tughlaq's reign was the gradual weakening of Delhi's authority over its distant provinces.

Although the Sultanate had achieved considerable territorial expansion under Alauddin Khalji and the early Tughlaqs, maintaining effective control over such a large empire required substantial military expenditure, communications infrastructure and cooperation from local governors.

Muhammad bin Tughlaq's ambitious policies, revenue difficulties, frequent military expeditions and harsh treatment of rebellious officials contributed to growing political instability.

As the central government's resources were increasingly strained, several provincial governors and regional political powers asserted their independence.

### 3.1 Emergence of the Madurai Sultanate (1335 CE)

In the southern region of **Ma'bar**, corresponding broadly to parts of present-day Tamil Nadu, the governor **Jalaluddin Ahsan Shah** declared independence from Delhi around 1335 CE.

He established the Madurai Sultanate, with Madurai as its political centre.

Muhammad bin Tughlaq attempted to restore his authority in the south, but disease, logistical difficulties and other crises prevented the successful reconquest of the region.

The establishment of the Madurai Sultanate marked an important stage in the loss of Delhi's direct control over the far south.

The decline of the Delhi Sultanate's authority in the Deccan and southern India created conditions favourable for the emergence of new regional powers.

The Vijayanagara Empire is traditionally dated to 1336 CE and associated with the brothers **Harihara I** and **Bukka Raya I**, founders of the **Sangama dynasty**.

The kingdom developed around the **Tungabhadra River** region in present-day Karnataka, with Vijayanagara (Hampi) becoming its celebrated imperial capital.

Over time, Vijayanagara emerged as one of the most powerful kingdoms in southern India and played an important role in regional politics, commerce, religious patronage and the development of art and architecture.

Its foundation should be understood within the wider process of regional state formation, rather than as a direct consequence of a single policy of Muhammad bin Tughlaq.

In 1347 CE, a rebellion among influential nobles and military leaders in the Deccan resulted in the establishment of the **Bahmani Sultanate**.

Its founder was **Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah**, also known as **Hasan Gangu** or **Zafar Khan** in historical accounts.

Gulbarga, renamed Ahsanabad, became the initial capital of the new kingdom.

The rise of the Bahmani Sultanate represented a major loss of political authority for Delhi in the Deccan. It also created an independent regional power that subsequently competed with Vijayanagara for control of territories, resources and strategic routes.

The emergence of both the Bahmani and Vijayanagara kingdoms marked a major transformation in the political geography of medieval southern India.

### Other significant rebellions

Muhammad bin Tughlaq also faced revolts in Bengal, Gujarat and other parts of the Sultanate. The rebellion of Ain-ul-Mulk, an important official associated with Awadh, further illustrated tensions between the Sultan and his nobility.

The repeated use of military force to suppress provincial resistance placed growing pressure on the treasury and weakened the administration.

UPSC analytical point: The fragmentation of the Sultanate resulted from the interaction of geographical overextension, fiscal difficulties, provincial ambitions and failures of political integration. It cannot be attributed exclusively to the five administrative experiments.

## 4. Intellectual personality and religious policy

Muhammad bin Tughlaq was widely recognized for his intellectual interests. Contemporary accounts describe his knowledge of philosophy, logic, mathematics, medicine, literature and religious learning.

He participated in scholarly discussions and displayed considerable interest in rational inquiry. His intellectual accomplishments distinguished him from several other rulers of the Delhi Sultanate.

However, his personality has been interpreted very differently by historians.

On the one hand, he was an intellectually ambitious ruler who attempted innovations in monetary administration, agriculture and imperial governance. On the other, he was frequently criticized for harsh punishments, sudden policy decisions and an inability to accommodate opposition.

His religious policy was also complex. Although personally committed to Islam, he interacted with scholars and religious figures from different traditions, including Jain intellectuals. Nevertheless, episodes of religious coercion and severe treatment of certain Muslim religious figures also appear in historical accounts.

Therefore, describing him simply as either a religiously tolerant ruler or a religious fanatic would be an oversimplification.

UPSC analytical point: Muhammad bin Tughlaq's reign demonstrates that intellectual ability and administrative effectiveness are not necessarily the same. A ruler may possess advanced ideas yet fail to build the institutions necessary to implement them successfully.

## 5. Foreign relations and diplomatic contacts

Muhammad bin Tughlaq maintained diplomatic contacts beyond the Indian subcontinent, reflecting the Sultanate's participation in wider Asian political and commercial networks.

One important connection was with China during the **Yuan dynasty**. The famous **Moroccan traveller Ibn Battuta**, who served at Muhammad bin Tughlaq's court, was appointed to lead a diplomatic mission to China in 1342.

These contacts indicate that Delhi remained connected with major political and commercial centres across Asia despite its growing internal problems.

The Sultan's interest in Khurasan similarly reflected his awareness of political developments beyond India's north-western frontiers.

## 6. Death at Thatta (1351 CE)

During the later years of his reign, Muhammad bin Tughlaq faced continuing rebellions in western India, especially in Gujarat and Sindh.

One of the important rebels was **Taghi**, whose activities prompted the Sultan to undertake a military campaign.

While pursuing Taghi towards the lower Indus region, Muhammad bin Tughlaq became **ill and died in March 1351 CE**, near **Thatta in present-day Sindh, Pakistan**.

His cousin **Firoz Shah Tughlaq** was subsequently selected as Sultan and ruled from 1351 to 1388 CE.

Thus ended the twenty-six-year reign of one of the Delhi Sultanate's most ambitious rulers, whose policies sought to strengthen imperial authority but contributed, along with wider structural problems, to political instability.

## 7. Contemporary historians and historical interpretation

Two important sources for understanding Muhammad bin Tughlaq are Ziauddin Barani and Ibn Battuta.

| Historian       | Work                 | Historical importance                                                                                                                          |
| --------------- | -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ziauddin Barani | Tarikh-i-Firuz Shahi | Describes the administrative experiments, political problems, revolts and financial difficulties of the reign                                  |
| Ibn Battuta     | Rihla                | Describes court life, cities, society, administration and the Sultan's personality from the perspective of a traveller who served at his court |
| Isami           | Futuh-us-Salatin     | Provides another account of Sultanate politics, especially events associated with the Deccan and the Tughlaq rulers                            |

Barani's writings are especially valuable because of his proximity to the political establishment. However, his religious and aristocratic outlook influenced his judgments, and his criticism should be examined in that context.

Ibn Battuta reached the Indian subcontinent in 1333 CE and served as qazi in Delhi. His observations provide valuable information about the Sultan's court and contemporary society. Nevertheless, he was not an eyewitness to all the events he described.

Isami's account provides an additional perspective, particularly on the political consequences of Tughlaq policies in the Deccan.

A careful historical interpretation requires comparing these sources rather than accepting every anecdote literally.

## 8. Critical assessment of Muhammad bin Tughlaq

Muhammad bin Tughlaq occupies an unusual position in medieval Indian history because his reign combined intellectual innovation with severe administrative instability.

His capital-transfer policy aimed to address the difficulty of governing a geographically extensive empire. The token-currency experiment attempted monetary innovation. His agricultural schemes reflected concern for increasing cultivation and improving state revenue. His foreign and Himalayan projects demonstrated wide-ranging imperial ambitions.

However, the principal weakness of his administration was the gap between policy objectives and practical implementation.

His decisions frequently underestimated geographical difficulties, social resistance, fiscal limitations and institutional weaknesses. Coercive enforcement further alienated peasants, nobles and provincial governors.

At the same time, contemporary chroniclers sometimes emphasized his failures while giving comparatively less attention to the constructive objectives behind his decisions.

Therefore, Muhammad bin Tughlaq should not simply be characterized as an irrational or unsuccessful ruler. He was an intellectually capable and ambitious sovereign whose ambitious policies often exceeded the administrative and financial capabilities of his state.

His reign provides an important historical example of the limitations of centralized authority when political ambition is not matched by administrative capacity, fiscal stability and social cooperation.

## 11. Model UPSC Mains answer

Question: “Muhammad bin Tughlaq was a ruler whose ambitious ideas failed because of inadequate administrative preparation.” Critically examine. (250 words)

Muhammad bin Tughlaq (1325–1351 CE) was among the most intellectually accomplished and ambitious rulers of the Delhi Sultanate. His reign was characterized by innovative administrative experiments intended to strengthen imperial authority, improve revenue and expand territorial influence. However, several policies failed because their implementation exceeded the institutional capabilities of the Sultanate.

The transfer of the administrative centre from Delhi to Daulatabad aimed to improve control over the Deccan and reduce vulnerability to north-western invasions. Nevertheless, coercive relocation, logistical difficulties and popular resistance forced a partial reversal.

Similarly, the introduction of copper and brass token currency represented an innovative monetary experiment. However, widespread counterfeiting and insufficient safeguards undermined public confidence, compelling its withdrawal.

The increase in land revenue in the Ganga–Yamuna Doab coincided with famine and agricultural distress, provoking peasant resistance. Subsequent agricultural loans and the Diwan-i-Amir-i-Kohi demonstrated attempts at recovery but yielded limited results.

The abandoned Khurasan project and disastrous Qarachil expedition further exposed the limitations of imperial ambition without adequate financial and logistical preparation.

Meanwhile, the emergence of independent powers such as Madurai, Vijayanagara and the Bahmani Sultanate reflected the weakening of central authority.

Nevertheless, dismissing Muhammad bin Tughlaq as irrational overlooks the strategic and economic objectives behind his policies. Environmental crises, provincial resistance and structural limitations also contributed to their failure.

Thus, his reign illustrates the necessity of combining innovative governance with institutional capacity, administrative consultation, fiscal prudence and effective implementation.

| --- | --- | --- |
| 1335 | **Madurai** | **Jalaluddin Ahsan Shah** makes the south independent. This is not Malik Kafur's raid of 1311. |
| 1336 | **Vijayanagara** | **Harihara** and **Bukka** of the Sangama house, on the Tungabhadra. |
| 1347 | **Bahmani** | **Alauddin Hasan Bahman Shah**. The capital settles at **Gulbarga**. |

Delhi's direct hold on the far south ends in this reign. The founding of Vijayanagara is not a decree of the sultan.

## Death and the two writers

He died in **1351** at **Thatta** in Sindh, on campaign against the rebel **Taghi**. His cousin **Firoz Shah Tughlaq** succeeded. Firoz's reign is the next chapter.

**Ziauddin Barani**, *Tarikh-i-Firuz Shahi*, wrote under Firoz and is hostile. **Ibn Battuta** was in Delhi from about **1333 to 1342**, served as qazi, and wrote the *Rihla*. He describes the court he saw. He was not present for the move of 1327 or the death of 1325.

## Traps

| If you are about to write this | Write this instead |
| --- | --- |
| He issued paper money | A copper token, valued as a silver tanka, 1329–1330. |
| He emptied Delhi and never let it recover | The shift was ordered in 1327. A return was later allowed. |
| Khurasan and Qarachil are one campaign | Khurasan was an army raised and then disbanded. Qarachil was the hill expedition that was destroyed. |
| The diwan-i-amir-kohi caused the famine | It was set up after the Doab tax and the famine. |
| He founded Vijayanagara | Harihara and Bukka, 1336, as Delhi's hold failed. |
| Ibn Battuta watched the capital move | He reached Delhi about 1333. The move was 1327. |
| He died in Delhi | Thatta, Sindh, 1351. |
| He proved he killed his father | The fact to write is Afghanpur, 1325, the pavilion. That belongs to the previous note. |

## One answer

Muhammad bin Tughlaq, formerly Jauna Khan, succeeded when the pavilion at Afghanpur collapsed in 1325. His exam plans are the shift of the capital to Daulatabad in 1327, the copper token of 1329–1330, a sharp tax increase in the Doab during famine, a Khurasan army that was paid and disbanded, and a Qarachil expedition destroyed in the hills. Madurai broke away in 1335, Vijayanagara was founded in 1336, and the Bahmani kingdom in 1347. He died at Thatta in 1351, and Firoz Shah Tughlaq succeeded.
