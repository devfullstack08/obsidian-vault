Firoz Shah Tughlaq was the third important ruler of the Tughlaq dynasty and one of the longest-reigning Sultans of Delhi. His reign represented a major change in the political and administrative direction of the Delhi Sultanate.

Unlike his predecessor Muhammad bin Tughlaq, who pursued ambitious military expansion and experimental economic policies, Firoz Shah generally preferred political reconciliation, administrative moderation, agricultural development and public welfare.

However, his policy of granting hereditary privileges to nobles and soldiers, together with his dependence on religious authorities and a large slave establishment, weakened the long-term foundations of centralized government.

His reign therefore presents an important historical contrast: relative stability and prosperity during his lifetime, followed by rapid political fragmentation after his death.

## 1. Early life and accession to the throne (1351 CE)

Firoz Shah Tughlaq belonged to the ruling Tughlaq family and was a cousin of Muhammad bin Tughlaq. **He was the son of Rajab, a brother of Ghiyas ud-Din Tughlaq**.

His accession took place during a period of serious political uncertainty.

In March 1351, Muhammad bin Tughlaq died in Sindh while campaigning against the rebel Taghi near Thatta. His death left the Sultanate without an immediately secure succession arrangement.

Influential nobles and military commanders accompanying the army selected Firoz Shah as the new Sultan. His accession received support from important religious and political figures, including the influential scholar and official **Khan-i-Jahan Maqbul.**

Firoz Shah initially showed reluctance(અનિચ્છા) according to some accounts, but ultimately accepted the throne.

He inherited a weakened empire. Bengal had asserted independence, the Bahmani Sultanate controlled much of the Deccan, Vijayanagara had emerged as a major southern power, and repeated rebellions had strained the central treasury.

Recognizing these difficulties, Firoz Shah avoided many of the ambitious policies associated with his predecessor. Instead, he sought to restore stability through conciliation and cooperation with the ruling elite.

Historical significance: His accession marked a transition from Muhammad bin Tughlaq's expansive imperial ambitions towards a more limited and conservative form of governance.

## 2. Administrative reforms and political policy

### Reconciliation with nobles and religious scholars

Firoz Shah believed that maintaining the **loyalty of the nobility** and **religious establishment** was essential for political stability.

Under Muhammad bin Tughlaq, several nobles and officials had faced severe punishments, confiscations and uncertain political conditions. Firoz Shah attempted to reduce these tensions by restoring privileges, distributing rewards and adopting a more conciliatory policy towards influential groups.

He relied heavily on the support of the ulama, or Islamic religious scholars, and presented his rule as consistent with Islamic legal principles.

This strategy reduced opposition among sections of the political elite and helped create a relatively peaceful environment during much of his reign.

However, it also made the Sultan increasingly dependent on the cooperation of powerful nobles and hereditary officeholders.

### Hereditary appointments and the Iqta system

One of his most consequential policies was the extensive recognition of hereditary claims to administrative offices, military service positions and revenue assignments.

Under the Iqta system, land-revenue rights from designated territories were assigned to officials and military commanders in return for administrative or military service.

Earlier Delhi Sultans had generally sought to preserve the authority to transfer, replace or dismiss these officials. Firoz Shah, however, increasingly permitted positions and associated privileges to pass from fathers to sons or other relatives.

This policy pleased nobles and military officers because it gave their families greater financial security and political influence.

Nevertheless, it weakened the principle that offices and revenue assignments depended primarily upon the Sultan's appointment.

Over time, powerful local families gained a stronger position, and the central government found it more difficult to exercise effective control over provincial administration.

UPSC analytical point: Hereditary privileges helped Firoz Shah secure immediate political loyalty but contributed to the erosion of central authority in the long run.

### Military administration

Firoz Shah also introduced concessions(છૂટછાટો) within the military system.

He allowed certain military appointments and privileges to become hereditary and relaxed aspects of the rigorous military control associated with Alauddin Khalji.

Although these measures reduced dissatisfaction among soldiers, they also weakened discipline and encouraged dependence on inherited positions rather than demonstrated military capability.

Consequently, the army became less effective as an instrument of centralized authority, particularly during the political crises following his death.

## 3. Revenue and taxation reforms

Firoz Shah Tughlaq is remembered for simplifying aspects of the revenue system and presenting his taxation policies as consistent with Islamic law.

He stated that he abolished numerous additional taxes and cesses that he considered unlawful.

He recognized four major categories of taxation:

|Tax|Meaning|
|---|---|
|Kharaj|Land revenue, particularly from agricultural land|
|Khams / Khums|The state's prescribed share of war booty|
|Jizya|A tax imposed on eligible non-Muslim subjects|
|Zakat|A religious levy associated with Muslim wealth and charitable obligations|

The distinction between these taxes is important because they had different legal and economic purposes. They should not all be treated as ordinary taxes on agricultural production.

### Jizya on Brahmins

Firoz Shah is particularly known for extending the collection of jizya to Brahmins, who had traditionally enjoyed exemption under earlier practices in the Delhi Sultanate.

The policy reflected his desire to align taxation with the interpretation of Islamic law supported by the religious scholars around his court.

Although the Sultan represented this as a religious duty, the measure increased the burden on affected Brahmin communities and demonstrates the more explicitly orthodox character of his administration.

### Irrigation tax — Haq-i-Sharb

Firoz Shah introduced an **irrigation-water charge** commonly known as **Haq-i-Sharb.**

The charge was associated with agricultural land benefiting from state-constructed irrigation facilities and is generally described as approximately **one-tenth of the produce**.

This created an additional source of revenue while encouraging the use of irrigation infrastructure.

It is important to distinguish this water charge from the four principal taxes emphasized in the Sultan's account of religiously sanctioned taxation.

UPSC analytical point: His taxation policy combined reductions in certain miscellaneous demands with the continuation of land revenue, religious taxation and charges for irrigation facilities.

One of the most important achievements of Firoz Shah Tughlaq was his emphasis on **agricultural development through irrigation**.

He recognized that agricultural prosperity was essential for a stable revenue system. In northern India, cultivation depended heavily on monsoon rainfall, and shortages of water could produce crop failures, famine and political unrest.

To reduce this dependence, he commissioned the construction and improvement of canals, reservoirs, wells and other irrigation works.

His best-known irrigation undertaking connected the Yamuna River with the Hisar region, helping supply water to an otherwise relatively dry area.

Other canal works drew upon the Sutlej and Ghaggar river systems, according to historical accounts.

The expansion of irrigation increased the potential area under cultivation, encouraged the development of settlements and helped improve agricultural productivity in certain regions.

His policy contrasted with Muhammad bin Tughlaq's agricultural experiments because it placed considerable emphasis on durable infrastructure rather than relying primarily on revenue changes and direct cultivation projects.

Nevertheless, some historical accounts contain exaggerated numerical descriptions of the scale of these works, and not every canal remained functional in later centuries.

Historical significance: The irrigation system illustrates the growing recognition of public infrastructure as a means of supporting agricultural production and securing long-term state revenue.

## 5. Public welfare and charitable institutions

Firoz Shah Tughlaq introduced several institutions designed to provide relief and assistance to selected sections of society.

His welfare initiatives included charitable assistance, medical facilities, religious endowments and provisions for dependants.

### Diwan-i-Khairat — Department of charity

Firoz Shah established the **Diwan-i-Khairat** to administer charitable assistance.

The department is especially associated with financial support for the marriages of poor Muslim women and assistance to other eligible beneficiaries.

The institution reflected the Sultan's interest in combining public welfare with religiously motivated charity.

### Diwan-i-Bandagan — Department of slaves

Another important institution was the Diwan-i-Bandagan, responsible for managing the Sultan's large establishment of slaves.

Slaves were employed in domestic service, administration, craftsmanship, agricultural activities and other state functions.

The contemporary chronicler **Shams-i-Siraj Afif** reported that the number of slaves associated with the establishment reached approximately 180,000.

This figure should be treated as a chronicler's estimate rather than a precisely verified population count.

Although some slaves received training and financial support, the institution was fundamentally based upon slavery and should not be represented as a modern welfare institution.

### Dar-ul-Shifa — Hospital

Firoz Shah also established the **Dar-ul-Shifa**, a hospital where medical treatment was provided, particularly to the poor and needy.

The hospital was associated with state-supported medical care and reflected the Sultan's interest in charitable and public-service institutions.

These measures helped strengthen the reputation of Firoz Shah as a ruler concerned with welfare, although their benefits were not distributed equally across all social and religious communities.

## 6. Military campaigns and foreign policy

Firoz Shah inherited a Sultanate that had already lost control over large territories. Unlike his predecessor, he did not attempt to reconquer the whole of southern India.

His military policy concentrated on asserting authority over selected northern and eastern regions, obtaining tribute and maintaining political stability.

### 6.1 Bengal expeditions (1353–1354 and 1359–1360)

Firoz Shah undertook two major military expeditions into Bengal, which had become effectively independent under regional rulers.

During the first expedition, he confronted **Shamsuddin Ilyas Shah**, founder of the **Ilyas Shahi dynasty of Bengal**. Ilyas Shah withdrew into the fortified city of **Ekdala**, where he resisted the Delhi army.

Firoz Shah failed to secure a decisive conquest and eventually withdrew.

A second expedition was launched during the rule of Sikandar Shah, the successor of Ilyas Shah. Despite another siege of Ekdala, Bengal continued to maintain its independence.

The expeditions demonstrated that Delhi still possessed substantial military resources, but was increasingly unable to incorporate distant territories into a stable administrative structure.

Outcome: Bengal was not permanently recovered. Descriptions of tribute or negotiated submission should not be confused with effective annexation.

### 6.2 Jajnagar expedition — Odisha (c. 1360–1361)

Following his Bengal campaign, Firoz Shah launched an expedition into Jajnagar, in present-day Odisha, which was ruled by the **Eastern Ganga dynasty**.

The campaign involved military action against regional forces and attacks on religious establishments. Accounts describe plunder and damage associated with the **Jagannath temple at Puri**.

The Sultan obtained booty and asserted his military authority, but did not establish lasting administrative control over Odisha.

Outcome: A raid and temporary assertion of power, not the permanent annexation of Odisha.

### 6.3 Nagarkot expedition — Kangra (c. 1360–1361)

![Explore ancient stone Kangra fortress in lush greenery, bathed in sunlight](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/UZpm4IdNTgrcvJBmukpwPtywDuPw8ocjeX91LgVXM9xaqtqWKBbZZmwfN8n-4PWbcrjoODLVTXiuE6v1BKdXF5hFFQ6iammlWLGp_kMzGMLKOiwAY85QYL-DqScuArxG7dAeJNWc1JM1ipnuJJdMPa9zXDH06bx1csajin3QpwPy-gsxl0CbcrVorhNvJ2do?purpose=inline)

Nagarkot = present-day Kangra, Himachal Pradesh.

The fortress of Nagarkot was an important strategic centre in the Himalayan foothills and had previously attracted the attention of Mahmud of Ghazni in 1009 CE.

Firoz Shah undertook an expedition against Nagarkot and reportedly besieged the fort for several months.

The local ruler eventually submitted to the Sultan, who accepted his submission rather than establishing direct administrative control over the entire region.

Accounts of the expedition also describe the acquisition of Sanskrit manuscripts, some of which were translated into Persian with royal encouragement.

Historical significance: The campaign reflects the importance of Himalayan frontier territories, political tribute and cultural exchanges during the Sultanate period.

### 6.4 Thatta expedition — Sindh (c. 1365–1367)

Firoz Shah's campaign against Thatta in Sindh was among the most difficult military operations of his reign.

He confronted the local Samma rulers, but his forces suffered from shortages of supplies, difficult terrain, disease and logistical problems in the Indus region.

The Sultan withdrew temporarily towards Gujarat, reorganized his army and subsequently returned to the region.

The campaign eventually ended with a negotiated settlement involving submission or recognition of Delhi's overlordship, rather than durable direct administration.

Historical significance: The campaign demonstrated the difficulties involved in projecting military power into distant territories and maintaining effective supply lines.

### 6.5 Policy towards the Deccan

Firoz Shah made no major attempt to recover the territories controlled by the Bahmani Sultanate and the Vijayanagara Empire.

His decision reflected a relatively realistic appreciation of Delhi's reduced financial and military resources.

However, the absence of an aggressive reconquest policy also confirmed that the Delhi Sultanate was no longer capable of exercising the broad territorial authority associated with its earlier expansion.

UPSC analytical point: Firoz Shah's military policy favoured limited objectives and conciliation, but the repeated inability to secure lasting territorial gains revealed the structural weakness of the Sultanate.

## 7. Architecture, cities and public works

Firoz Shah Tughlaq was an enthusiastic builder who founded new settlements, commissioned public infrastructure and restored several older monuments.

His building activities reflected the political importance of urban development, administrative control, religious patronage and water management.

### 7.1 Foundation of Firozabad and Firoz Shah Kotla

Firoz Shah established Firozabad in Delhi around 1354 CE, developing another important urban centre of the Delhi Sultanate.

Its fortified palace complex, today known as Firoz Shah Kotla, included royal buildings, a congregational mosque, water facilities and defensive structures.

The location near the Yamuna provided access to water and communication routes.

The architecture used comparatively simple masonry, arches and practical construction techniques rather than elaborate decoration.

Meerut Ashokan pillar at Delhi Ridge

One of Firoz Shah's most remarkable activities was the transportation of two ancient stone pillars originally erected during the reign of Emperor Ashoka (3rd century BCE).

- **The Topra pillar,** from present-day Haryana, was transported to Firozabad and erected on a specially constructed platform in the Firoz Shah Kotla complex.
    
- **The Meerut pillar**, from present-day Uttar Pradesh, was transported to Delhi and erected on the Ridge, near his hunting palace.
    

The enormous sandstone pillars were moved using elaborate arrangements involving labourers, carts, protective materials and river transport.

Their transportation illustrates both the engineering resources available to the Sultanate and the interest of Firoz Shah in incorporating prestigious ancient monuments into his royal landscape.

Important distinction: Firoz Shah did not construct these pillars or inscribe Ashoka's edicts on them. They originated in the Mauryan period, many centuries before his reign.

Their Brahmi inscriptions were not successfully deciphered until the nineteenth century, especially through the work of James Prinsep in 1837.

### 7.4 Restoration of monuments

Firoz Shah repaired several existing monuments in Delhi, including the **Qutb Minar,** which had suffered lightning damage.

He also undertook restoration and construction activities at **Hauz Khas**, an important water reservoir originally associated with Alauddin Khalji.

He developed educational buildings and religious structures around the reservoir, including a madrasa.

### 7.5 Tomb of Firoz Shah Tughlaq

Real photograph of Firoz Shah Tughlaq's tomb at Hauz Khas, Delhi.

His tomb is located within the Hauz Khas complex in Delhi.

It is an important example of Tughlaq-period architecture, characterized by relatively restrained decoration, strong masonry and a domed structure.

The tomb forms part of a larger complex associated with religious learning, water management and royal patronage.

UPSC analytical point: Firoz Shah's building programme combined political symbolism with practical public infrastructure. His projects demonstrate that the architecture of the Delhi Sultanate was not limited to forts and religious monuments but also included canals, reservoirs, settlements and educational institutions.

## 8. Religious policy and relations with the Ulama

Firoz Shah Tughlaq's religious policy was substantially more orthodox than that of Muhammad bin Tughlaq.

He sought legitimacy through close cooperation with the ulama, the scholars responsible for interpreting Islamic law. He presented the Shariat as an important foundation of public administration and introduced measures that he considered consistent with religious obligations.

His religious policies included the imposition of jizya on Brahmins, patronage of Islamic scholars and religious institutions, and restrictions on certain non-Islamic religious practices.

In his own account, Futuhat-i-Firoz Shahi, he described actions against some temples and religious activities, presenting them as achievements of his reign.

These policies strengthened his relationship with sections of the orthodox religious establishment but also contributed to religious discrimination against affected non-Muslim communities.

At the same time, his reign witnessed the translation of some Sanskrit works into Persian and the preservation or reuse of ancient monuments such as the Ashokan pillars.

This combination illustrates the complexity of his rule: architectural and scholarly patronage coexisted with exclusionary religious policies.

UPSC analytical point: Firoz Shah's policies reveal how religious orthodoxy could serve as a source of political legitimacy while also restricting the inclusive character of state institutions.

## 9. Education and literary contributions

Firoz Shah patronized scholars, historians, theologians and educational institutions.

He supported the establishment and maintenance of madrasas, including an important institution at Hauz Khas in Delhi.

Religious education, Persian literature, Islamic jurisprudence and administrative learning received royal support.

Some Sanskrit manuscripts acquired during his campaigns were also translated into Persian, contributing to exchanges between intellectual traditions.

## 10. Death and decline of the Tughlaq dynasty (1388 CE)

Firoz Shah Tughlaq died in September 1388 CE, after approximately thirty-seven years on the throne.

Although his long reign had provided a measure of political stability, his death exposed serious structural weaknesses within the Delhi Sultanate.

The recognition of hereditary offices and revenue assignments had strengthened powerful nobles and local families. Military organization had weakened, and the large slave establishment became another influential group within court politics.

Disputes over succession contributed to instability. His grandson, Ghiyas ud-Din Tughlaq II, succeeded him but was overthrown in 1389. Rival claimants and competing noble factions subsequently struggled for control of Delhi.

During the following decade, the Sultanate fragmented further as provincial rulers asserted their independence.

In 1398 CE, the Central Asian conqueror Timur invaded northern India and sacked Delhi, devastating an already weakened political centre.

The Tughlaq dynasty continued nominally until 1414 CE, when Khizr Khan established the Sayyid dynasty.

Thus, Firoz Shah's death marked the beginning of a particularly rapid phase of political disintegration, although the wider weakening of the Sultanate had begun before his reign.

## 11. Critical assessment of his reign

Firoz Shah Tughlaq occupies an important position in the history of the Delhi Sultanate because his reign combined administrative reconstruction and public welfare with long-term institutional weaknesses.

His emphasis on irrigation canals, agricultural improvement, charitable institutions, hospitals, new cities and restoration of public buildings represented significant contributions to the economy and infrastructure.

His conciliatory policies towards nobles and religious scholars helped reduce immediate political tensions and enabled him to maintain a relatively stable government for nearly four decades.

However, his achievements were accompanied by serious limitations.

The extensive recognition of hereditary military and administrative privileges weakened the Sultan's ability to appoint officials according to merit or remove ineffective personnel.

The large slave establishment imposed financial and political burdens. Dependence on powerful nobles reduced the authority of the central government, while religious orthodoxy contributed to discrimination against certain communities.

His military campaigns generally failed to restore Delhi's control over Bengal and the Deccan, confirming the reduced territorial reach of the Sultanate.

Thus, Firoz Shah can be regarded as a ruler who succeeded in creating relative peace and prosperity during his lifetime but failed to preserve the institutional strength necessary for long-term political stability.

## 14. Model UPSC Mains answer

Question: “Firoz Shah Tughlaq's reign was marked by administrative welfare and political conciliation, but his policies weakened the long-term foundations of the Delhi Sultanate.” Critically examine. (250 words)

### Introduction

Firoz Shah Tughlaq (1351–1388 CE) succeeded Muhammad bin Tughlaq during a period of political instability and territorial fragmentation. His reign represented a shift from ambitious imperial expansion towards administrative reconciliation, public welfare and religious orthodoxy.

### Body

Administrative and economic achievements: Firoz Shah introduced measures to moderate taxation, abolished several additional cesses and encouraged agricultural development through irrigation canals. His Yamuna–Hisar canal supported cultivation and settlement. He established charitable institutions such as the Diwan-i-Khairat, hospitals such as Dar-ul-Shifa and educational centres.

Political conciliation: He restored privileges to nobles and strengthened relations with the ulama. These measures reduced political opposition and helped maintain relative stability during his long reign.

Institutional weaknesses: However, making offices, military appointments and revenue assignments increasingly hereditary undermined merit-based administration and weakened central control. The expansion of the slave establishment created further financial and political burdens.

Religious policy: His orthodox religious measures, particularly the extension of jizya to Brahmins, strengthened ties with religious scholars but contributed to discrimination.

Military limitations: His Bengal expeditions failed to achieve permanent annexation, while independent Deccan kingdoms remained outside Delhi's authority.

### Conclusion

Firoz Shah's reign demonstrated the effectiveness of conciliation and public works in securing immediate stability. Nevertheless, hereditary privileges, military weakness and dependence on powerful elites undermined centralized authority. Thus, his policies produced short-term prosperity but contributed to the Sultanate's vulnerability after 1388.

## 15. One-paragraph revision note

Firoz Shah Tughlaq (1351–1388 CE), cousin of Muhammad bin Tughlaq, was selected as Sultan by nobles near Thatta in 1351. He pursued conciliation with nobles and the ulama, promoted hereditary appointments and introduced measures intended to moderate taxation.
