## Noble revolts and the fall of the dynasty

Ibrahim Lodi succeeded his father Sikandar in 1517 CE. He inherited a relatively strong territorial kingdom, but relations between the monarchy and Afghan chiefs soon deteriorated.

### Conflict with the Afghan nobility

Problem: Powerful Afghan chiefs considered their political privileges essential to the structure of Lodi rule.

Ibrahim attempted to assert stronger royal authority, demanded stricter obedience and punished nobles who challenged his decisions.

His policies conflicted with the more conciliatory political traditions associated with Bahlul Lodi.

Action: Ibrahim removed or imprisoned some nobles, sought greater personal control over administrative and military decisions, and attempted to weaken independent aristocratic factions.

Result: Important Afghan nobles became hostile to his rule, producing rebellions and political instability.

UPSC significance: The struggle between Ibrahim and the Afghan aristocracy reflected a conflict between centralized monarchy and the political independence of hereditary military elites.

### Rebellions in Punjab and eastern India

Problem: Ibrahim's relations with important governors and members of the ruling family deteriorated(બગડી ગયેલું).

His brother **Jalal Khan Lodi** became involved in a succession struggle, while several provincial nobles challenged the Sultan.

In Punjab, **Daulat Khan Lodi**, the governor of Lahore, increasingly resisted Ibrahim's authority.

Other dissatisfied Afghan nobles and royal relatives also sought outside support against the Sultan.

Action: Ibrahim attempted to suppress rebellions through military force and the punishment of opponents.

Result: Internal divisions weakened the political unity and military effectiveness of the Sultanate.

This instability created an opportunity for Babur, the Timurid ruler of Kabul, to intervene in northern Indian politics.

### Babur's invasion and the First Battle of Panipat (1526)

Problem: Dissatisfied nobles, including **Daulat Khan Lodi**, encouraged Babur's intervention. Babur also pursued his own territorial ambitions and claims to northern Indian territories.

Action: Ibrahim assembled a large army to resist Babur's advancing forces.

The decisive confrontation took place at Panipat on 21 April 1526.

Babur's army employed mobile cavalry, field artillery and coordinated battlefield tactics, including Tulughma (flanking manoeuvres) and Araba (the use of carts as defensive field barriers).

Ibrahim's forces included war elephants and a substantial army, but numerical superiority was insufficient against Babur's battlefield arrangements.

Result: Ibrahim Lodi was defeated and killed on the battlefield.

The Lodi dynasty and the conventional Delhi Sultanate period ended in 1526 CE.

Important qualification: Babur's artillery was significant, but the victory was not caused by artillery alone. Cavalry tactics, leadership, battlefield organization and divisions among Ibrahim's nobles also contributed.
