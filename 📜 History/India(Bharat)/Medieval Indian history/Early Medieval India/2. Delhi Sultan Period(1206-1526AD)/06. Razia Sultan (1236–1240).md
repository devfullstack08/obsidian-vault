The exam name is **Razia**, also written Raziyya. Her title on the coins is **Sultan**. She is Iltutmish’s daughter, not his wife, and not Shah Turkan.

Previous: [[05. Iltutmish (1211–1236)]].

![[razia-dates.png]]

## Say this

> **Razia, 1236–1240, is the only woman who ruled the Delhi Sultanate in her own name. Iltutmish chose her because his son was not strong enough. The Turkish nobles accepted her for a few years, then removed her. The pivot is their refusal to obey a woman who broke their hold on office.**

She is not the only woman who ever ruled in India. **Rudrama Devi** of the Kakatiyas is the other name to keep beside her. Do not merge the two.

## Why she got the throne, and why it took two steps

Iltutmish’s eldest son, Nasiruddin Mahmud, had already died in Bengal in **1229**. The son who remained, **Ruknuddin Firuz**, was given to pleasure. Iltutmish had let Razia manage Delhi when he was away on campaign. He judged her the safer heir.

The Turkish nobles did not. When he died they raised Ruknuddin, and **Shah Turkan**, Ruknuddin’s mother, held the real power in those months. She moved against Razia.

The chronicle account is that Razia went to the congregational mosque in red clothes and appealed to the people of Delhi. The crowd turned on Shah Turkan. Ruknuddin was deposed and killed. Razia took the throne in **1236**.

So the first pivot is the people of the capital against a weak brother and his mother. The second pivot, four years later, is the nobles against Razia herself.

## Why 1240 happened

![[razia-places.png]]

She ruled in person. She sat in open court. She gave high office outside the circle of Turkish slave-nobles, the **Group of Forty**.

The appointment to remember is **Jamaluddin Yaqut**, an Abyssinian, made **amir-i akhur**, master of the horse. Aibak had once held that same office under Ghori. In Razia’s reign it became the insult. A non-Turk stood where the Turkish maliks thought only one of themselves should stand. A later gossip makes Yaqut her lover. Do not write the gossip as the cause. The cause is the office.

**Malik Ikhtiyaruddin Altunia**, governor of **Tabarhindah** (Bhatinda), rebelled. Razia marched(કૂચ કરી) out. Yaqut was killed. She was held at Bhatinda. She then married Altunia. That marriage was a political alliance, a way back to an army, not a romance to memorise.

They marched on Delhi together. By then the nobles had raised **Bahram**. Razia and Altunia were defeated, and both were killed near **Kaithal** in **1240**. Accounts differ on the last hour. The exam fact is the place and the year, and that she did not die on the throne in Delhi.

## What the reign was for

Use these together.

1. **Iltutmish had already built the state.** Delhi, the iqta, and the tanka were there. She inherited a sultanate. She did not found one.
2. **She tried to rule, not to reign in a veil.** Open court was the point. A secluded queen would not have frightened the Forty.
3. **She broke the Turkish monopoly of office.** Yaqut is the example. The Forty could endure a slave-sultan of their own kind. They would not endure a woman who promoted an outsider.
4. **She could win Delhi once.** The mosque and the crowd removed Ruknuddin. They could not keep the provincial maliks loyal.
5. **The return from Bhatinda failed.** Marriage to Altunia made her a rebel against the new sultan, not a queen restored by consent.

Minhaj’s judgement is the line to remember in spirit: she had the qualities of a ruler, and she was not obeyed because she was a woman. Do not add that Indians rejected women in every century. The Kakatiya queen Rudrama Devi ruled in the Deccan in this same age.

She struck **silver tankas** in the style Iltutmish had set, and the title on them is **Sultan**. Some pieces also carry her father’s name, which borrows his legitimacy. There is no gold mohur, and there is no need to treat “Sultana” as a different, lesser office. In English the word only means a woman sultan. On her coins she is Sultan.

## What you can still see

![[razia-tomb.jpg]]

Two graves in a small walled court in Old Delhi, near **Turkman Gate**, in the lane called Bulbuli Khana. Tradition calls one of them Razia’s, and the other her sister’s. The identification is traditional. It is not a contemporary inscription you can read, and the shed and the houses are modern. She was killed near Kaithal, so this Delhi grave, if it is hers, is a later burial or a later claim. Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0, File:Tomb of Razia Sultana 001.jpg.

## What comes next

Bahram rules only to **1242(2 year)**. Then **Alauddin Masud** to **1246**. Then the younger **Nasiruddin Mahmud**, 1246–1266, with **Balban** as the power behind the throne. Balban becomes sultan himself in **1266**. He is the man who later breaks what remains of the Group of Forty. That is a later chapter. Do not give his reign to Razia.

The Mongols sack **Lahore in 1241**, the year after her death, under these weak successors. That disaster is not hers.

## One answer

Razia was the daughter of Iltutmish, and the heir he named because Ruknuddin was not fit to rule. In **1236** the nobles and Shah Turkan put Ruknuddin up first. Razia appealed to the people of Delhi, took the throne, and ruled as **Sultan** until **1240**. She governed in person and made the Abyssinian Yaqut master of the horse, which the Turkish Group of Forty would not accept. Altunia held her at Bhatinda. She married him and marched back. They were defeated and killed near **Kaithal**. Bahram took Delhi. Her fall did not end the Slave dynasty. It showed that the nobles, not the crowd of the capital, still decided who sat on the throne.
