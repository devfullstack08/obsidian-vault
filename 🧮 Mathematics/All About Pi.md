# All About Pi (π)

This note explains everything about the mathematical constant **Pi ($\pi$)**: what it is, why we use the fraction $22/7$, who discovered it, and why it is indispensable(અત્યંત આવશ્યક) to our world.

---

## 1. What is Pi ($\pi$)?

**Pi ($\pi$)** is simply a **ratio**. It is the ratio of a circle's **Circumference** (the distance all the way around the circle) to its **Diameter** (the distance across the circle through the center).

$$\pi = \frac{\text{Circumference}}{\text{Diameter}} = \frac{C}{d}$$

### The Universal Rule:
No matter how small a coin is, or how large a planet is, if you draw a perfect circle, the distance around it is always roughly **3.14 times** the distance across it. This ratio is constant for every circle in the universe.

---

## 2. Why is Pi equal to $22/7$?

The number $\pi$ is **irrational**. This means:
1. Its decimal expansion goes on forever ($3.1415926535...$).
2. It has no repeating pattern.
3. It cannot be written exactly as a simple fraction.

Since we cannot write down an infinite number of digits in daily life or engineering, we use fractions that are **extremely close** to $\pi$:

* **The $22/7$ approximation:**
  $$\frac{22}{7} = 3.142857...$$
  $$\pi = 3.141592...$$
  * Difference: $\mathbf{0.00126}$ (accurate to **99.96%**).
  * Because it is so close and easy to calculate with, we use $22/7$ in school.

* **The $355/113$ approximation (Chinese / Madhava approximation):**
  $$\frac{355}{113} = 3.14159292...$$
  * Correct to **6 decimal places**!

---

## 3. Who Discovered/Calculated Pi?

Throughout history, different civilizations calculated closer approximations of $\pi$:

* **Ancient Babylonians & Egyptians (c. 1900–1600 BCE):** Used rough approximations like $3$ or $3.125$ or $(16/9)^2 \approx 3.16$.
* **Archimedes of Syracuse (Ancient Greece, 287–212 BCE):** First to calculate $\pi$ using a rigorous mathematical algorithm. By drawing 96-sided polygons inside and outside a circle, he proved that $\pi$ lies between $223/71$ and $22/7$.
* **Aryabhata (India, 476–550 CE):** In his treatise *Aryabhatiya*, he wrote that the ratio of circumference to diameter is $62,832 / 20,000 = 3.1416$ (correct to 4 decimal places). He also noted this was an *"Asanna"* (approximate) value, showing he knew $\pi$ was irrational.
* **Madhava of Sangamagrama (India, 1340–1425 CE):** Discovered the infinite power series for $\pi$ (known as the Madhava-Leibniz series) and calculated $\pi$ to **11 decimal places**.

---

## 4. Why do we need Pi? Can we live without it?

Without $\pi$, **modern science, engineering, and technology would collapse**. It is impossible to calculate anything involving curves, spheres, rotations, waves, or cycles without $\pi$.

### What we cannot calculate without Pi:
1. **Areas and Volumes:**
   * Area of a circle: $A = \pi r^2$ (pizzas, fields, gears).
   * Volume of a cylinder: $V = \pi r^2 h$ (water tanks, engines).
   * Volume of a sphere: $V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ (balls, planets).
2. **Space Navigation:** NASA uses $\pi$ to calculate trajectories of spacecraft, orbits of satellites, and sizes of planets.
3. **Electricity & Signal Processing:** Alternating Current (AC) electricity, sound waves, light waves, and WiFi signals propagate in sine waves. The math representing these waves uses $\pi$ to measure frequency and cycles.
4. **Statistics:** The Bell Curve (Normal Distribution) used in census data, IQ tests, and financial modeling contains $\pi$ in its formula.

## 5. Practical Examples to make it 100% Clear

### Example A: Designing a Circular Running Track
Suppose you want to design a circular running track with a diameter ($d$) of **100 meters**. You need to buy fencing to put around the boundary of the track. How much fencing do you need?
* You need the **Circumference ($C$)**.
* Formula: $C = \pi \times d$
* Using $22/7$:
  $$C = \frac{22}{7} \times 100 = 314.28\text{ meters}$$
* Using actual $\pi$:
  $$C = 3.14159 \times 100 = 314.16\text{ meters}$$
* **Result:** You buy approximately 314 meters of fencing. Without $\pi$, you would have no idea how long the circular path is!

### Example B: Calculating Pizza Area
Which pizza gives you more food: **one 12-inch pizza** or **two 8-inch pizzas**?
* Recall: The size of a pizza is its diameter.
  * For a 12-inch pizza: Radius $r = 6\text{ inches}$.
  * For an 8-inch pizza: Radius $r = 4\text{ inches}$.
* Area of a circle: $A = \pi r^2$
  * **Area of one 12-inch pizza:**
    $$A = \pi \times 6^2 = 36\pi \approx 36 \times 3.1416 = \mathbf{113.1\text{ sq. inches}}$$
  * **Area of two 8-inch pizzas:**
    $$A = 2 \times (\pi \times 4^2) = 32\pi \approx 32 \times 3.1416 = \mathbf{100.5\text{ sq. inches}}$$
* **Result:** One 12-inch pizza is actually larger than two 8-inch pizzas! $\pi$ lets us calculate this counter-intuitive result easily.
