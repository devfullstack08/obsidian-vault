**If GIFT City is a newly planned smart city, why does it need disaster-resilience recognition when older Indian cities may face greater disaster risks?**

The answer is that **disaster management is not only about correcting existing problems. It is also about preventing future disasters.**

### 1. First, understand what UNDRR actually did

On **17 November 2026**, GIFT City was recognised as India's first **MCR2030 Resilience Hub**.

This does not mean that UNDRR discovered GIFT City was unsafe and ordered it to become disaster-resilient by 2030.

It means GIFT City was recognised for its disaster-risk reduction efforts and its potential to help other cities improve their resilience.

Also, **2030 is the target year of the international initiative**, not a specific deadline imposed on GIFT City to become safe.

### 2. Why does a newly developed city need disaster management?

**Example 1 — Earthquakes**
Even a newly constructed building faces earthquake hazards.
Modern construction standards can reduce vulnerability, but they cannot prevent earthquakes from occurring.

**Example 2 — Urban flooding**
A planned city may have modern drainage, but unusually intense rainfall, blocked drains or infrastructure failures can still cause flooding.

**Example 3 — Infrastructure failure**
GIFT City contains important financial and technological infrastructure.
A power failure, fire or severe disruption could affect businesses and essential services, even if buildings remain structurally safe.

**The key concept is that a planned city can reduce disaster vulnerability, but it cannot eliminate every hazard.**

### 3. Your argument about older cities is also important

You argued that older cities often have more problems and therefore may need greater disaster-management support.

That is a reasonable observation.

For example, many older urban areas may have ageing infrastructure, congested roads, inadequate drainage or densely populated vulnerable settlements.

However, disaster risk depends on more than the age of a city.

Disaster = Hazard * Exposure * Vulnerability / Capacity 
(Conceptual relationship only, not an exact mathematical formula).

A new city may have stronger infrastructure but also a high concentration of valuable assets and essential services.

An older city may have more vulnerable buildings but also established emergency-response systems.

Therefore, **both types of cities need disaster-risk management, although their priorities may differ.**

### 4. Why was GIFT City selected instead of an older city?

This is the most important distinction.

UNDRR's Resilience Hub programme does not simply select the city facing the greatest disaster risk.

Its published criteria focus on cities with established disaster-risk reduction experience that can share knowledge, support and mentor other cities.

This is why your concern about older cities does not conflict with GIFT City's recognition.

### Final conclusion

Your argument is **right in identifying the special needs of older cities**, but it would be incorrect to conclude that newly developed cities do not require disaster management.

For your examination, remember this distinction:

**Disaster management is not only about responding after a disaster. Disaster-risk reduction begins before the disaster occurs.**

This is closely connected to the **Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030)** and **SDG 11 — Sustainable Cities and Communities**.

**GIFT City represents the importance of integrating resilience into new urban development, while older cities require risk-sensitive retrofitting of existing infrastructure.**

The key takeaway is: **A resilient city is not necessarily the city with the newest infrastructure. It is a city that understands its risks, protects its vulnerable population and can prepare for, withstand and recover from disasters.**
