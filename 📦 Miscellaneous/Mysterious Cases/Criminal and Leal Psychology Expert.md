
The phrase **“Criminal and Legal Psychology Expert” is usually not the name of one fixed Government of India post**. That distinction matters.

That credit describes  **area of expertise**; it does not mean the Government of India created a post with that exact title.

The government career closest to what you're asking about is generally called **Forensic Psychology / Behavioural Forensics**, and posts can have titles such as **Scientist, Scientific Officer, Psychologist, Forensic Psychology Expert, counsellor, researcher, or faculty**, depending on the organisation.

### What does a criminal/forensic psychologist actually do?

They apply psychology to crime and the justice system. For example, they may study offender behaviour, conduct psychological assessments, assist investigators with interviewing strategies, evaluate cognitive or psychological functioning, work with juveniles(કિશોરો) or prisoners, conduct research, and sometimes provide expert opinions to courts.

It is important not to imagine the movie version where a psychologist looks at a crime scene and instantly says, “The killer is a 34-year-old left-handed man.” Real forensic psychology is much more evidence-based and cautious.

One particularly concrete legal role exists under the **Juvenile Justice Act, 2015**. When a child aged 16–18 is accused of a heinous offence, the Juvenile Justice Board conducts a preliminary assessment concerning matters such as the child's mental and physical capacity and ability to understand the consequences. The Act permits the Board to take assistance from experienced psychologists or psycho-social workers/other experts.

### Which department do they work under?

There isn't only one.

At the **Central Government forensic-laboratory level**, the important organisation is:

Ministry of Home Affairs → Directorate of Forensic Science Services (DFSS) → Central Forensic Science Laboratories (CFSLs).

DFSS's stated purpose is to provide credible forensic services to the justice-delivery system, and DFSS records specifically show activity in **Forensic Psychology** as a forensic discipline.

Similar professionals can also work with state FSLs, police/investigation organisations, prisons and correctional institutions, Juvenile Justice Boards, universities/research institutes, courts as independent experts, hospitals/mental-health institutions, NGOs, and private forensic/consulting organisations.

**No. There is no single “Criminal Psychology Officer Examination.”**

Government forensic posts are advertised separately according to vacancies. Some Central Government scientific posts are recruited through **UPSC direct recruitment**, rather than through the Civil Services Examination. UPSC itself distinguishes its regular competitive examinations from its recruitment advertisements/recruitment tests.

So you generally **do not need to become IAS/IPS first**.

A vacancy might say, for example:

> Scientist-B — Forensic Psychology  
> Central Forensic Science Laboratory  
> Directorate of Forensic Science Services  
> Ministry of Home Affairs

and then prescribe its own education, experience, age limit and selection process.
You must read the notification for that particular recruitment because these requirements can change.

### What should you study?

**Bachelor's degree → Psychology → Master's in Forensic Psychology / Psychology with relevant specialization**

or, depending upon the particular job:

**Bachelor's → Criminology/Psychology → Master's in Criminology with Forensic Psychology specialization.**

India actually has a university specifically focused on this ecosystem: **National Forensic Sciences University, or NFSU**, headquartered in Gandhinagar.

NFSU currently offers a **2-year M.Sc. in Forensic Psychology**, as well as an **M.A. Criminology with specialization in Forensic Psychology**, postgraduate investigative-psychology training, and doctoral opportunities.

Its School of Behavioural Forensics covers fields including forensic psychology, criminology and related behavioural sciences.

### And this connects to Gujarat in an interesting way

The institutional history is especially relevant if you're from Gujarat.

The predecessor of NFSU was **Gujarat Forensic Sciences University (GFSU)**. It was conceived in **2008** to address the shortage of trained forensic experts and came into existence in Gandhinagar on **11 February 2009**.

Then, on **1 October 2020**, the Government of India established it as the **National Forensic Sciences University under the NFSU Act, 2020**, with Institution of National Importance status.

So there wasn't an event like:
> “A famous murder happened → Government invented criminal psychology.”

That would be an incorrect history.

The development was broader: India needed more scientifically trained personnel for **crime investigation, crime prevention, courts, policing, behavioural science and forensic laboratories**. NFSU explicitly describes addressing the acute shortage of forensic experts as one of its missions.


### Why is psychology needed in criminal justice?

Because physical evidence answers only some questions.

DNA may tell investigators **who was present**.

Toxicology may tell them **what substance was consumed**.

Ballistics may tell them **which firearm fired a bullet**.

But criminal justice also encounters questions such as:

**Can this juvenile understand the consequences of the alleged offence?**

**What psychological factors affect an interview or interrogation?**

**Is somebody psychologically competent for a particular legal proceeding?**

**What behavioural patterns are relevant to an investigation?**

**What treatment or rehabilitation risks exist for an offender?**

Those are behavioural rather than purely chemical or biological questions.

That's where forensic psychology becomes useful.

### Criminal psychology vs forensic psychology vs legal psychology

They overlap, but they're not identical.

**Criminal psychology** focuses heavily on offenders and criminal behaviour — _why a person commits crime, behavioural patterns, personality, motivation, offending pathways, etc._

**Legal psychology** is broader psychology applied to the legal system — eyewitness memory, testimony, decision-making, interviewing, juries/courts, competency and similar subjects.

**Forensic psychology** sits at the intersection of psychology and law/criminal justice and is usually the most useful career label to search for in India.

So:

> **Criminal psychology = understanding criminal behaviour**  
> **Legal psychology = psychology inside the legal system**  
> **Forensic psychology = applying psychological expertise to investigation + courts + criminal justice**

### Can an expert directly decide who is guilty?
No.

A psychologist can **assess and give an expert opinion**. Police investigate, prosecutors present cases, and courts determine legal guilt.
That's a very important professional boundary.

### If your goal is actually to become one

Your practical roadmap would look approximately like this:

**12th → Bachelor's in Psychology → Master's in Forensic Psychology → internships/research/practical exposure → apply to FSL/CFSL/DFSS/state forensic labs/research/university/prison/JJ-related vacancies.**

NFSU is particularly worth looking at because it offers dedicated forensic-psychology education rather than requiring you to assemble the specialization from unrelated courses.

And then keep checking **UPSC recruitment advertisements**, DFSS vacancies, state FSL recruitment and university/research vacancies rather than searching only for “criminal psychologist government exam.”

“Criminal and Legal Psychology Expert” is an expertise/professional description, not one nationally standardized government designation. In government forensic work, the closest formal field is Forensic Psychology/Behavioural Forensics, with recruitment varying by department and post.
